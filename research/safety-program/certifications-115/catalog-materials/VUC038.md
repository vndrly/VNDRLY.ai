# VNDRLY/U Asset Integrity — Damage Mechanisms

VUC038 · Original teaching material · Available-source learning package, 2026-11-18

## What this program teaches

A damage-mechanism review explains how an asset can deteriorate, where that deterioration is likely, what observations would support or contradict the explanation, and what inspection or control is needed. Naming a mechanism is only the start. The learner must connect material, environment, stress and time to a defensible action. This course is for inspectors, corrosion technicians, maintenance engineers and operations personnel who can read equipment drawings, distinguish pressure from temperature, and perform elementary arithmetic. It does not confer API certification, NDE qualification, engineering approval authority or permission to operate damaged equipment.

The available-source basis requested by the user applies. API's public certification page identifies the whole third edition of RP571 as its examination basis. This original VNDRLY program is not a verified reproduction or equivalent of that examination. It uses accessible primary guidance, first-principles instruction and explicitly synthetic records. No unavailable numerical susceptibility boundary is invented. For a live asset, the employer's adopted engineering criteria and qualified specialists control acceptance.

Each chapter contains instruction, a worked decision and a transfer question. Read the explanation before attempting the learning checks. Preserve uncertainty: “credible,” “supported,” “confirmed by the stated evidence,” and “not assessed” mean different things. A mechanism can be credible even when no damage has yet been found. Conversely, finding a pit does not establish its cause.

## Q01 — Build an evidence-based mechanism review

Start with the component's identity and history: alloy and heat treatment, fabrication and repairs, fluid phases and contaminants, operating and transient temperatures, pressures, loads, time in service and prior inspections. Use actual conditions rather than a nominal process-flow diagram. A dry stream at the unit inlet can become wet in a cooler; a continuously hot line can spend shutdowns wet and exposed to air. A replacement spool can differ from the original metallurgy.

For each candidate mechanism write four things: the required conditions, the susceptible locations, the evidence presently available and the missing evidence that could change the decision. Then select a detection method and mitigation appropriate to that candidate. Do not eliminate a mechanism just because the last inspection did not look for it. “Not detected” is meaningful only with a method, coverage, sensitivity and date. Process changes after that date can invalidate the inference.

Separate prevention, detection and consequence control. A coating can reduce contact with an environment; a thickness survey can detect some wall loss; isolation and emergency response can reduce exposure after a leak. One does not replace the others. Assign an owner and due date to each unresolved condition. A risk ranking helps prioritize work, but cannot convert unknown wall condition into acceptable wall condition.

**Worked decision.** A line register says dry gas; startup records show water flushing and a low point without drainage. Internal aqueous corrosion remains credible during wet periods. Request the flushing chemistry, drainage record and low-point inspection, rather than marking all internal corrosion “not applicable.”

**Transfer.** The drain is now installed. What closes the old exposure? Installation prevents some future retention; it does not remove existing damage. A baseline examination and approved disposition are still needed.

## Q02 — Read materials and microstructure correctly

Carbon steel, low-alloy steel, austenitic stainless steel, duplex stainless steel, nickel alloys, copper alloys and cast irons do not respond identically to the same service. Composition controls only part of the response. Heat treatment, grain structure, cold work, weld zones, inclusions and hardness can change susceptibility. A material test report must be traceable to the actual component; a nearby component's certificate is not evidence for it.

Positive material identification can help establish alloy chemistry within the capabilities of the instrument. It does not automatically establish carbon content, heat treatment, toughness, strength, sensitization or freedom from cracks. A hardness test answers a different question from chemical analysis. Metallography examines structure; mechanical tests examine selected properties. Specify the property that must be established before selecting a test.

Austenitic stainless steel resists many environments that attack carbon steel, but it is not universally corrosion-proof. Duplex performance depends on an appropriate phase balance and fabrication history. Cast iron may retain its shape after selective attack has weakened the metallic matrix. A high-alloy repair can also create a dissimilar-metal interface requiring separate review.

**Worked decision.** Handheld chemistry identifies a stainless family on a replacement nozzle, but its weld history is missing. The result supports composition identification only. It does not close a concern about heat-affected-zone structure or cracking. Obtain the fabrication record and select the necessary property or flaw examination.

**Transfer.** Why is “upgrade everything to stainless” not a mechanism review? It can exchange one vulnerability for another, leave fabrication defects unaddressed and ignore the actual environment.

## Q03 — General corrosion, localized attack and measurement

In aqueous corrosion, anodic metal dissolution and cathodic reactions are coupled through the metal and electrolyte. Changes in chemistry, oxygen, temperature, deposits and flow change local conditions. General loss spreads across an area; localized attack concentrates loss. A component can have both. An average thickness can conceal a deep pit or a narrow groove.

For a like-for-like measurement, average loss rate equals thickness loss divided by elapsed time. Establish that both readings refer to the same location, method and surface condition. A negative apparent rate is not metal regeneration; check measurement uncertainty, location and records. If service changed, the historic average may not represent future behavior. Neither a linear rate nor a remaining-life arithmetic exercise is an automatic inspection interval or fitness-for-service decision.

Pits can create locally aggressive chemistry. Crevices restrict transport and may develop conditions unlike the bulk fluid. Inspect gasket interfaces, lap joints, deposits and stagnant regions. Clean surfaces only under an approved safe procedure and preserve evidence when failure analysis is needed. The deepest remaining ligament and the geometry of the affected area may matter more than the mean value.

**Worked decision.** Matched readings fall from 8.0 to 7.4 mm in two years: average loss is 0.30 mm/year. A mapped pit at 4.1 mm is not represented by that rate. Record the pit separately and obtain the applicable localized-damage assessment.

**Transfer.** A new survey reads 7.6 mm at the original spot. Reconcile instrument, position and uncertainty before using the apparent increase to lengthen any interval.

## Q04 — Atmospheric, soil and under-insulation corrosion

External corrosion depends on exposure as well as alloy. Water, salts, coating defects, soil conditions and wet/dry cycles can produce sharply different behavior along one line. Insulation can conceal water entry and damage. Weather seals, penetrations, supports, low points and termination details deserve particular attention. A sound outer jacket does not prove that the metal beneath it is dry.

Carbon steel under wet insulation can lose wall. Stainless steel under contaminated wet insulation can develop localized attack or chloride stress corrosion cracking. The consequence is that a thickness-only inspection strategy may be incomplete. Screening techniques can prioritize areas, but their limitations and required follow-up must be explicit. Removal of insulation is itself work requiring access, contamination and process-temperature controls.

Buried systems require a separate understanding of coating condition, electrical interference, soil exposure and any cathodic protection system. An aboveground reading does not establish the condition of a soil-to-air transition or the full buried run. Cathodic protection readings need a defined method, valid reference electrode and qualified interpretation; one favorable reading is not blanket proof of protection.

**Worked decision.** A stainless line has failed weather seals and saline deposits at a support. A few satisfactory spot thickness readings do not eliminate cracking. Plan an appropriate surface/crack examination with qualified personnel and investigate the water source.

**Transfer.** If the jacket is repaired today, why retain inspection follow-up? Weatherproofing addresses entry of new water, not the existing metal condition or retained contaminants.

## Q05 — Galvanic, crevice and selective corrosion

Galvanic corrosion requires an electrical connection between dissimilar electrochemical surfaces and an electrolyte. The relative areas and local exposure matter: a small anodic area coupled to a large cathodic area can suffer concentrated attack. Merely listing two alloy names is insufficient; determine electrical continuity, electrolyte contact and geometry. Coating choices must consider defects and the resulting exposed-area ratio.

Crevice attack arises from restricted local exchange, not necessarily a dissimilar-metal pair. A nonmetallic gasket can create a crevice. Selective corrosion removes or changes particular constituents of an alloy, leaving a weakened residue. Dezincification can affect susceptible brasses; graphitic corrosion can weaken cast iron while preserving a deceptively recognizable shape. Distinguish graphitic corrosion from high-temperature graphitization of steel, which is a microstructural change.

Detection must test the suspected weakness. Color, dimensions or a superficial tap are not adequate acceptance evidence. A specialist may require sectioning, metallography, chemistry and property evaluation. Repair or replacement must address the environment and coupling, not only restore appearance.

**Worked decision.** A brass component shows a porous copper-colored region and seepage. Treat selective attack as a candidate and preserve a representative sample. A coating over the outside does not restore the lost load-bearing alloy.

**Transfer.** A stainless patch is proposed on a carbon steel vessel. Review the joint, electrolyte exposure and area relationship as well as welding and design requirements; “more noble” does not mean every adjacent surface benefits.

## Q06 — Cooling water, deposits and microbiological influence

Cooling-water damage can include general corrosion, pitting, under-deposit attack, erosion-assisted loss, fouling and microbiologically influenced corrosion. Water chemistry at a clean sample point may differ from chemistry beneath a deposit or in a stagnant branch. Flow distribution, temperature, treatment history, leakage and intermittent operation influence local conditions.

Microorganisms can influence electrochemical conditions, deposits and local chemistry. A positive microbial test alone does not establish that MIC caused the damage; microorganisms may be present without being the controlling cause. Conversely, a negative bulk-water sample does not eliminate activity beneath an established deposit. Combine morphology, deposits, local sampling, process history and expert interpretation. Preserve samples promptly using the laboratory's specified collection and transport procedure.

Treatment is a controlled operating activity. Do not improvise biocide additions, mix chemicals or mechanically disturb suspect deposits on live equipment. Corrective measures can include approved cleaning, treatment control, stagnation reduction and material changes, each checked for unintended consequences. Monitor both the treatment and the asset response.

**Worked decision.** A heat exchanger has rising pressure drop, patchy deposits and clustered pits. The defensible conclusion is a set of candidates requiring evidence, not “MIC confirmed.” Map the damage, review treatment interruptions and arrange representative deposit and metal examination.

**Transfer.** A clean water sample after a chemical treatment does not prove the old deposit-covered surface is sound. Establish its condition separately.

## Q07 — Carbon dioxide, oxygen and sour-water environments

Carbon dioxide dissolved in water can create corrosive conditions for susceptible steels. Water availability, temperature, pressure, chemistry, flow and protective-film behavior influence the result. Dry CO2 and wet CO2 service are not interchangeable. Oxygen ingress can introduce or accelerate other reactions. Do not assume a small oxygen measurement at one location describes an entire intermittent system.

Sour water introduces sulfur species and may introduce hydrogen-related cracking concerns as well as metal loss. pH, contaminants and local concentration matter. A fluid described simply as “sour” does not identify the controlling mechanism. Distinguish general or localized aqueous corrosion from blistering, hydrogen-induced cracking, stress-oriented hydrogen-induced cracking and sulfide stress cracking. The inspection plan must consider each credible form separately.

Corrosion coupons and probes provide useful local evidence, but may not represent deposits, phase separation or cracking. Record where they are installed and what they measure. Inhibitor performance must be evaluated against the actual distribution and operating envelope, including startup, shutdown and interruptions.

**Worked decision.** A gas line normally runs dry but collects water at a low point during winter. An upstream coupon with low loss cannot clear that low point. Confirm liquid composition and retention, inspect the susceptible location and review drainage controls.

**Transfer.** Increasing an inhibitor dose is not proof that a crack-like indication has been arrested or made acceptable; obtain the required integrity assessment.

## Q08 — Acid, caustic and chemical-service corrosion

Chemical names alone do not determine compatibility. Concentration, temperature, contaminants, aeration, velocity and phase determine whether a material is suitable. Dilution can change corrosivity in unexpected ways, so neither “weaker acid” nor “lower concentration” automatically means safer service. Mixing and injection zones may experience a chemistry absent from the bulk stream.

Hydrochloric, sulfuric, hydrofluoric, phosphoric and organic-acid services need service-specific review. HF service also introduces serious personnel hazards and possible hydrogen-related damage concerns in susceptible materials. This course does not authorize sampling, opening or repairing those systems. Caustic service can cause thinning and stress corrosion cracking; local concentration beneath deposits or during evaporation can be important even when the bulk concentration appears modest.

Inspect injection hardware, mixing zones, drains, deadlegs and phase-change locations. Establish the correct corrosion-control and materials specialist before selecting an alloy or changing chemistry. A compatibility chart without its concentration and temperature basis is not a design approval.

**Worked decision.** An injection quill is shortened during repair and downstream localized thinning appears. Investigate mixing and impingement together with chemistry; replacing only the thinned spool leaves the changed exposure in place.

**Transfer.** A proposed water flush must be reviewed for reaction heat, dilution effects, waste handling and trapped chemical. A classroom example is not a flushing instruction.

## Q09 — Ammonium salts, sour-water concentration and amines

Ammonium chloride and ammonium bisulfide problems require attention to deposition, water availability, concentration and flow. A deposit can hide an aggressive local environment. The location where a salt forms or becomes wet can differ from the location of a bulk stream sample. Changes in feed contaminants, wash-water distribution or exchanger temperature can move the susceptible zone.

Separate salt deposition and fouling from corrosion underneath deposits and from high-velocity corrosive attack. A pressure-drop increase can signal fouling, but is not a metal-thickness measurement. Wash-water control requires an engineered basis; simply adding water can introduce other problems if mixing and distribution are inadequate.

Amine systems can experience corrosion and cracking influenced by solvent condition, contaminants, temperature, loading and fabrication stress. A general corrosion reading does not clear weld-related cracking. Record solution analyses, operating excursions, reclaiming/treatment history and weld examination coverage. Mitigation must address the system conditions and the existing damage.

**Worked decision.** After a feed change, an exchanger outlet has deposits and localized loss while its upstream coupon remains stable. Revisit the deposition and wetting model and inspect the affected exchanger and downstream region. Do not average away the local result.

**Transfer.** Cleaning restores flow but does not establish remaining integrity. Obtain post-cleaning examination and a controlled disposition.

## Q10 — Sulfidation and naphthenic-acid corrosion

Hot sulfur-containing process streams can attack susceptible alloys. Material composition, temperature and stream chemistry matter. Components within one circuit may behave differently, especially when material history is mixed. The Chevron Richmond investigation illustrates why measurements on selected fittings cannot automatically represent every pipe component. [S03]

Naphthenic-acid corrosion is another high-temperature process concern. Acid characteristics, temperature, velocity, phase and metallurgy interact; a single total-acid-number result is not a complete prediction. Sulfidation and acid attack can coexist or interact, so the review should not force every observation into one category. Examine locations of turbulence and phase change along with apparently uniform runs.

A thickness plan should follow actual component boundaries and credible attack locations. Material verification helps define groups but does not measure damage. Changes in crude blend or operating conditions require review of the inspection and control basis. An alloy upgrade must be justified for all credible mechanisms, including welds and transition pieces.

**Worked decision.** Four fittings remain near original thickness, but the intervening pipe has unknown chemistry. The fittings are not adequate proof of the pipe's condition. Establish the pipe components and examine them using the required coverage rather than extrapolating from a favorable sample.

**Transfer.** A new feed has the same sulfur content but different acid and boiling characteristics. Explain why the old mechanism review must be revisited rather than copied unchanged.

## Q11 — Oxidation, hot corrosion and carburizing environments

At elevated temperature, metal can react with gases to form surface scales. Whether a scale protects depends on composition, adherence and service conditions. Thermal cycling can disrupt a protective layer. High-temperature corrosion may therefore accelerate after a process or temperature change even without an aqueous electrolyte.

Oxidation, fuel-ash corrosion and hot-gas attack require attention to deposits, gas composition and metal temperature. Carburization introduces carbon into susceptible material and can change properties. Decarburization removes carbon from affected regions. Metal dusting is a distinct form of severe degradation in suitable carbonaceous environments; it is not simply loose external dirt. Nitriding can alter surface and near-surface properties in nitrogen-bearing service. These mechanisms need material-specific review rather than a single generic “heat damage” diagnosis.

Use temperature history, gas/deposit analysis, thickness or dimensional data and appropriate metallurgical examination. An infrared image is useful only with its emissivity, line-of-sight and operating context; it is not a direct measurement of internal microstructure. Repairing insulation or refractory may address overheating but does not reverse existing material degradation.

**Worked decision.** A furnace tube has changed diameter and an unusual surface layer after a firing imbalance. Establish actual tube-metal exposure and request metallurgical evaluation. Removing scale and measuring one wall point cannot establish retained mechanical properties.

**Transfer.** Normal process outlet temperature does not rule out a local hot spot. Identify what measurements describe the metal itself.

## Q12 — Dew-point corrosion and shutdown exposure

Cooling a gas stream can create a liquid phase with a chemistry far more aggressive than the bulk gas. Dew-point corrosion depends on the condensate composition and surface temperature, not merely the average gas temperature. Cold spots, air leakage, insulation defects and startup/shutdown transients can move the condensation region.

Acidic condensate in combustion or process systems can cause localized attack. A dry-service designation during normal operation does not clear shutdown conditions. Shutdown can also admit oxygen and moisture to deposits formed during operation, enabling different mechanisms such as polythionic-acid stress corrosion cracking in susceptible sensitized stainless materials.

Review the entire operating cycle: startup, steady service, upset, wash, steam-out, cooling, opening, storage and restart. Controls must be engineered for the particular service and applied before the damaging exposure occurs. An improvised neutralization or purge instruction can create serious hazards; use approved procedures and trained personnel.

**Worked decision.** A duct wall corrodes at a cold support while the gas temperature remains above its normal target. The wall temperature and local condensation conditions matter. Inspect the cold region, investigate gas chemistry and heat loss, and review the control basis.

**Transfer.** If a unit was sound before shutdown but exposed to wet air afterward, the old inspection date does not automatically cover the new exposure.

## Q13 — Stress corrosion cracking: the three-part interaction

Stress corrosion cracking requires a susceptible material condition, an environment capable of causing the mechanism and tensile stress. Residual fabrication stress can matter even when operating pressure is low. Cracks can be transgranular, intergranular or mixed depending on the system; no single crack path identifies every SCC mechanism.

Chlorides can threaten susceptible stainless steels; caustic, carbonate, amine, ammonia and other environments require their own material-specific reviews. Do not treat all SCC as chloride cracking or assume all stainless grades behave alike. Local concentration and weld condition often matter more than a bulk sample or nominal alloy description.

Cracking can be serious with little general wall loss. Select techniques capable of finding the expected crack orientation and depth, with qualified procedures and coverage. Surface methods do not necessarily size through-wall extent. Volumetric methods have geometry and material limitations. The responsible specialist must connect detection capability to the required integrity decision.

**Worked decision.** A welded ammonia-service vessel has satisfactory thickness but new linear indications near welds. Thickness is not a clearance for SCC. Escalate the indications, examine relevant weld regions and review service chemistry and residual-stress history. OSHA's pressure-vessel guidance identifies ammonia-service cracking as a distinct concern. [S05]

**Transfer.** Why might a low operating stress not eliminate the concern? Fabrication and repair can leave tensile residual stresses that the operating-pressure calculation does not represent.

## Q14 — Wet hydrogen damage: blistering, HIC, SOHIC and SSC

Hydrogen generated at a metal surface can enter susceptible steel. In wet sour environments, several different damage forms may be credible. Blistering involves internal accumulation and separation. Hydrogen-induced cracking can develop along internal features and link. Stress-oriented hydrogen-induced cracking describes arrays associated with stress. Sulfide stress cracking involves susceptible material and tensile stress in the sour environment. These are related concerns, not interchangeable labels.

Material cleanliness, hardness, weld condition, stress and environment influence susceptibility. A material certificate identifying grade does not prove that every weld or repair satisfies the applicable service requirements. Hardness screening answers only part of the question. A thickness reading may miss internal separations or cracking.

Select examination to match the candidate morphology and location. An ultrasonic approach intended for wall thickness is not automatically a qualified HIC or crack assessment. Record coverage around welds, heat-affected zones and plate regions as applicable. Avoid drilling, grinding or heating an unexplained blister on operating equipment; escalation and a controlled inspection plan come first.

**Worked decision.** A sour-water vessel has a bulge and internally aligned ultrasonic indications. Do not describe it merely as corrosion allowance consumption. Establish whether hydrogen-related separation and cracking are present and obtain the required engineering disposition.

**Transfer.** A new lower-hardness weld procedure can reduce a future susceptibility; it does not remove existing internal cracking from adjacent plate.

## Q15 — High-temperature hydrogen attack

High-temperature hydrogen attack is different from wet sour cracking and ordinary corrosion thinning. Hydrogen can react within susceptible steel, degrading its microstructure and developing internal damage. Temperature, hydrogen partial pressure, exposure time, material and stress history matter. Use actual hydrogen partial pressure rather than total system pressure alone.

The Tesoro Anacortes investigation demonstrated the consequences of missed HTHA in a carbon-steel exchanger. It also shows why a historic screening assumption or ordinary thickness readings should not be treated as definitive clearance. [S04] A chart boundary is not a substitute for verifying the material, actual exposure and limitations of the assessment method.

Specialized inspection needs an appropriate procedure, qualified personnel, suitable calibration/validation and a documented understanding of detection limits. No single classroom description makes an operator qualified to diagnose HTHA. Metallurgical confirmation may be needed. Once degradation has occurred, reducing temperature does not restore the original microstructure.

**Worked decision.** A process stream is 60% hydrogen at 100 bar absolute total pressure. The simple gas-mixture estimate is 60 bar absolute hydrogen partial pressure, subject to the engineering model's assumptions. Using 100 bar gauge or a stale composition would misstate the basis. This calculation does not establish a safe operating limit.

**Transfer.** A normal thickness result after an excursion does not establish absence of internal HTHA. Specify the missing assessment rather than calling the equipment safe.

## Q16 — Fatigue, vibration and corrosion fatigue

Fatigue develops under repeated stress cycles. Local stress concentration, weld details, supports, vibration, starts/stops and operating changes can matter more than the nominal average load. Crack initiation and growth can occur without appreciable general corrosion. A static strength calculation alone does not address fatigue life.

Mechanical vibration can drive many small cycles, particularly at small-bore connections, unsupported attachments and rotating-equipment interfaces. Thermal cycling can also produce fatigue when parts expand differently or a temperature gradient repeatedly changes. Corrosion fatigue combines cyclic stress with a damaging environment; an air-based fatigue assumption may not represent that service.

Identify the load source and cycle history, inspect susceptible details with suitable crack methods and correct the initiating condition. A weld repair without correcting resonance, support or thermal mixing may fail again. Temporary bracing or grinding requires engineering control; it is not an improvised field cure.

**Worked decision.** A small-bore drain repeatedly cracks after a pump operating change. Rewelding it a third time is not a complete response. Evaluate vibration and support, characterize the crack, inspect similar connections and obtain an engineered correction.

**Transfer.** Why can a short upset matter even when daily averages are normal? Peak stress and cycle count are concealed by an average, and fatigue depends on the repeated local stress history.

## Q17 — Creep, stress rupture and thermal distortion

Creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained load at elevated temperature. Material condition, metal temperature, stress and time govern its significance. Stress rupture is a possible end result. Bulging, dimensional changes or microstructural evidence may indicate concern, but absence of a visible bulge does not establish remaining life.

Local overheating can sharply change the exposure of a tube or component. Process-fluid temperature is not necessarily the highest metal temperature. Deposits, flame impingement, refractory defects and loss of cooling can create hot regions. Compare operating history with the design and material basis using qualified engineering analysis rather than a generic maximum-temperature rule.

Assessment may require dimensional survey, thickness, metallography, hardness and other specialized evidence. Each method has limits. Surface replication samples a particular area and does not automatically describe the full wall or all components. Record the locations and relation to the hottest or most highly stressed regions.

**Worked decision.** Two heater tubes have identical outlet-fluid temperature, but one has heavy internal deposit and measurable bowing. They do not have equivalent thermal evidence. Investigate local metal temperature and degradation before assigning the same remaining-life conclusion.

**Transfer.** Restoring firing balance prevents some future exposure but cannot undo accumulated creep damage; the condition assessment remains necessary.

## Q18 — Embrittlement, phase changes and brittle fracture

Some damage mechanisms reduce toughness or ductility without large wall loss. Temper embrittlement, sigma-phase formation, 475°C embrittlement, strain aging and other material-specific changes depend on alloy, thermal history and structure. Their names are not universal temperature limits. A mechanism review must establish whether the actual material and exposure can support the proposed change.

Brittle fracture is a failure mode involving inadequate resistance to crack propagation under the applied conditions. Temperature, toughness, flaw size, constraint, thickness and stress matter. A pressure test can introduce a hazardous combination rather than prove all future conditions safe. Follow the approved testing and minimum-temperature basis.

Graphitization in susceptible steels is a high-temperature microstructural concern and differs from selective graphitic corrosion of cast iron. Spheroidization and softening may change strength in affected steels. Identify which property is at risk and choose the corresponding examination or test. Hardness alone is not a universal toughness measurement.

**Worked decision.** A thick older vessel is scheduled for pressurization during unusually cold weather. A recent thickness survey does not establish brittle-fracture resistance. Confirm the material, flaw and temperature assessment and approved operating/test limits before proceeding.

**Transfer.** An alloy identification result does not establish that a long thermal exposure has left its original toughness unchanged.

## Q19 — Erosion, cavitation, impingement and FAC

Mechanical removal by particles or droplets, bubble-collapse damage and chemically assisted wall loss can produce similar-looking thinning but require different controls. Erosion concentrates where flow and geometry direct damaging contact. Cavitation involves vapor formation and collapse under suitable pressure conditions. Flashing, impingement and two-phase flow deserve distinct process review.

Flow-accelerated corrosion involves dissolution and transport of a protective surface film in susceptible water/steam service; it should not be reduced to mechanical abrasion. Chemistry, temperature, alloy and hydrodynamics interact. NRC and OECD guidance distinguish FAC from mechanical erosion. [S06] Surface appearance is useful evidence, not a complete diagnosis.

Examine elbows, reducers, downstream restrictions, impingement regions and representative straight runs as required by the mechanism model. Changing flow velocity or materials without checking process performance and other mechanisms can exchange one problem for another. Restoration of nominal thickness does not correct the flow condition.

**Worked decision.** A carbon-steel wet-steam elbow thins without solids in the stream. Lack of abrasive particles does not eliminate FAC. Review water chemistry, steam condition, material and flow distribution, and map susceptible components.

**Transfer.** A pump casing with noise and pitted impeller regions may need cavitation investigation, not merely an inhibitor increase.

## Q20 — Dissimilar joints, liquid metals and fabrication interactions

Dissimilar-metal joints combine materials with different thermal expansion, properties and environmental responses. Weld metallurgy, transition geometry and thermal cycling can create local concerns. A joint may have passed fabrication examination yet develop service-related damage later. Separate original fabrication imperfections from deterioration occurring in service, while recognizing that an imperfection can initiate later damage.

Liquid-metal embrittlement is a material/environment-specific interaction in which contact with a liquid metal can produce severe loss of ductility under relevant conditions. Contamination by low-melting metals during fabrication or maintenance therefore deserves control. Do not infer compatibility from the fact that a contaminant amount seems small. Specialized evaluation is required when suspected.

Reheat-related cracking and dissimilar-metal weld cracking require attention to alloy, weld heat treatment, residual stress and service history. A generic surface examination cannot answer every metallurgical question. Preserve consumable, procedure, heat-treatment and repair records so the specialist can reconstruct the actual condition.

**Worked decision.** A hot-service dissimilar joint develops indications after repeated starts. Review differential expansion and weld history as well as corrosion candidates. Replacing one side with a stronger alloy without reviewing the joint can leave the initiating condition unchanged.

**Transfer.** A maintenance contaminant is discovered before restart. Hold the affected decision for a material-specific evaluation instead of assuming cleaning the visible surface closes the issue.

## Q21 — Refractory, linings and nonmetallic degradation

Refractory and linings protect equipment by controlling exposure or temperature, but they can themselves crack, detach, erode or chemically degrade. Their failure may expose a shell to conditions outside its intended envelope. A cold outer surface in one location is not proof of continuous lining integrity elsewhere.

Polymers and composites can degrade through chemical attack, swelling, permeation, aging, ultraviolet exposure, thermal cycling or mechanical loading. Material family names alone are insufficient for compatibility. Resin, reinforcement, cure, temperature and service chemistry can all matter. A steel-wall corrosion technique may not be suitable for a composite or lined component.

Inspection should distinguish the protective layer's condition from the substrate's condition. Repairs need the specified preparation, cure and quality checks. Recoating over contamination or an unassessed crack may hide damage. A holiday test detects particular coating discontinuities under a specified procedure; it does not prove chemical compatibility or structural capacity.

**Worked decision.** A vessel has a new exterior hot spot after an internal refractory repair. Treat the change as evidence requiring investigation and operating review. The repair's completion date is not evidence that the substrate stayed within its temperature limit.

**Transfer.** A lining that passes a discontinuity test can still be unsuitable for a new chemical service. Review compatibility separately.

## Q22 — Match inspection to the question

Write the question before ordering the method: Is wall missing? Is there a surface-connected crack? Is there internal separation? Has the microstructure changed? Is the alloy correct? A method's name is not its capability statement. Geometry, surface preparation, temperature, access, material, flaw orientation, equipment, procedure and operator qualification affect performance.

Visual examination establishes accessible surface condition. Thickness methods sample or map wall under their stated limitations. Surface crack methods detect particular surface or near-surface conditions depending on the technique and material. Volumetric methods can examine internal regions, but coverage and sensitivity are not universal. Chemistry, hardness, metallography and mechanical testing answer additional, different questions.

HSE's NDT guidance stresses matching techniques to damage and recognizing their limits. [S07] A useful report states what was examined, with what procedure, what was found, what could not be examined and what follow-up is required. “No defects” without that context is an unsafe overstatement. Independent review is needed when results conflict with a credible mechanism or process evidence.

**Worked decision.** A report says “UT satisfactory,” but contains only six thickness points on a vessel susceptible to weld cracking. It does not demonstrate a crack examination. Request the actual method/coverage and plan the missing examination.

**Transfer.** A more expensive instrument does not remove the need for a suitable procedure and qualified interpretation.

## Q23 — Operating windows, change and mitigation

An integrity operating window links a monitored process condition to a damage concern and a defined response. It needs an engineering basis, measurement location, units, responsible role, response time and escalation path. An alarm with no action owner is not a complete control. A window exceedance is evidence to investigate, not automatic proof of damage or automatic proof that equipment is safe afterward.

Management of change must consider feed, chemical treatment, pressure, temperature, throughput, phase distribution, materials, repairs, insulation, instrumentation and operating modes. Temporary changes can matter as much as permanent ones. Include startup and shutdown. Review whether existing inspection locations still represent the altered service.

Mitigation may remove exposure, improve materials, control stress, manage chemistry, restore protective systems or change operation. Verify effectiveness with appropriate evidence. Inspection detects some damage; it does not itself prevent the mechanism. Deferring an action requires the proper authorized risk decision and controls, not a training exercise's score.

**Worked decision.** A chloride alert clears after one normal sample. The event still requires review of duration, local concentration, susceptible materials and inspection needs. Record the excursion and disposition rather than deleting it from history.

**Transfer.** Replacing a failed sensor restores monitoring but does not reconstruct the unmeasured exposure. State the uncertainty and assessment needed.

## Q24 — Produce a defensible integrity handover

The final review should let another competent person reconstruct the decision. Identify the asset, service period, sources, candidate mechanisms, exclusions with reasons, inspection evidence, uncertainties, required controls, outstanding actions and responsible approvers. Link each significant conclusion to evidence. Separate an observation from an interpretation and an interpretation from an authorization.

Prioritize active leaks, crack-like findings, unexpected rapid loss and conditions outside the established basis through the site's emergency and integrity procedures. Personnel should not approach a leak to collect a better measurement merely to complete a course task. Preserve records and samples safely. Similar-service equipment may require review even when the first finding is isolated to one asset.

A good handover includes disagreement. If process history suggests a mechanism that the examination could not detect, retain the concern and specify the next step. If a mechanism is excluded, state the evidence and the change that would reopen it. A final signature cannot cure missing evidence; it identifies responsibility for an actual decision.

**Worked decision.** The inspection team finds localized loss, operations reports an unrecorded feed change and the maintenance file contains an unverified replacement spool. The handover must combine all three. A wall-loss repair alone does not close material identification or the new service review.

**Transfer.** Write a one-paragraph handover containing a verified fact, a credible mechanism, an uncertainty, an immediate restriction and an owner for follow-up. Avoid declaring an asset fit for service unless the authorized assessment actually establishes that result.

# Specialist mechanism clinics — VUC038

These clinics extend the course's grouped chapters. They teach recognition and the evidence needed for specialist disposition. They do not supply unverified API temperature bands, material-selection tables, inspection intervals or chemical-treatment recipes. Each exercise requires a written response; the worked decision is supplied for formative review.

## C01 — Ethanol service and cracking

Fuel-grade ethanol service can introduce stress corrosion cracking concerns for susceptible steel equipment. It is not adequately screened by saying that the product is less corrosive than an acid. Composition, contaminants, oxygen, stress and operating history require a service-specific assessment. PHMSA-sponsored research documents ethanol-related SCC concerns; NIST research also addresses environmentally assisted fatigue behavior. Those findings are not a universal acceptance rule for every ethanol blend. [S11,S12]

**Exercise.** A carbon-steel tank changes from petroleum product to ethanol service. Its thickness is satisfactory but weld condition and new-service compatibility are unreviewed. **Worked decision:** hold the service-change approval, review material/stress and environment, and specify suitable cracking coverage. A good wall reading does not resolve the new mechanism. **Changed case:** a low-contaminant sample arrives; it establishes only that sample's chemistry, not all future conditions or existing crack condition.

## C02 — Polythionic-acid SCC during shutdown

Sulfide-containing deposits, air and moisture can produce a damaging shutdown environment for susceptible sensitized stainless material. The exposure can occur when the normal process is no longer running. Metal temperature, material history and scale condition influence the review. MTI's bulletin describes the shutdown reaction and cracking concern. [S08] Prevention must be planned before opening; this course does not prescribe a universal wash or neutralization mixture.

**Exercise.** A hot sulfur-service stainless exchanger is opened to humid air after years of service. “No corrosion in service” is offered as clearance. **Worked decision:** evaluate sensitization, deposits and the shutdown exposure; apply the approved prevention and examination plan. **Changed case:** low-carbon material is documented. That is relevant evidence, not automatic proof that every fabrication and exposure issue is resolved.

## C03 — Titanium hydriding and contamination

Titanium's good performance in many environments does not make it immune to hydrogen-related degradation. Hydrogen can affect properties through material-specific mechanisms, including hydride formation. Welding and cleaning history matter. TWI identifies hydrogen-related degradation and the importance of contamination control in titanium work. [S09] An alloy-chemistry reading does not establish retained ductility or hydride absence.

**Exercise.** A titanium component becomes brittle after an undocumented cleaning operation. **Worked decision:** preserve the history and sample, investigate chemical exposure and hydrogen-related damage with a specialist, and do not authorize re-use from thickness alone. **Changed case:** the cleaning contractor says the process was used successfully on steel. That is not evidence of titanium compatibility.

## C04 — Oxygen service and ignition damage

An oxygen system needs compatible materials, cleanliness and controlled ignition hazards. A component acceptable in air can be unsuitable in oxygen-rich service. Seals, lubricants, particles, compression heating and mechanical contact require system-specific review. NASA and HSE guidance treat compatibility as an end-use assessment, not a property inferred from appearance. [S10,S13]

**Exercise.** A regulator receives a visually identical replacement seal with no oxygen-service traceability. **Worked decision:** do not use it until compatibility and the approved replacement basis are established. **Changed case:** the pressure rating matches; this resolves pressure rating only, not ignition compatibility or cleanliness. No classroom oxygen ignition experiment is permitted.

## C05 — Temper embrittlement versus softening

Temper embrittlement concerns a loss of toughness in susceptible alloy/thermal histories, not necessarily a large decrease in hardness. Softening through other microstructural changes can affect strength. A learner must identify the property under question before choosing evidence. A satisfactory tensile strength or hardness value alone is not a complete toughness assessment.

**Exercise.** An aged low-alloy vessel has normal thickness and hardness; cold startup is proposed. **Worked decision:** review thermal history, toughness and the approved brittle-fracture/startup basis. **Changed case:** new hardness data agree with old values. The toughness question remains. Explain the different information supplied by hardness, impact data and structural assessment.

## C06 — Sigma phase and 475°C embrittlement

Different alloy systems can undergo different embrittling transformations. Sigma-phase formation and 475°C embrittlement are not interchangeable labels or proof that every alloy held at the named temperature fails. Composition, phase balance, time and thermal history determine susceptibility. Welds and heat-affected zones may have a different history from base material. Examination must establish the relevant microstructural/property condition.

**Exercise.** A duplex component has an undocumented high-temperature excursion. **Worked decision:** obtain actual exposure and fabrication records, request a qualified material-specific evaluation and do not infer fitness from corrosion resistance alone. **Changed case:** chemistry matches the specified grade. Grade identification does not establish phase balance or retained toughness.

## C07 — Graphitization versus graphitic corrosion

Graphitization of susceptible steel concerns transformation of its carbon-bearing microstructure during thermal exposure; location and distribution affect significance. TWI identifies weld heat-affected zones as an important location in welded carbon steels. [S14] Graphitic corrosion of cast iron is selective corrosion that can leave a weakened residual structure. Similar names do not imply similar causes or detection plans.

**Exercise.** Compare a hot steel weld zone with internal structural change against a wet cast-iron pipe whose shape remains but strength is degraded. **Worked decision:** the first needs thermal/metallurgical evaluation; the second needs selective-corrosion condition assessment. **Changed case:** both have normal outside diameter. Geometry does not clear either property concern.

## C08 — Spheroidization, decarburization and strength

Spheroidization changes carbide morphology in susceptible steels under suitable thermal histories. Decarburization changes carbon content in affected regions. Both require interpretation against the material and intended service; they are not synonyms for a surface oxide layer. A metallographic observation must be connected to the engineering question, not merely named.

**Exercise.** A hot-service sample shows altered carbides and a softened surface region. **Worked decision:** determine depth, distribution, history and property consequences with qualified analysis. Do not machine away an assumed shallow layer without a repair/design basis. **Changed case:** a surface hardness test is normal elsewhere; it does not characterize the sampled region or full depth.

## C09 — Reheat and stress-relaxation cracking

Cracking can develop during heat treatment or high-temperature service as stresses relax in a susceptible weld/material structure. The relevant combination includes alloy, fabrication, heat treatment, restraint and service history. TWI's research identifies the interaction of loading and microstructure in stress-relaxation cracking. [S15] “Stress relieved” is therefore not universal proof against every cracking mechanism.

**Exercise.** A weld develops indications after an elevated-temperature treatment despite passing the initial examination. **Worked decision:** compare the timing and location with treatment records, characterize the indications and obtain specialist evaluation. **Changed case:** the treatment chart is complete but its thermocouple position is unverified. Preserve that uncertainty rather than declaring the entire weld followed the intended cycle.

## C10 — Hydrogen-assisted cracking outside wet sour service

Hydrogen-related cracking can arise in susceptible high-strength materials from sources other than wet H2S, including fabrication or electrochemical processes. Do not require visible rust or a sour-service label before considering it. Material strength/hardness, hydrogen source, stress and time after processing are relevant. Delayed failure can occur after an apparently satisfactory initial inspection.

**Exercise.** High-strength fasteners fail after an undocumented plating operation. **Worked decision:** quarantine the affected batch within the responsible quality process, preserve processing records and request metallurgical analysis. Do not merely replace individual failed bolts from the same unassessed batch. **Changed case:** chemistry matches the grade; processing-related hydrogen and property condition remain unassessed.

## C11 — Ammonia, carbonate, amine and caustic SCC distinctions

These labels identify different environment/material systems, not one common universal cracking threshold. Ammonia-service cracking in steel has a different review basis from ammonia effects on copper alloys. Carbonate, amine and caustic systems require their own chemistry, weld-stress and material review. Local concentration can depart from bulk samples. A control suitable for one service can be ineffective or harmful in another.

**Exercise.** A plant proposes copying its amine crack-inspection plan to a caustic vessel without reviewing temperature, concentration or weld condition. **Worked decision:** retain useful administrative structure but rebuild the mechanism-specific technical basis. **Changed case:** both are carbon steel. Common base alloy does not establish identical exposure, morphology or control requirements.

## C12 — Acid-service differentiation

Sulfuric, hydrochloric, hydrofluoric, phosphoric and organic-acid services cannot share an unqualified material-compatibility decision. Concentration and contaminants can change the mechanism; phase change can concentrate a corrosive liquid; turbulence can remove protective films. HF also requires its specialized exposure controls. Do not instruct a learner to verify compatibility by a field drop test, unprotected sample or trial mixing.

**Exercise.** A supplier gives a compatibility statement without concentration or temperature. **Worked decision:** obtain its exact applicability and the process envelope before acceptance. **Changed case:** normal conditions fit but startup dilution does not appear on the statement. The startup condition requires review; the normal-service statement cannot be extended by assumption.

## C13 — Fretting and wear at contact points

Small repeated relative movements at a loaded contact can damage surfaces and initiate cracks. Supports, clamps and attachments deserve attention when vibration or thermal motion exists. A contact scar can coexist with corrosion; finding rust does not prove corrosion alone caused the loss. Inspect the contact geometry, movement history and adjacent crack-prone regions.

**Exercise.** A pipe support has a polished wear band, debris and recurring movement. **Worked decision:** investigate movement and local condition, select suitable examination and have engineering resolve the support/load arrangement. **Changed case:** adding a tighter clamp stops visible movement but changes restraint. That modification needs a stress/thermal-expansion review rather than automatic acceptance.

## C14 — Boiling, condensation and concentration cells

Phase change can concentrate dissolved species, expose alternate wet and dry regions or change gas/liquid contact. A boiler-water bulk sample may not represent a deposit-covered tube surface. Condensate can have a different chemistry from feedwater. Local oxygen, pH and flow history matter. Treat boiler-water corrosion, under-deposit attack and steam/condensate concerns as location-specific questions.

**Exercise.** Bulk chemistry is within the site's target while deposits cover a hot tube and localized loss is found. **Worked decision:** investigate local concentration and heat transfer, characterize deposits and metal condition, and review treatment/cleaning through the responsible specialist. **Changed case:** a treatment adjustment improves the sample but does not establish tube integrity or remove old deposits.

## C15 — Material-specific high-temperature attack

Nitriding, carburization, metal dusting and oxidation have different chemical and structural consequences. A hard surface can conceal reduced ductility; loss of metal can coexist with altered substrate properties. Fuel-ash deposits can change the hot-surface environment. Record gas composition, metal temperature, deposits, exposure time and alloy before selecting the candidate set.

**Exercise.** A tube has a hard altered surface and internal carbonaceous exposure. **Worked decision:** investigate carburization and related candidates with suitable analysis rather than assuming hardness means improved service resistance. **Changed case:** a second tube has oxide scale under an oxidizing atmosphere. Rebuild its mechanism model; appearance of a dark scale alone does not make the two cases equivalent.

## C16 — Chloride SCC and pitting are not interchangeable

Localized corrosion can create stress concentration or aggressive chemistry, but a pit and an SCC crack remain different assessment problems. A smooth thickness map does not establish absence of tight cracks. A positive surface indication needs characterization and disposition, not an automatic label based only on chloride exposure. Material susceptibility and stress still matter.

**Exercise.** A stainless weld area has branching indications after wet chloride exposure, while a neighboring region has isolated rounded pits. **Worked decision:** document two morphologies, obtain suitable crack and localized-loss assessments and review their common exposure. **Changed case:** chloride is not detected in a later rinse sample. That does not reconstruct the historic concentrated environment or erase existing indications.

## Clinic assessment record

For each C01–C16, record: learner/date; two mechanism-specific facts; one missing discriminating observation; one appropriate next action; one prohibited inference; changed-case response. Score each of the five content elements0–2, pass8/10, and require correction of every unsafe intervention or unsupported fitness declaration. The instructor accepts equivalent correct reasoning and records the reason for any0 or1. For reassessment, use the changed case without showing its worked response, then ask what additional evidence would close the specific uncertainty. These are formative applications; they do not replace the controlled final or its separate scoring.

# VUC038 application workbook

All assets, measurements and acceptance criteria in this workbook are synthetic teaching data. They are not API limits or approval to operate equipment. Work is performed on paper, drawings or inert teaching specimens. No pressurized, hot, chemically contaminated or energized equipment is used. Worked answers teach reasoning; the separate controlled final uses different records.

## Lab A — A corrosion-rate record that does not tell the whole story

**Record A1.** Carbon steel spool CS-18, same marked location and calibrated procedure: 2022-11-01 9.2 mm; 2024-11-01 8.8 mm; 2026-11-01 8.0 mm. Synthetic engineering minimum for this exercise: 6.0 mm. The feed changed in January 2025. Mapping finds a separate local minimum of 5.7 mm at the six-o'clock position. The mapping report gives that minimum an uncertainty of ±0.2 mm. The routine point has no stated uncertainty in the old report.

**Tasks.** Calculate long- and short-term average rates, a simple arithmetic remaining time at the routine point using each rate, and the difference between local minimum and supplied criterion. Explain which result controls escalation and what the arithmetic cannot establish.

**Worked solution.** Long term: (9.2−8.0)/4 = 0.30 mm/year. Short term: (8.8−8.0)/2 = 0.40 mm/year. Routine-point time is (8.0−6.0)/0.30 = 6.67 years or /0.40 = 5 years. These are extrapolations, not approved intervals. The mapped minimum is 0.3 mm below the supplied criterion; even its upper uncertainty bound of 5.9 mm remains below. The local result needs immediate integrity disposition under the exercise procedure, independent of the favorable routine-point arithmetic. The changed feed also undermines an assumption of stationary loss rate. Request the mechanism review, coverage and approved localized-damage assessment.

**Changed record A2.** Replace the minimum with 6.1±0.2 mm and remove the feed-change record. The interval spans 5.9–6.3 mm; it straddles the criterion. Do not claim a demonstrated pass or average the uncertainty away. Resolve measurement and operating-history uncertainty.

**Scoring, 10 points.** Two each for correct rates, times with limits, local comparison, uncertainty interpretation and actionable handover. Passing requires8/10 and no operating approval based solely on arithmetic. Remediation: compare a point trend with a spatial map, then solve A2 independently.

## Lab B — Candidate mechanisms across a real operating cycle

**Record B1.** E-28 is a stainless process cooler. Normal operation: hot nominally dry gas; outlet cooler than inlet. Shutdown: water wash, two days open to air, no verified drying record. External insulation: coastal exposure, broken seal at a support. Weld heat-treatment and sensitization evidence unavailable. Last inspection: external visual jacket review and six spot thickness readings; no insulation removal or crack examination. A repair proposal says “no general corrosion, return to service.”

**Tasks.** Produce separate internal-operating, shutdown and external candidate lists. Identify facts needed to distinguish candidates and the inadequacy in the repair proposal.

**Worked solution.** Internal candidates depend on condensate and contaminants; obtain actual outlet phase/chemistry rather than assuming the whole exchanger is dry. Shutdown creates a different wet/air exposure; sensitization and deposits must be investigated before excluding relevant shutdown SCC mechanisms. External wet chloride exposure makes pitting and chloride SCC credible for susceptible material. Nothing in the existing spot measurements establishes crack coverage or condition beneath insulation. Request material/weld history, deposit and wash records, targeted surface/volumetric examinations selected by a specialist and an authorized disposition. No single mechanism is confirmed by this record.

**Changed B2.** The stainless material is replaced with carbon steel while the exposure stays the same. Rebuild the candidates: external wet corrosion remains credible, but do not mechanically transfer stainless-specific SCC claims. Explain how material identity changes both mechanism and method selection.

**Scoring.** Five criteria0–2: cycle separation, material specificity, evidence requests, method limits, controlled disposition. Pass8/10 with no claim that thickness clears cracking.

## Lab C — Sampling bias in hot-process piping

**Record C1.** A circuit has12 components. Components1,4,7,10 are fittings with traceable composition and thicknesses7.8,7.9,7.7,7.8 mm. The eight pipe segments have unknown traceability; only segment6 has been measured, at4.2 mm. Original nominal wall is8.0 mm. The stream is hot and sulfur-containing. No current approved minimum is supplied. The inspection plan calls all12 components “represented by four fittings.”

**Tasks.** Calculate measured component coverage and fitting-only coverage; identify the unsupported inference; design a follow-up record without inventing a minimum.

**Worked solution.** Five of12 components have a measurement:41.67%. Four of12 are the selected fittings:33.33%. Neither fraction establishes adequate coverage. The large difference at segment6 disproves the assumption that the fittings necessarily represent all pipe. Sulfidation is credible, but additional chemistry and service evidence may identify other contributors. Obtain component-level material and condition evidence, expand examination according to the qualified plan, and obtain the actual engineering criterion. Record7 components unmeasured, not “passed.” Nominal wall is not an acceptance criterion.

**Changed C2.** All12 components are measured once but four have unexamined circumferential regions. Component count is100%; area coverage remains incomplete. Explain why “100% inspected” without defining the coverage unit is misleading.

**Scoring.** Correct fractions2; unsupported inference2; mechanism evidence2; missing criterion2; coverage distinction2. Pass8/10; inventing an allowable wall is a critical error.

## Lab D — Hydrogen-service exposure and method selection

**Record D1.** A carbon steel exchanger record lists total pressure80 bar absolute, hydrogen mole fraction0.70, normal metal temperature340°C and a recorded excursion of390°C lasting6 hours. The design file's old screening sheet uses40 bar hydrogen partial pressure. Inspection is routine thickness UT. No HTHA-specific assessment is supplied. These temperatures are facts in a synthetic record, not safe/unsafe boundaries.

**Tasks.** Estimate hydrogen partial pressure, compare the stale input, and specify the investigation. Explain the difference between wet sour cracking and HTHA in this record.

**Worked solution.** Ideal-mixture estimate:80×0.70=56 bar absolute,16 bar above the old input, or40% higher. Confirm process-model applicability and actual composition. The record calls for qualified HTHA susceptibility/condition review using actual material and exposure history; routine thickness alone is insufficient. Wet sour mechanisms require their own wet-environment evidence and cannot be substituted merely because hydrogen is involved. Preserve excursion duration and location. Reducing the temperature does not reverse prior metallurgical damage.

**Changed D2.** Total pressure is80 bar gauge and ambient pressure is1 bar for the exercise. Absolute pressure is81 bar; estimated partial pressure56.7 bar. The pressure basis must be corrected before comparison. No safe-service conclusion follows from either calculation.

**Scoring.** Calculation2, pressure basis2, mechanism distinction2, suitable specialist/method request2, irreversible-damage limit2. Pass8/10 with no chart-free declaration of safety.

## Lab E — Water/steam thinning: mechanical or chemical?

**Record E1.** A carbon-steel wet-steam line has scalloped thinning after a reducer. No abrasive solids are found. Dissolved-oxygen and pH control records show interruptions. A downstream straight run also has loss. The proposed remedy is “install a solids filter.” A pump elsewhere has noise, fluctuating suction conditions and impeller pitting; its chemistry records are stable.

**Tasks.** Build separate candidate models and identify why one remedy cannot address both assets.

**Worked solution.** FAC is credible in the steam line; investigate material, water chemistry, moisture, flow and geometry. The absence of solids does not eliminate chemically driven film dissolution. A filter alone does not address that model. For the pump, cavitation is a candidate needing suction/pressure and hydraulic investigation. Pitting appearance does not conclusively identify it. Inspection and engineering evaluation must characterize each asset before selecting controls. Do not alter water chemistry or pump operation from this classroom record.

**Changed E2.** The line carries a slurry and has directional gouging at the elbow. Mechanical erosion becomes a stronger candidate; corrosion can still interact. Request particle, flow and material evidence rather than forcing the original FAC diagnosis.

**Scoring.** Candidate separation2, physical/chemical distinction2, evidence2, remedy limitations2, safe handover2. Pass8/10.

## Lab F — Fatigue and thermal history

**Record F1.** A small-bore branch cracked twice at the same attachment. Repairs used the same detail. Pump speed changed three months before the first crack. The branch vibrates at a measured dominant frequency during one operating mode. No dynamic assessment exists. Another mixing tee experiences120 documented hot/cold transitions per day, versus20 in the original operating assumption. There are no supplied fatigue design curves.

**Tasks.** Identify the likely initiating histories, calculate the change in daily transition count, and state what cannot be calculated.

**Worked solution.** The branch requires vibration/support and local stress evaluation plus crack examination; repeated rewelding does not remove the driver. The tee experiences six times the assumed daily transitions, a500% increase. That does not mean exactly one-sixth life: stress amplitude, sequence, temperature and environment matter. Without a valid fatigue model and inputs, do not calculate remaining life. Escalate both changed service histories.

**Changed F2.** The dominant vibration disappears after a support change, but no post-repair crack examination has occurred. The improved excitation is not proof that the component is free of existing damage or that the support modification is approved.

**Scoring.** Separate drivers2, arithmetic2, fatigue-model limits2, repair/source distinction2, required follow-up2. Pass8/10; fictitious life calculation is critical.

## Lab G — Inspection report challenge

**Record G1.** Report N-38 says “all good.” Its attachment lists eight UT thickness points, no scan map, no procedure revision and no inaccessible-area statement. The asset has a credible weld-cracking mechanism and an internal lining. Chemistry identification was performed on one nozzle only. Operations asks whether the whole vessel can return to service.

**Tasks.** Rewrite the conclusion as defensible statements and issue a focused request for missing evidence.

**Worked solution.** Eight recorded thickness values are available at the stated points; their procedure and representativeness remain unresolved. No demonstrated weld-crack examination is supplied. One nozzle's chemistry does not identify the full vessel. Lining and substrate condition need separate coverage. Request procedure/revision, calibration/qualification, location map, limitations, suitable crack examination, material traceability and authorized acceptance criteria. The report cannot support the requested whole-vessel clearance.

**Changed G2.** A qualified scan map arrives but covers only accessible welds. State precisely which welds remain unassessed and what decision remains held. Do not erase an access limitation by changing “all” to “most.”

**Scoring.** Evidence/claim distinction2, crack-method gap2, material limitation2, access/lining limits2, actionable request2. Pass8/10; whole-vessel approval is critical.

## Lab H — Integrity operating window and change control

**Record H1.** Synthetic site window IOW-7 requires an engineering review when a specified monitored contaminant exceeds20 mg/L for more than2 hours. This is a supplied project trigger, not an API threshold. Samples at08:00,10:00,12:00 are24,27,23 mg/L; at14:00 the result is18. The operator closes the event because the last sample is normal. A new feed began at06:00. No continuous record exists.

**Worked solution.** Three samples over four hours exceed the trigger. The normal14:00 sample does not assess prior damage. Review feed change, sampling reliability, susceptible assets and any required inspection. **Changed H2.** Calibration error invalidates the10:00 sample. The other results and feed change remain. Correct the evidence and reassess; do not treat invalid data as either proof of continuous exceedance or proof of no event.

**Scoring.** Evidence2, duration uncertainty2, response2, change review2, honest record correction2. Pass8/10.
