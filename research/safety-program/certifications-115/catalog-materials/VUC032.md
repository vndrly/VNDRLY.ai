# VNDRLY/U Offshore Safety Orientation — offshore instruction

Original VNDRLY instruction · Version 1.0 · 17 November 2026

These eight offshore foundation chapters are reused within VUC032 and followed by its fourteen survival chapters. The original VUC002 Energy Safety Core component is not part of this foundation extract. It prepares a worker to recognize offshore exposures and act within an assigned role. It does not issue a SafeGulf card or qualify someone to operate a crane, enter a confined space, conduct rescue, or perform helicopter underwater escape. Actual transport and facility instructions govern equipment-specific actions.

## O01 — Preparing for an offshore assignment

### Objectives

Prepare a travel and personal-readiness plan; distinguish identification, access permission and task qualification; resolve fatigue, medication and extended-stay concerns before boarding.

An offshore assignment starts before departure. A worker who arrives at the shorebase without the correct documents, too fatigued to follow a briefing, or without an adequate supply of necessary medication has a work-planning problem, not merely a personal inconvenience. Distance, transport schedules and weather can delay access to ordinary support. Preparation makes those limitations visible while they can still be addressed.

Confirm the destination, reporting time, dispatcher contact, transport mode and baggage arrangements with your employer. Ask which identification, operator-accepted orientation, medical clearance and task qualifications are required for your particular assignment. These are different records. A general safety course does not establish medical fitness or authorization to operate equipment. A maritime access credential does not prove completion of a site induction. Check names, dates and the method used to verify records before travel rather than asking the dispatcher to resolve everything at boarding.

Plan the journey so you can arrive rested. Driving through the night and then starting a demanding shift makes the journey part of the exposure. Tell the responsible supervisor when delays or an extended previous shift undermine the plan. Stop safely before consulting navigation or communicating a route change. Do not try to recover lost time by speeding. The useful question is whether the revised journey and work schedule remain safe, not whether the original arrival time can still be reached.

Use the employer's confidential process for medication and fitness questions. Prescription ownership, correct use and fitness for a particular duty are separate issues. An over-the-counter product can affect alertness; a prescribed product can also require review. Keep medication in its identified container and follow the relevant travel and company requirements. Do not take another person's medication or stop a prescribed treatment merely to pass a work check. Consult the appropriate healthcare professional and employer fitness contact about safe arrangements. Share necessary fitness restrictions through the designated channel rather than broadcasting medical details to coworkers.

Prepare for a delayed return. Arrange essential home responsibilities, sufficient permitted personal supplies and a way to contact the appropriate family or support person. Confirm how the employer handles weather extensions and medication shortages. A backup plan is not a promise that a worker will remain offshore indefinitely. If an essential need cannot be met, report it early so the responsible people can change the travel or assignment plan.

Check prohibited-item rules before packing. Weapons, ammunition, alcohol, illegal drugs, ignition sources and other restricted articles may be controlled by the facility or carrier. Never assume an item allowed in a private vehicle is permitted in baggage or on a helicopter. Declare uncertainty before entering a controlled area. Security staff, not a coworker's informal assurance, resolve an access issue.

### Worked situation

Mara has a current orientation record but has driven seven hours after a night shift. Her flight is delayed, and she has only enough prescribed medicine for the planned return date. She proposes boarding first and sorting the details out offshore. The appropriate action is to tell dispatch and her supervisor about the fatigue and supply problem before departure, use the confidential fitness channel, and obtain a revised plan. Her orientation record does not remove either limitation. The error would be concealing the problem to avoid missing transport; that removes the opportunity to arrange a safe alternative.

### Practice and explained response

List the separate decisions in Mara's situation. A complete response identifies transport eligibility, present fitness, medication continuity and work scheduling. The learner should name the responsible contacts and state that a travel booking alone resolves none of these decisions. If the response is only “get more sleep,” revisit how travel, duty and delayed return interact.

## O02 — Shorebase arrival, security and accountability

### Objectives

Use the authorized access process; explain maritime security levels; correct manifest discrepancies; maintain accountability from the shorebase to the offshore destination.

A shorebase combines passenger movements, freight handling, traffic and controlled access. Arriving at the right address does not mean every route through the property is open to you. Park where directed, secure the vehicle, report to the named dispatcher or security contact, and stay within the permitted waiting area. Read the local instructions rather than following a group that may have a different destination or permission.

For covered maritime secure areas, a Transportation Worker Identification Credential concerns access screening. The facility or vessel controls actual entry. Where an authorized escort arrangement is available, follow that arrangement; do not assume an escort must be offered. A person with a valid card can still be denied a particular area or directed to remain with an escort. Never lend an access card, follow closely through a controlled door, or treat a familiar uniform as proof of authorization. Resolve missing or expired documentation through security before entry.

MARSEC is a maritime-security alert framework. Level 1 maintains baseline protective measures. Level 2 adds protection for heightened risk. Level 3 applies further specific measures when an incident is probable, imminent or has occurred. Workers do not select the level themselves. Follow the current facility instructions, which can change screening, waiting locations or movement permissions. Do not photograph or publicize sensitive access arrangements to explain a delay. Report a suspicious item or behavior to the designated security contact without investigating it yourself.

The passenger manifest is an accountability record. Confirm your identity, destination and any required passenger or baggage information through the carrier's process. Do not understate weights, move baggage between people to conceal a limit, or change seats when seating is assigned for loading or emergency arrangements. A spelling discrepancy is worth resolving before departure because an emergency team may depend on that record. Notify dispatch if you miss a movement or transfer to another authorized transport; an unchanged manifest can leave rescuers looking in the wrong place.

Attend the transport briefing even if you have travelled before. The vessel, aircraft, exits, flotation equipment, route and conditions may differ. Tell the crew before departure if you cannot hear, understand or physically perform the required passenger action. Ask for a demonstration or other understandable instruction. Nodding through an unintelligible briefing does not make a passenger prepared.

Stay clear of freight operations while waiting. Suspended loads, reversing equipment and mooring activity can cross what looks like an empty walkway. Use designated pedestrian routes, respect barriers and obtain positive direction when the route changes. Do not move a barrier or duck beneath a load to reach a boarding group. Report the conflict so the movements can be separated.

### Worked situation

The manifest lists Jon for Platform East, but his supervisor's message says Platform West. A coworker suggests boarding because both are “nearby.” Jon stays in the designated waiting area and asks dispatch to reconcile the destination with the supervisor. He does not simply amend a paper list himself or board a different craft. A wrong destination can break both work authorization and emergency accountability.

### Practice and explained response

Security announces additional screening while a worker insists that a valid access card means immediate entry. Explain the error. The card is one part of access control; current facility security instructions still apply. The appropriate response is to follow screening and contact the authorized security representative about the delay, not evade the line or borrow another entrance.

## O03 — Helicopter travel and helideck awareness

### Objectives

Identify passenger hazards around a helicopter; use the crew's briefing and loading instructions; distinguish ordinary passenger preparation from specialist underwater-escape training.

Rotor movement, noise, wind and constrained deck space make a helicopter different from a car with overhead blades. The safest passenger movement is the one directed by the flight or helideck crew for that aircraft and location. Remain in the designated waiting place until directed. Do not infer permission from an open door, another passenger's movement, or your previous trip.

Before entering the movement area, secure loose belongings and follow instructions for baggage, headwear, eye and hearing protection, and clothing. A loose article can become a distraction or an airborne hazard. Do not chase a dropped item toward an aircraft or over the deck edge. Stop in a safe location, alert the crew, and let them control recovery. Keep long objects and baggage in the manner the crew specifies; do not lift them overhead. Never walk toward the tail or choose an unbriefed route around the aircraft.

Provide accurate information for the passenger and baggage loading process. Aircraft loading concerns both total load and its distribution. An apparently small unreported item or unauthorized seat change can defeat that plan. Let the crew handle loading limits and seating; passengers do not calculate an exception or trade places informally. Report a seatbelt or seat problem before movement rather than improvising a restraint.

In the briefing, locate your restraint, normal exit and the emergency arrangements relevant to your seat. Understand who directs door operation, how instructions will be communicated, and what passenger safety equipment is provided. Ask the crew to resolve a point you do not understand. Do not touch aircraft controls or open doors except as instructed. During a critical phase, avoid distracting the crew; communicate an urgent safety problem using the briefed method.

Overwater equipment is aircraft- and operation-specific. The briefing and required specialist training establish how to use the provided flotation or breathing equipment and when to act. This written orientation cannot replace a supervised helicopter underwater-escape or water-survival course. It deliberately does not teach a universal ditching sequence: seat orientation, exits, restraints and equipment differ. Before accepting an assignment, confirm that any required transport survival training is current and accepted by the operator.

Arrival still requires direction. Stay secured until the crew authorizes the next movement. Leave by the designated route, maintain the instructed clearance and go to the identified safe area. The helideck can have simultaneous freight or fuel activity, weather exposure and limited escape paths. Do not stop for photographs or stand in a route while searching your bag. If the deck is wet or moving, follow the crew's movement instructions and report any inability to proceed safely.

### Worked situation

A passenger's cap blows away as the group approaches a helicopter. The passenger turns toward the tail to retrieve it. The safe response is to stop the unsafe movement from a safe position and alert the crew. Another passenger should not run after the person through the same hazard. Recovering property is secondary to keeping everyone in the controlled passenger route.

### Practice and explained response

A coworker offers to demonstrate emergency exit operation using a method learned on another helicopter type. Explain why you should use the current crew's briefing instead. The aircraft and exit can differ; unapproved practice may damage equipment or create confusion. Ask the crew for the correct demonstration and complete any required specialist training before transport.

## O04 — Vessel travel and personnel transfers

### Objectives

Recognize vessel-motion and transfer hazards; follow the master and transfer crew; identify conditions requiring a hold; explain why this course does not qualify transfer operators.

A vessel and an offshore structure may move differently. A gap that looks manageable can change while a passenger is committed to crossing it. Slippery surfaces, wind, wake, hand injuries and loose baggage add to the exposure. Do not choose your own transfer method or timing. The authorized transfer team determines whether conditions, equipment and personnel are suitable.

Before boarding, receive the vessel briefing and identify the passenger area, alarms, muster location and relevant emergency responsibilities. Locate the instructions or station bill provided by the crew. Make sure the manifest reflects your presence. Wear the specified personal flotation and other protective equipment as instructed; ask the crew to correct fit or a defect before transfer. A carried flotation device is not the same as one correctly worn when required. Do not substitute an unapproved item because it is more comfortable.

Use only the designated boarding arrangement when released by the crew. Keep hands away from surfaces that can close together, moving lines and unguarded edges. Do not use mooring lines as handholds or stand inside loops of line. Keep baggage under the crew's arrangement so it does not block your view or occupy hands needed for safe movement. If the route, handhold or footing is unclear, ask before committing to the transfer. Do not jump a gap or attempt a transfer because the person ahead succeeded.

During transit, remain in permitted areas, secure belongings and use the handholds or seating arrangements provided. Report illness or impairment early. Seasickness can reduce the ability to follow instructions; hiding it until transfer leaves fewer options. Never lean beyond a rail to retrieve property, dispose of waste or obtain a better view. A person overboard requires an immediate alarm and the vessel's response, not an untrained passenger entering the water.

Personnel transfer devices require equipment-specific instruction and a controlled operation. Designs vary: a method suitable for one device may be unsafe on another. Before use, the transfer team must brief the passenger's position, restraints or handholds, baggage rules, signals and response to an aborted transfer. The designated crew handles equipment inspection, crane operation and transfer authorization. Passengers must report a defect or inability to follow the method, rather than treating a current orientation certificate as competence to rig or operate the system.

If communication is lost, weather changes, a passenger becomes unwell, or the landing area is obstructed, report it immediately and follow the crew's hold or abort instruction. Do not improvise signals. Schedule pressure is not a control for vessel motion. An incomplete transfer can mean remaining offshore or returning ashore under a revised plan; it does not justify self-directed climbing or jumping.

### Worked situation

A supply vessel arrives after dark. A waiting worker sees the platform crew wave but cannot tell whether the signal is directed at passengers or cargo handlers. The worker remains in the designated safe place and asks the passenger-transfer lead for a clear release. A general wave is not a substitute for the agreed signal. The delay prevents a person from entering a cargo movement or a transfer not ready for passengers.

### Practice and explained response

Identify three reasons to request a transfer hold. Suitable answers include an obstructed landing area, unclear communications, defective passenger equipment, or inability to follow the briefing. The learner must describe reporting the condition to the transfer lead and awaiting direction, not personally declaring the entire operation safe after a quick glance.

## O05 — Arrival, living offshore and exposure at the worksite

### Objectives

Complete a site-specific orientation; establish routes, contacts and restrictions; recognize dropped-object, hand, clothing and living-area hazards; respond to changing weather and extended duty.

Transport arrival and permission to begin work are separate events. Report to the designated person, confirm accountability and receive the facility-specific orientation before starting an assignment. Find out who is in charge, how the shift is organized, which areas are restricted, and what operations are occurring. Know the alarm meanings, muster arrangements, emergency contacts and alternate routes. Ask how a visitor obtains an escort or task authorization. A general course cannot provide the current layout or today's conflicting operations.

Walk the permitted route with the appropriate guide. Locate the actual muster area and understand an alternative if the normal path becomes unsafe. Identify where required protective equipment is worn and where equipment must be kept out of accommodation or food areas. Look for changes in floor level, open gratings, wet stairs, hoses and overhead work. Use handrails and maintain stable footing; do not carry a load that prevents seeing the route or using required support. Report damaged stairs, guards or lighting rather than normalizing them because other people pass that way.

Above and below are connected offshore work areas. A small tool dropped from height can strike someone far below, while a loose fitting may fall beyond a tidy-looking exclusion zone. Respect dropped-object controls and do not pass beneath overhead work. Workers assigned tools at height need the correct tool-retention system and training; tying an improvised cord to an unsuitable point can create another hazard. Report unsecured objects from a safe position. Do not reach over an edge to retrieve one or remove a barrier because the task appears paused.

Protect hands by understanding where movement can trap them. A door, hatch, pipe, transfer device or shifting cargo can close a gap suddenly. Use the designated handles and keep hands out of pinch points; a glove cannot prevent crushing. Select task-appropriate protection through the employer's assessment and instruction. Cut resistance, impact protection, chemical resistance and electrical protection are not interchangeable. Check condition, fit and compatibility. Fire-resistant clothing also has limits: damage, contamination, inappropriate layering or incorrect wearing can undermine protection. Follow the specified clothing requirements rather than treating a brand label as a complete protective system.

Living quarters are part of the work environment. Observe quiet periods, hygiene and smoking restrictions, keep escape routes clear, and store personal items where they cannot fall or obstruct movement. Report illness through the designated medical or supervisor process. Respect confidential personal information and maintain professional conduct in shared accommodation. Do not tolerate threats or harassment as an unavoidable feature of close quarters; use the reporting routes explained by the employer and seek immediate help for an urgent threat.

Weather can affect access, visibility, footing, vessel movement and evacuation options. Follow the facility's weather restrictions and announcements. Do not invent a personal wind or wave limit. If conditions change during a task, pause the affected work and notify the responsible supervisor. The same applies to fatigue from extended duty or interrupted rest. A revised schedule requires a revised readiness decision; a worker's presence offshore does not mean that person remains fit for every task.

### Worked situation

After arrival, a contractor is asked to carry equipment through a stairwell beneath scaffold work. The marked route is closed, and the alternative has not been explained. The contractor asks the site contact to identify an authorized route and coordinate the conflicting work. Choosing the closed route because it is shorter defeats both access control and dropped-object protection. Starting the task before orientation would also leave the worker uncertain about accountability and emergency routes.

### Practice and explained response

Explain why high-impact gloves do not justify placing a hand between moving cargo and a rail. The protection does not remove the crushing exposure. The safer response is to keep clear and use the authorized handling method, with the responsible crew controlling the movement.

## O06 — Applying SEMS to an ordinary work decision

### Objectives

Explain how the safety and environmental management system affects a worker's job; distinguish stop-work and restart decisions; identify changes requiring review; communicate hazards and preserve accurate records.

An offshore safety and environmental management system connects management decisions to the conditions in which work occurs. For a worker, the practical question is not whether a binder exists. It is whether the correct information, people, procedure and protective arrangements are available for the actual task. On covered U.S. offshore operations, the operator's SEMS program includes documented arrangements for hazards, changes, procedures, training, equipment integrity, emergency response, learning from incidents and worker participation. Follow the program applicable to your facility and role.

Think of the system as a chain of decisions. Facility information describes what is present. Hazard analysis identifies how people or the environment could be harmed. A task-level job safety analysis makes those concerns concrete for the job. Operating procedures and safe work practices explain the authorized method. Training establishes who has the skills and knowledge to perform it. Equipment-integrity and pre-startup arrangements address whether the physical system is ready. A break in any link should not be covered up with another signature.

Before a task, participate in the briefing. State the step you will perform, the exposure you could encounter, the control you depend on and the person controlling the interface. Ask what happens if conditions change. If the work pack identifies one pump but the physical label identifies another, stop before intervention. A permit authorizes specified work under specified conditions; it does not make contradictory information harmless. Do not operate a valve to discover which equipment is connected.

Changes can be physical or organizational. A replacement component, revised procedure, different crew, temporary bypass or altered simultaneous operation can affect risk. Workers identify the change and refer it through the facility process; they do not decide that a change is “like for like” merely because a replacement fits. Management-of-change review and a task briefing serve related but different functions. Updating a JSA cannot by itself approve an engineering change, and an approved engineering change still needs communication to the people doing the work.

Stop-work authority means a person can raise a hazardous condition and initiate the site's safe stop process. Use a clear message about the location, exposure and requested hold. Do not create another emergency with an unplanned abrupt equipment action; follow the procedure for stopping safely. The facility also identifies ultimate work authority for operational safety decisions. Knowing that responsible role matters when a disagreement or unresolved hazard persists. The person who reports a concern is not automatically the person permitted to restart the equipment.

Employee participation includes contributing observations, asking questions and reporting unsafe conditions. Use the internal routes provided, and understand the available route for reporting concerns to BSEE for covered operations. A supervisor's reluctance to hear a concern does not erase that route. Use the current official reporting information provided in the source register or facility posting. Give factual information: what was observed, where, when and what exposure may remain. Do not alter evidence, speculate about motives or enter danger to collect proof.

Records should support the next decision. A truthful JSA, inspection report, handover or incident account identifies the actual condition and any remaining restriction. Do not sign for an inspection you did not perform, mark someone present when unaccounted for, or erase an unsuccessful observation to make a record look clean. Incident investigation and audits should lead to corrected conditions and verified learning. A completed form is not evidence that the physical control works.

### Worked situation

A valve replacement is delayed, so a supervisor proposes a temporary hose connection for one shift. A worker recognizes that the connection differs from the work pack and may affect pressure containment and routing. The worker requests a hold and refers the proposed change to the responsible engineering and operating authorities through the facility process. The worker does not select a hose rating or approve the bypass from this orientation. Restart follows the authorized review, suitable equipment, revised instructions and communication to affected crews.

### Practice and explained response

Who resolves a conflict between a schedule request and an unresolved control? Identify the facility's responsible authority and escalation route. A complete answer preserves the hold, communicates the specific hazard, and distinguishes authorization to resume from a general request to hurry.

## O07 — Marine debris, spills and environmental accountability

### Objectives

Prevent loss of material to the sea; distinguish routine waste handling from release response; report a loss accurately; recognize that orientation does not replace required environmental training.

Marine debris begins as an ordinary work-planning problem: loose packaging, an unsecured lid, a damaged waste container or material staged where wind and movement can carry it away. Once in the water, an object may be difficult to recover and can harm wildlife, vessels or other users. Prevention is more reliable than trying to retrieve a lost item from an exposed edge.

Before unpacking or moving supplies, identify where wrapping, offcuts and small parts will go. Use the designated containers and secure them under the facility's arrangements. Keep lids or other closures effective; do not place light material on an open bin and assume it will remain there. Inspect the route and staging location for wind, drainage openings, edges and nearby work. A tidy indoor packing method may fail on an exposed deck. Ask the responsible person to correct a damaged container or unsuitable staging arrangement before material is released.

Keep waste streams identified and separated according to the site plan. A food container, contaminated absorbent, oily rag, chemical container and damaged battery do not become the same waste because they fit in one bag. Consult the label, applicable product information and designated environmental contact when classification is uncertain. Do not pour unwanted liquid into a drain or overboard. Do not use an unlabeled container to make a material easier to transport. The person responsible for waste handling determines the authorized storage and disposal route.

Report a dropped object or release promptly. From a safe position, identify the material if known, approximate amount, location, time, movement and any immediate exposure. State uncertainty instead of inventing a quantity. Keep others out of the affected area and follow the site's notification instructions. Do not climb outside rails, lean overboard or enter the water to recover property. Recovery decisions belong to authorized personnel using a planned method.

A spill may involve more than pollution. Vapors, fire, slippery footing, toxicity or pressure can make an apparently small release dangerous. Do not judge an unknown material only by the size of the puddle. Follow the alarm, isolation and withdrawal arrangements for your role. An incidental cleanup is appropriate only when the employer's procedure, training, equipment and conditions establish that it is within your assigned capability. If those facts are missing, obtain the appropriate response. Never wash a spill away to improve appearances or conceal the event.

Marine-debris instruction has its own applicable program requirements. BSEE's offshore marine-debris program calls for recurring worker training and operator training records. This orientation explains prevention decisions but does not silently certify completion of every required annual program or operator-specific module. Verify the separate requirement with the employer, record the actual training completed and use the current official guidance. The same distinction applies to emergency spill response and hazardous-waste duties.

### Worked situation

A crew opens a pallet of parts on a windy deck. Plastic wrap begins moving toward the edge. A worker suggests stepping outside the rail to catch it. The useful intervention is to stop unpacking, alert the crew, secure remaining material and arrange proper waste containment. If material has already entered the sea, report it accurately. Creating a fall or person-overboard event to avoid reporting debris would compound the incident.

### Practice and explained response

A drip tray contains an unidentified liquid and is close to overflowing. What should you report? State the location, observed condition, unknown identity and any immediate exposure. Keep clear and obtain the responsible response. Do not identify it by smell, mix it with another waste or choose a cleanup method from appearance alone.

## O08 — Offshore alarms, muster, evacuation and recovery

### Objectives

Act on the facility's alarm and muster instructions; preserve accountability; identify the limits of orientation during abandonment or rescue; distinguish emergency reentry from authorization to resume work.

An offshore emergency can affect escape routes and transport options at the same time. Preparation therefore depends on the actual facility plan: alarm meanings, reporting method, primary and alternate muster locations, assigned emergency roles and available evacuation arrangements. Learn these on arrival, not while an alarm is sounding. Confirm how you will receive instructions in noisy work areas or accommodation, and tell the site contact if you cannot perceive the alarm or understand the briefing.

When an alarm or instruction requires withdrawal, follow the site procedure for making your immediate activity safe only within your assigned role and without delaying escape. Do not remain to diagnose the alarm, finish a short task or retrieve personal property. Move by the instructed safe route. If that route is blocked or exposed, use the site's alternative arrangement and communicate your location from safety. Do not enter smoke, an unknown atmosphere or a restricted area because it appears to provide a shortcut.

At muster, report to the accountability person and remain available for instructions. Identify yourself and provide factual information about anyone you last saw, including location and time if known. Do not mark another worker present based on an assumption, leave without being recorded, or circulate rumors that complicate the count. If you are assigned a muster role, follow the specific training for that role. Being helpful does not authorize an untrained person to conduct a search.

Evacuation and abandonment are controlled operations. Lifeboats, rafts, transfer equipment and passenger positions have equipment-specific procedures. Follow the designated crew and your required survival training. This course does not provide a universal water-entry method or authorize a practice jump. Water entry can introduce impact, cold, entanglement, burning-surface or separation hazards; never use it as an improvised shortcut. Raise a concern if you cannot use the planned arrangement and obtain the responsible instruction before an emergency where practicable.

If someone goes overboard, raise the alarm immediately using the vessel or facility's method, report the location and maintain observation from safety if you can. Do not enter the water or improvise a rescue from an unprotected edge. Trained responders and the responsible marine crew coordinate recovery. If assisting from a protected location, perform only the actions you are trained and directed to perform. A second casualty makes the original recovery harder.

Medical response offshore also depends on communication. Give the precise facility location, access route, observed condition and known hazards. Follow the trained responder's instructions within your competence. Do not enter an unsafe scene, move an injured person casually, or provide treatment for which you are not trained. Emergency transport decisions involve medical and operational authorities, weather and suitable transport. Your factual report helps those people plan; a general safety credential does not transfer their authority to you.

After an all-clear, confirm what it permits. Return to accommodation, reentry to a deck and restart of a maintenance task may be separate decisions. Permits, isolations, damaged equipment and conflicting work may need reassessment. Report any control disturbed during the emergency. Participate in the debrief with observations about routes, communications and accountability; record deficiencies so they can be corrected before the next event.

### Worked situation

During an alarm, a worker reaches the primary route and sees smoke beyond the next door. The worker withdraws from that exposure, uses the briefed alternate route and reports to the designated accountability contact. At muster, the worker reports where a colleague was last seen but does not mark that colleague safe. After the all-clear, the worker obtains the responsible task restart instructions instead of assuming the earlier permit automatically remains valid.

### Practice and explained response

Why is “I will go back and check” an unsafe default when someone is missing? The missing person creates a response need, but not safe entry conditions or rescue competence. Give the last-known location to the response team, remain accounted for and perform only an assigned, trained action from safety.

# VNDRLY/U Offshore Safety — Initial Survival

Original VNDRLY teaching, version 1.0, 17 November 2026. Intended for new or returning offshore workers. This package teaches initial survival decisions and supplies supervised practical assessment materials. It does not issue an OPITO certificate, replace a carrier's travel acceptance, or establish that a learner has performed a water exercise. EBS and CA-EBS are different equipment variants; record which is actually taught and assessed. The complete offshore orientation foundation is included in the assembled coursebook, followed by these survival chapters.

## D01 — Readiness and the authority to stop

Offshore emergencies combine isolation, restricted exits and limited time. Training therefore needs both correct decisions and demonstrated actions. A person who can describe a harness but cannot release the actual buckle has an unresolved skill gap. A person who can operate equipment but conceals a health condition also creates a preventable risk. The instructor's job is to make both limitations visible before exposure, not to force everyone through the same exercise regardless of condition.

Read joining instructions, disclose relevant conditions confidentially and confirm the medical pathway for the actual training jurisdiction and activity. Fitness for ordinary offshore employment and fitness for compressed-air shallow-water training are not interchangeable assumptions. Respiratory or ear/nose/throat concerns, a new illness, medication effects or anxiety affecting participation require review through the designated medical route. Instructors do not diagnose or override restrictions. A declaration made last month does not describe a new chest infection today. Stop and report a change before entering the water.

Learn the stop signal on land. Any learner can stop an exercise without being penalized for protecting safety. The team must acknowledge the signal, stabilize the learner, recover safely and review before restarting. An incomplete activity remains incomplete; it is not silently converted to a pass. Privacy matters: the class needs to know an exercise is postponed, not another person's diagnosis.

Worked case: Lena holds an offshore medical but reports painful ears on arrival. A colleague says the pool is shallow so it cannot matter. Lena reports the change; the responsible medical and training personnel decide fitness before pressure exposure. Completing the classroom portion does not authorize an underwater attempt. Practice stating the stop signal, identifying the medical contact and separating attendance from demonstrated competence.

## D02 — Offshore hazards, barriers and personal action

Hydrocarbon release can lead to fire, explosion or oxygen displacement; machinery introduces stored energy, moving equipment and crushing hazards. Suspended loads, slippery decks, noise, chemicals, work at height and confined spaces create additional exposures. The worker's contribution is to recognize the exposure, stay within the authorized task and report a failed barrier. A survival course does not authorize valve isolation, confined-space entry, gas testing or rescue-team duties.

Read the task briefing and find the control that prevents exposure rather than merely the PPE that reduces injury. An exclusion zone separates people from a load; a helmet does not make standing beneath it acceptable. A permit coordinates authorized work but does not make a changed atmosphere safe. When conditions differ from the plan, stop the affected task and notify its responsible controller. Do not cancel another crew's isolation or move a barrier to shorten a route.

At arrival, identify the installation's alarm meanings, temporary refuge, primary and alternative muster points, emergency communications and escape routes. Confirm the person directing the response. Different jurisdictions use different legal frameworks; the facility induction identifies those applicable there. The included offshore foundation explains the US OCS SEMS worker interface as one jurisdictional example, not universal law. Workers should report illness, fatigue, environmental hazards and near misses early. Secure waste and loose materials; an item swept overboard can harm marine life and create a navigation hazard.

Worked case: work beside a noisy compressor changes when a hydrocarbon alarm sounds. The worker does not finish a short task or investigate the leak alone. They follow the alarm response and designated safe route, report location and account for themselves. Practice explaining which action belongs to every worker and which belongs to a trained emergency team.

## D03 — Passenger preparation and the helicopter briefing

Before boarding, confirm identity, destination, manifest, baggage and prohibited-item controls. Tell dispatch about a mismatch rather than boarding on someone else's place. Equipment must fit the individual and the aircraft seating arrangement. Where a shoulder-width measurement or other operator requirement applies, verify it through the required process; do not estimate from clothing size. Travel clearance, medical clearance and survival training are separate checks.

Listen to the briefing even if the aircraft looks familiar. Identify the actual seat restraint, nearest and alternative exits, how an exit opens, brace position, EBS controls and lifejacket inflation method. Practise only as instructed. A familiar-looking buckle may operate differently. Ask before departure if you cannot reach or understand a control. Loose straps, baggage or clothing must not obstruct another passenger's escape path.

Approach or leave only when directed by the ground crew and within the approved route. Keep clear of the tail rotor and other restricted areas. Secure loose articles and carry items as instructed. Never chase a dropped object near a helicopter. If a hat blows away, stop and tell the crew; the object is replaceable, a passenger entering a rotor hazard is not. During flight follow crew instructions and remain restrained until the appropriate release instruction or emergency sequence applies.

Worked case: Ravi changes seats after briefing and assumes the same exit remains his primary route. He must understand the new seat's restraint, reference points and exits before departure. The lesson is not to memorize one direction, but to build an aircraft-specific escape plan. In the dry trainer, identify these controls by touch as well as sight.

## D04 — Survival clothing and lifejacket readiness

An immersion suit reduces exposure; a lifejacket provides flotation. A combined approved system can perform several functions, but no item should be credited with an unverified capability. Check the issued system against its instructions: identity and service status, visible damage, closures, correct size, fitted straps, accessible inflation controls and survival aids. Obtain a replacement for a defect rather than taping it over. A badly fitted lifejacket may ride up; a suit closure left open can admit water and defeat protection.

Don in the demonstrated sequence and complete the matching buddy check. The wearer must still understand the equipment after a helper checks it. Confirm reach to restraint and breathing system with the complete ensemble. Do not add unfamiliar accessories that can snag or obstruct controls. Adjustments made after inspection require another relevant check. Training equipment must be distinguished from flight-service equipment so a depleted training cylinder or inert component is never issued for travel.

Follow the helicopter procedure for flotation. Premature inflation inside a cabin can obstruct movement and trap a person against the interior. Inflate only at the appropriate point after clearing the aircraft, as taught for that system and emergency. Outside the aircraft, confirm effective flotation and use the taught backup inflation method if necessary. Do not remove flotation merely because swimming feels easier without it.

Worked case: a passenger's suit fits while standing but prevents reaching the buckle when seated. The correct response is to resolve the fit before boarding, not to hope the emergency will allow more room. Practice the seated reach check, identifying a deliberately tagged defective training item and explaining why inflation timing matters.

## D05 — EBS and CA-EBS: function, limits and checks

A rebreather EBS and a compressed-air EBS cannot be treated as interchangeable. A rebreather depends on its specific breathing-bag and deployment arrangement; a CA-EBS supplies a finite volume of compressed breathing air through its designed regulator. Neither is a general diving system, smoke-escape respirator or authorization to remain underwater. The purpose is limited emergency escape. Learn the exact unit's pre-use inspection, deployment, breathing and recovery instructions with a qualified instructor.

A pressure indication alone does not prove air is available at the mouthpiece. NOPSEMA's 2023 alert described a flight on which multiple cylinders were not correctly configured despite checks. The lesson is to check the complete approved configuration and function through the operator/manufacturer procedure rather than rely on a reassuring gauge color. Do not invent a universal valve direction, pressure threshold or duration: those depend on the unit. Report damaged hoses, contaminated mouthpieces, inaccessible controls, uncertain valve state or an unsuccessful check.

For compressed-air training, pressure changes create hazards distinct from ordinary swimming. Do not hold your breath while ascending after breathing compressed air. Do not descend, ascend or try underwater deployment beyond the instructed controlled exercise. Shallow water does not remove the need for fitness screening, supervision and rescue arrangements. Ear pain, breathing difficulty, chest discomfort or distress means stop and follow the rescue/medical response. Never force pressure equalization or keep attempting because another learner found it easy.

Worked case: a learner practiced with a rebreather last year but receives CA-EBS today. They need the relevant equipment instruction and assessment, not a handwritten conversion on the certificate. During dry practice identify each control and explain the finite-air and pressure-exposure limitations. Actual underwater competence is assessed separately.

## D06 — Dry escape rehearsal and brace preparation

Dry rehearsal reduces the number of unfamiliar actions during a stressful exercise. Sit in the assigned trainer seat with the approved ensemble. Identify the restraint release, stable hand references, primary exit and alternative. Have the instructor demonstrate how the actual exit opens. Practise maintaining orientation while reaching the necessary controls, without pulling another passenger or using a loose object as a handhold. Secure loose straps that could snag.

On a warned emergency, follow crew instructions for brace and preparation. The exact brace posture depends on seating and restraint; use the aircraft briefing rather than a generic pose from a different aircraft. Prepare the breathing system at its prescribed stage. Keep the restraint secured through the phase when it prevents impact injury and loss of orientation. An urge to release immediately is understandable but can leave a person displaced from their known exit.

The training progression is briefing, demonstration, coached dry practice, independent dry demonstration and only then the approved water progression. Before each stage, the instructor checks readiness and confirms stop signals. A learner who cannot reliably identify the release in dry conditions needs more practice before immersion. Speed is not the first success measure; correct control, orientation and response are.

Worked case: an instructor asks a learner to identify an alternative exit and the learner reaches for a window that is not an emergency exit. Correct the understanding in the dry trainer. Do not use submersion to reveal a basic knowledge gap. Practice a second seat position so the learner demonstrates understanding rather than a memorized reach.

## D07 — Controlled helicopter underwater escape

Only a purpose-designed facility with competent staff, a validated exercise plan and immediate rescue capability conducts underwater escape. This written lesson is preparation, not permission to practise in a private pool or submerged vehicle. The instructor controls immersion, rotation, emergency stop and recovery. Learners are briefed on the exact progression and can stop at any stage. No surprise inversion, competitive breath-holding or forced participation is acceptable.

During the approved escape sequence, maintain a known reference and follow the taught timing for restraint and exit operation. Water, reduced visibility and inversion can make normal visual assumptions unreliable. Follow the actual exit path and equipment instructions rather than swim toward a guessed light source. Avoid premature release that loses orientation or premature inflation inside the cabin. Clear the opening without obstructing other occupants and follow the trained route to the surface and survival area.

If disoriented or unable to complete the sequence, use the stop/distress signal taught by the center; trained rescuers intervene. A rescue intervention is valuable evidence of a skill gap, not a shameful event to hide. Record what happened, correct the cause and use a fresh appropriate reassessment only after readiness review. An observer cannot mark an unseen buckle release as correct simply because the learner eventually surfaced.

Worked case: a learner performs an upright escape but freezes during an inverted exercise. Recover safely, check wellbeing, review the precise difficulty and determine the suitable retraining progression. Do not call the inverted exercise passed on the strength of the upright attempt. Each required condition has its own observation record.

## D08 — Cold-water response and conserving capability

Sudden cold-water immersion can disrupt breathing and movement before prolonged cooling becomes the main problem. Use flotation to support the airway, concentrate on regaining controlled breathing and avoid immediately exhausting yourself in a long swim. Keep the mouth and nose clear of spray as the equipment allows. An immersion suit and lifejacket improve protection but do not eliminate cold stress or guarantee a rescue time.

Once stable, assess the realistic safe refuge: a nearby raft under instruction is different from a distant platform against current. Stay with effective flotation and the group where safe. Reduce unnecessary movement and heat loss using the taught supported posture or group arrangement; never adopt a posture that submerges the airway. A person without effective flotation may need movement to remain afloat. Do not impose one posture regardless of the person's condition or equipment.

Use the whistle, light or other approved signaling aids correctly. Conserve energy, keep awareness of aircraft/raft hazards and report a deteriorating companion. Do not tether people together with an improvised arrangement that can entangle or pull them under. In a raft, reduce wind and water exposure, protect casualties and follow the person coordinating survival. Do not drink seawater. Inventory supplies and use them under the survival plan.

Worked case: two survivors want to swim toward a structure that appears close but is moving away in current. Their raft is nearby and the rescue team has their location. Stabilizing flotation, boarding the raft when directed and signaling is safer than an unsupported endurance swim. Practice explaining the decision from current conditions, not an assumed swimming ability.

## D09 — Muster, evacuation and totally enclosed survival craft

An alarm initiates the installation's response, not a private decision to launch a lifeboat. Stop safely as directed, follow the designated route and report at the correct muster point. Know the alternative if the primary route is unsafe. Do not collect personal possessions or return to look for a missing coworker. Tell the muster controller the person's last known location; rescue is assigned to capable teams.

Muster accountability depends on truthful identity and location information. Report yourself once through the prescribed system and correct an error immediately. Do not answer for someone merely because they were seen earlier. A temporary refuge provides a managed place of protection; remain attentive to announcements and changes. Moving to another muster point without telling the controller can create a false missing-person search.

A TEMPSC is a totally enclosed motor-propelled survival craft. Its coxswain and assigned crew control preparation and launch. As a passenger, board only on instruction, use designated handholds, take the assigned seat, secure the restraint and keep access clear. Recognize that launch/release mechanisms are not passenger controls. Remain restrained as directed and follow ventilation, hatch and disembarkation instructions. No classroom learner operates a live release system as a demonstration.

Worked case: smoke blocks the route to muster A. The worker uses the posted safe alternative to muster B and reports identity and reason for relocation. They do not enter smoke to reach the original point or disappear into accommodation. In a static craft trainer, practise boarding, seating and accountability without energizing launch equipment.

## D10 — Liferafts, water entry and rescue interfaces

Controlled evacuation into survival craft is preferable to unnecessary exposure in the sea. Water entry is a last-resort action governed by the emergency and equipment instructions. Check the landing area and hazards, prepare the ensemble correctly and use the trained entry method and permitted height. Never jump into unknown water or perform a high entry because a photograph shows another facility doing so. A practice entry needs its own engineered environment, rescue cover and authorization.

At a liferaft, follow the instructed approach and boarding method. Avoid lines, canopy components and other entanglement points. Use the designed boarding aid and coordinated assistance. Once inside, move to balance the load and make space for others. Do not stand up unnecessarily or use a sharp object carelessly near an inflatable chamber. The assigned person manages lines, sea anchor and equipment in accordance with the raft instructions; do not cut a line merely because it is inconvenient.

For rescue, follow the vessel or helicopter crew's signals. A winch strop or rescue device must be applied as instructed; never improvise a connection to clothing or grab a dangling aircraft component. Control loose articles and await the crew's approach instructions. In training, use a non-lifting ground demonstration unless a qualified facility separately authorizes a rated lifting exercise with its own safeguards.

Worked case: a tired survivor cannot climb the raft ladder alone. Other occupants use the demonstrated coordinated assistance while keeping the raft stable and the person's airway clear. Pulling only on a loose lifejacket can displace it. Practise on the designated trainer and record the actual assistance used.

## D11 — Fire recognition and the decision to withdraw

Fire needs fuel, heat and an oxidizing environment; removing one element can stop combustion, but the method must fit the material and situation. Offshore fires can involve pressurized hydrocarbons, electricity, confined spaces and rapid smoke spread. A worker's initial responsibility is to raise the alarm, communicate the location and follow the emergency plan. Being trained in a portable extinguisher does not make someone a fire-team member.

Consider attempting only a small, developing fire within the person's training and employer authorization, with an effective escape route and an appropriate extinguisher. Withdraw if smoke threatens breathing or visibility, fire spreads, the escape path is threatened, the agent is wrong or the first attempt is ineffective. Never enter an enclosed smoke-filled compartment to investigate. Do not extinguish a pressurized gas flame while allowing unburned gas to continue accumulating; isolation and fire-team control are specialist functions.

Read extinguisher markings and the local fire-class system; letter meanings differ between countries. Select by the actual fuel and electrical hazard, not a remembered color alone. Water is inappropriate for energized electrical equipment and can spread burning flammable liquid. A CO2 discharge can create a serious atmosphere hazard in a confined space, and its discharge components can be extremely cold. Report the fire and the equipment used even if the visible flame disappears.

Worked case: an electrical cabinet emits smoke and an employee reaches for the nearest water extinguisher. The correct action is alarm, withdrawal and authorized isolation/response, not opening the cabinet. Practice explaining the decision and finding the labeled suitable equipment for a separate safe trainer scenario.

## D12 — Portable extinguisher use and escape through low visibility

In a controlled trainer exercise, inspect the extinguisher and identify the approved operating distance, discharge control and limitations. Keep an exit behind or otherwise immediately available, alert others and operate using the manufacturer's demonstrated method. For a conventional appropriate unit this generally involves releasing its safety device, directing agent at the fire base, operating the control and moving the discharge across the burning area. Do not advance blindly into smoke or continue until the cylinder empties when conditions are worsening. Back away safely while monitoring for re-ignition and report the discharge.

Escape equipment must be matched to the hazard. A filter hood is not interchangeable with supplied air and may not protect in oxygen deficiency. Learn the exact device's seal, activation and limitations before relying on it. An escape device is for escape, not permission to enter a dangerous atmosphere, rescue someone or finish a task. If conditions exceed its capability, follow the designated safe refuge/alternate route and emergency instructions rather than invent protection.

Low-visibility training uses a controlled, non-toxic environment and a known rescue system, not actual hazardous smoke. Maintain orientation by the taught route-reference technique, move carefully and communicate as briefed. If a route is blocked, use the known alternative and report at muster. Do not open a hot or suspect door merely to see what is behind it. The facility briefing establishes the actual escape route and door procedures.

Worked case: the planned route in a smoke-simulation exercise is blocked. The learner identifies and follows the briefed alternative, then reports the changed route. Passing means reaching the designated safe point through a defensible method, not crawling rapidly until an instructor pulls them out.

## D13 — Immediate care after rescue

First make access safe and summon the installation emergency medical response. A casualty in hazardous water or a dangerous atmosphere requires capable rescue before a first aider approaches. Once accessible, check responsiveness and normal breathing, call for the AED and appropriate help, and provide care within training. An adult who is unresponsive and not breathing normally needs resuscitation; occasional gasps are not normal breathing. Use dispatcher or medical-team guidance and do not delay for an amateur diagnosis.

For this offshore module, the supervised drowning drill follows the Resuscitation Council UK 2025 sequence: trained rescuers give five initial ventilations, then standard adult CPR. Use a barrier device and ventilation equipment only within training; avoid excessive breaths. Standard adult CPR is thirty compressions to two breaths, at 100–120 compressions per minute and 5–6 cm depth, allowing recoil. Use a manikin to practise, never compress a healthy person's chest. An AED is used after safe removal from water and appropriate chest preparation; keep everyone clear for analysis/shock and resume CPR promptly. Do not waste time trying to drain water from the lungs. Other jurisdictions may use a different approved initial sequence; the instructor records the selected protocol and teaches one coherent method.

A rescued person who is breathing needs observation, protection from cold and medical assessment. Handle a severely cold or injured person carefully, insulate from wind and ground, and remove wet clothing when feasible without delaying urgent care or exposing the person further. Do not rub limbs vigorously, give alcohol or force drinks into an impaired person. Report deterioration immediately. For major bleeding, use direct pressure and the trained escalation method; for burns, stop the source safely, cool appropriately and obtain medical advice.

Worked case: a survivor is talking at first, then becomes drowsy and breathless. Do not assume successful recovery from water ends the emergency. Alert medical responders, reassess and prepare to provide resuscitation if breathing becomes abnormal. Give factual times, observations and treatment in the handover.

## D14 — Integrated survival and keeping the qualification honest

Emergency performance is a sequence of decisions, not a set of disconnected tricks. Travel preparation makes equipment usable; briefing establishes orientation; controlled escape gets a person clear; flotation protects the airway; muster and signaling connect the person with rescue; first aid supports survival until definitive care. A failure early in this chain may not be repaired by enthusiasm later.

Use a structured final scenario. Receive a fictional destination and equipment record, identify a mismatch, correct it through the responsible person, respond to a warned emergency, demonstrate the approved trainer sequence, join the survival group and complete a casualty handover. The assessor records each learner independently. Watching a successful team does not establish the performance of a person who never operated the equipment.

After an incident or drill, account for people, isolate defective equipment and document observations without blame or invented certainty. Preserve the first attempt when reassessment is required. Identify the exact missing skill, provide instruction, then observe a changed exercise under safe conditions. A written pass cannot cancel an unresolved critical practical failure. Changes in aircraft, breathing system, health, employer requirements or elapsed time can require additional training before travel.

Worked case: an employee passes all written questions but has not completed the underwater portion because of a medical restriction. Their record states classroom completion and practical outstanding. It must not say full initial-survival qualification. The materials package can be complete while a particular learner's qualification remains incomplete; these are different facts.

The VNDRLY record names the assessed equipment variant, conditions, dates, evaluators and limitations. It never issues OPITO approval or guarantees acceptance by a carrier. An employer checks actual destination and operator requirements before dispatch. Review lessons periodically, rehearse through authorized drills and report a readiness gap before it becomes an emergency.

A loose article blows toward the aircraft. Retrieve it?

**Answer:** Stop and tell ground crew; do not chase it.

**Reason and remediation:** Rotor hazards outweigh the value of the article. Revisit D03 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D04-L1

A suit fits standing but blocks seated buckle access. Accept it?

**Answer:** No; resolve fit and recheck before travel.

**Reason and remediation:** Essential controls must remain reachable. Revisit D04 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D04-L2

Why not inflate the lifejacket inside the cabin?

**Answer:** It can obstruct movement and trap the wearer; follow the taught clearance/inflation sequence.

**Reason and remediation:** Flotation must be deployed at the appropriate stage. Revisit D04 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D05-L1

Does a green pressure indication alone prove CA-EBS readiness?

**Answer:** No; complete the model-specific configuration/function checks.

**Reason and remediation:** Indicated pressure does not establish the entire breathing path is usable. Revisit D05 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D05-L2

Can rebreather practice automatically qualify someone on CA-EBS?

**Answer:** No; teach and assess the actual variant.

**Reason and remediation:** Equipment function and deployment differ. Revisit D05 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D06-L1

Why rehearse release and exits on land first?

**Answer:** To resolve basic control and orientation errors before immersion.

**Reason and remediation:** Water should not be used to discover an avoidable knowledge gap. Revisit D06 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D06-L2

Should a learner release restraint immediately on a warning?

**Answer:** No; follow the actual emergency sequence and timing.

**Reason and remediation:** Premature release can cause injury or loss of orientation. Revisit D06 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D07-L1

Can this written course authorize private underwater-escape practice?

**Answer:** No; a competent purpose-designed facility and controlled rescue plan are required.

**Reason and remediation:** Uncontrolled restraint/immersion can be fatal. Revisit D07 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D07-L2

If a rescue team intervenes, may the attempt be silently marked independent?

**Answer:** No; record intervention and reassess after review.

**Reason and remediation:** An assisted recovery is not proof of independent performance. Revisit D07 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D08-L1

What is the immediate priority after cold immersion?

**Answer:** Support the airway with flotation and regain breathing control.

**Reason and remediation:** Immediate uncontrolled swimming can worsen exhaustion and breathing difficulty. Revisit D08 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D08-L2

Does an immersion suit guarantee survival for a fixed period?

**Answer:** No.

**Reason and remediation:** Condition, fit, exposure and rescue circumstances differ. Revisit D08 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D09-L1

May you answer present for a friend seen earlier?

**Answer:** No; report last known facts and uncertainty.

**Reason and remediation:** False accountability can delay rescue. Revisit D09 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D09-L2

Who controls TEMPSC launch?

**Answer:** The authorized coxswain/assigned crew under the emergency plan.

**Reason and remediation:** Passengers are not authorized to operate release mechanisms. Revisit D09 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D10-L1

May training water-entry height be chosen from a photograph?

**Answer:** No; use the facility-approved equipment/environment procedure.

**Reason and remediation:** Another installation does not establish safe conditions here. Revisit D10 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D10-L2

Why move to an assigned position after raft boarding?

**Answer:** To keep access clear and balance the raft.

**Reason and remediation:** Uncoordinated crowding can impede others and destabilize the situation. Revisit D10 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D11-L1

Does a portable-extinguisher lesson authorize smoke entry?

**Answer:** No.

**Reason and remediation:** It does not qualify fire-team operations or atmospheric protection. Revisit D11 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D11-L2

What if smoke threatens the escape path?

**Answer:** Withdraw and raise/maintain the alarm.

**Reason and remediation:** A small-fire attempt requires a safe exit. Revisit D11 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D12-L1

What should remain available during an authorized extinguisher attempt?

**Answer:** A safe escape route.

**Reason and remediation:** Fire, smoke or heat must not cut off withdrawal. Revisit D12 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D12-L2

Can an escape hood authorize entry to rescue a coworker?

**Answer:** No.

**Reason and remediation:** Escape capability is not entry/rescue capability. Revisit D12 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D13-L1

Should a rescuer enter dangerous water solely because they know CPR?

**Answer:** No; call capable rescue and use safe aids within competence.

**Reason and remediation:** First-aid training is not aquatic rescue qualification. Revisit D13 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D13-L2

Should you try to drain water from lungs before resuscitation?

**Answer:** No.

**Reason and remediation:** It delays effective ventilation and CPR. Revisit D13 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D14-L1

Can a written pass cancel a failed critical practical?

**Answer:** No.

**Reason and remediation:** Knowledge and demonstrated performance are separate requirements. Revisit D14 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## D14-L2

What must a credential record about equipment?

**Answer:** The variant/model and conditions actually assessed, with limitations.

**Reason and remediation:** A generic label can overstate capability. Revisit D14 and explain the failure in the rejected approach.

## Acceptance distinction

Materials completion means these written instructional and assessment resources exist and have been author-reviewed. It does not mean a facility has been approved, a real learner has passed, current private OPITO clauses have been verified or travel permission has been granted. Those claims require their own evidence and are not fabricated in this package. The training provider must record actual facility, equipment, medical and evaluator details when delivering; blank forms are reusable evidence instruments, not pre-certified outcomes.
