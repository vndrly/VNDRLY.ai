# Program definition, delivery and skills evaluation

VUC016 • VNDRLY/U First Aid, CPR and AED •2026-11-17. Audience: workplace/community lay responders. No prior clinical qualification required. Learners need accessible instruction and safe participation arrangements. A person unable to demonstrate a physical skill can receive a clearly limited knowledge record; do not mark that skill passed or exclude reasonable accommodation. This program does not issue AHA/Red Cross cards, EMS licenses, medication prescribing authority or hazardous/water-rescue qualification. Employer/regulatory acceptance of an own-provider credential is separate and must not be implied.

Outcomes are the fourteen F chapters: safe assessment; adult CPR; AED; pediatric CPR; choking; hemorrhage; injuries; burns/exposures; sudden illness; respiratory/allergy/overdose; heat/cold; poisoning/bites; drowning priorities; handover/readiness. All have instruction, worked application, two explained formative items and four separate final responses.

## Facilitated teaching plan

Plan twelve instructional hours, excluding breaks, extending as needed for safe individual practice and remediation. F01 activation 45 min; F02 adultCPR90; F03 AED60; F04 pediatric 90; F05 choking 60; F06 bleeding 60; F07–F08 injury/burn 75; F09–F10 illness/medication-trainer 60; F11–F13 environment/exposure/drowning 45; integrated practiceF14 andstations 45; controlled final 60; correction/records 30. Total 720 min. Do not award hours from reading length or declare actual delivery through authorship.

Use qualified first-aid/CPR instructors with current subject competence, familiarity with 2025 resuscitation changes, safe training technique and the actual devices. Before first delivery the responsible training director/clinical reviewer verifies alignment with current guidance and local protocols. This author review is not a claim that such independent clinical review has occurred. Instructor competence and delivery review are real-world delivery responsibilities, not pre-signed endorsements.

Equipment: adult/child/infant feedback manikins with measurable compression depth/rate/recoil; replaceable/cleanable lungs and barriers; non-shocking AED trainer with adult/pediatric modes/pads; choking trainers; bleeding/wound-packing/limb simulators and training tourniquets; bandages/gloves; inert inhaler/epinephrine/naloxone trainers; scene cards/maps and simulated dispatcher. No live shocks, medicines, forceful choking maneuvers or CPR on classmates. Clean equipment between learners following manufacturer instructions, provide individual barriers and handle sharps only as inert props. Use at most six learners per hands-on instructor as VNDRLY policy, with enough stations for each person; reduce ratio when necessary. Keep actual emergency access and a stop signal available.

Demonstrate, explain, practice with feedback, then independently assess a new scenario. Numerical CPR targets below are clinical targets; the 90%performance consistency threshold is VNDRLY assessment policy, not a claim that AHA mandates that exact passing percentage. During two-minute independent compression segments require at least 90%of measurable compressions in the applicable rate/depth range with full recoil; review device traces and visual position, all pauses under 10 seconds except a device-directed interval, correct breath ratio and visible rise without forceful excess. Where the manikin cannot measure a required parameter, obtain appropriate equipment rather than claim objective measurement. Preserve the trace and instructor observations; repeat until criteria are demonstrated. The nominal duration does not override failed skills.

## Controlled assessment and correction

56 final responses,2 points each:2 complete correct findings/reasons,1 safe incomplete,0 wrong/absent/unsafe. Pass 90/112 and 6/8 in every domain. Critical unsafe rescue entry, delay in indicated CPR to search for pulse, shock-contact failure, infant abdominal thrusts, blind sweeps, unauthorized medication, deliberate harmful bleeding care or false records requires remediation regardless of score. Final keys are kept privately. Nominal 60 minutes plus accommodations; no completed examples/key or answer coaching.

Nine stations below, each five 0–2 criteria, require 8/10 and all starred criteria 2, plus the specified measurable CPR requirements. A critical failure stops the simulated action. After correction use a fresh scenario and independent observation, preserving original scores. Two reassessments maximum before an individualized additional-instruction plan. Written reassessment covers failed domains plusF14; a written pass never substitutes for a physical failure.

## A — Adult assessment, CPR and AED

Input: safe collapsed adult, gasping, then AED shock/no-shock variants. Task: assess, allocate emergency call/AED, perform two-minute 30:2 manikin CPR and integrate trainerAED. Criteria:*recognition/activation without pulse delay;*measured quality and correct breaths; appropriate pad placement;*clear analysis/shock and prompt CPR resumption; coordinated handover. Repeat with noisy gate/access complication and no-shock prompt. Expected: gasps are abnormal, no-shock does not mean recovery, safe scene remains prerequisite.

## B — Child and infant CPR

Input: unwitnessed pediatric collapse, phone available; second version no phone, then second trained rescuer arrives. Task: demonstrate on both child and infant manikins, single 30:2 and two-rescuer 15:2, with appropriate AED trainer preparation. Criteria:*activation sequence/recognition;*measured age-appropriate depth/rate/recoil; current infant hand technique and child hand choice; controlled visible-rise breaths and ratios; AED pads apart/appropriate mode. Repeat with witnessed sudden collapse and adult-onlyAED. A pass requires both age groups and both ratios, not just an explanation. Newborn resuscitation is not assessed.

## C — Choking transitions

Input: effective cough changing to severe obstruction, then unresponsive; infant variant and late-pregnancy adult card. Task: identify changing condition and demonstrate on appropriate trainers only. Criteria: effective-cough support;*adult/child five-back/five-abdominal sequence;*infant five-back/five-chest without abdominal thrust; chest-thrust adult modification;*CPR transition and visible-object-only removal. Repeat with inability to encircle adult abdomen. Expected: no blind sweep, no force on classmates, emergency activation and follow-up.

## D — Hemorrhage control

Input: limb simulator with ongoing severe flow; second deep packable simulated wound and embedded-object card. Task: activate help, use barriers, maintain direct pressure, apply trainer tourniquet according to actual device procedure until simulated bleeding stops, record time, and pack only the suitable trainer wound under the taught procedure. Criteria: recognition/barrier/activation;*effective pressure;*effective suitable tourniquet placement/tightening with no loosening; appropriate packing/embedded-object boundary; time/response handover. Repeat with persistent flow requiring correction under the actual device protocol. Do not credit merely attaching a tourniquet. No neck/torso tourniquet or chest/abdominal packing.

## E — Injury and exposure priorities

Input: leg deformity with normal breathing, then separate gasping casualty; thermal burn card; electrical contact card; chemical-eye splash using clean eyewash trainer. Task: prioritize lifesaving care, demonstrate safe support/dressing and describe safe cooling/irrigation. Criteria:*no energized contact;*breathing priority over splinting; appropriate injury support/no object removal; safe burn cooling/no harmful remedy; chemical-specific safe irrigation/escalation. Repeat with smoke-inhalation signs. Expected: no neutralization improvisation or movement that delays necessary care.

## F — Sudden illness and medication assistance

Input: stroke last-known-well 09:10/discovery 09:25; seizure 6 minutes; unresponsive low-sugar card; asthma/allergy/overdose cards and inert trainers. Task: recognize urgency, call, choose safe assistance and demonstrate device-specific trainer actions without real medication. Criteria:*appropriate emergency activation; correct last-known-well/seizure protection;*nothing orally to impaired swallowing; appropriate prescribed inhaler/epinephrine trainer procedure;*overdose CPR/breaths/naloxone without delaying resuscitation. Repeat with uncertain aspirin contraindication: wait for professional advice, not an automatic dose.

## G — Environment, poisoning and safe rescue

Input: confused sweating worker, wet cold worker with reduced responsiveness, unknown ingestion, snakebite and drowning recovery. Task: select safe priorities and demonstrate dry-land CPR-with-breaths response plus safe reach/throw explanation. Criteria:*heat emergency cooling/EMS; safe cold handling/no hot bath for impaired person; poison guidance/no induced vomiting; safe bite care/no capture/tourniquet;*no hazardous water entry and prompt appropriate drowning CPR. Repeat with unavailable rescue equipment. No live water or temperature exposure.

## H — Recovery monitoring and transfer

Input: unresponsive but normally breathing nontrauma manikin, then breathing becomes abnormal. Task: demonstrate airway protection/recovery positioning, continued reassessment and transition to CPR, then EMS handover. Criteria: appropriate initial position;*continuous breathing observation;*prompt deterioration recognition/CPR; factual times/care; confirmed transfer/privacy. Repeat with suspected trauma to test avoidance of unnecessary movement while maintaining lifesaving priorities.

## I — Integrated workplace response/readiness

Input: collapsed adult at locked access gate, AED trainer not at expected location, blood-exposure card after care. Task: run independent call/CPR/AED scenario, coordinate access, hand over and restore readiness. Criteria:*safe activation/care; named team/access tasks; equipment readiness fault response; exposure reporting/privacy; truthful skill/incident record and follow-up. Repeat with known device location but inaccessible gate. Maintain CPR while another helper solves access; do not stop lifesaving care to finish paperwork.

# VNDRLY/U First Aid, CPR and AED

Original lay-rescuer instruction, United States emergency-service context. Clinical basis:2025 AHA adult and 2025 AHA/AAP pediatric basic life support, with 2024 AHA/Red Cross first-aid guidance. This is not an AHA/Red Cross-branded course or healthcare-provider qualification. Learn and demonstrate physical skills with qualified instructors, manikins and trainer equipment; never practice compressions, forceful choking maneuvers or medication administration on a healthy person.

## F01 — Scene safety, consent and the first assessment

Look for traffic, electricity, machinery, fire, violence and hazardous atmosphere before approaching. A first-aid credential does not make an alarmed vessel safe to enter. Activate specialist help when access is unsafe. Once access is safe, introduce yourself, ask a responsive person for permission and explain the intended help. For an unresponsive person, provide emergency care under applicable consent rules and dispatcher guidance. Respect a capable person's refusal; summon appropriate help and monitor without forcing treatment.

Check responsiveness and normal breathing promptly. Shout, tap appropriately and look for breathing; do not spend more than about ten seconds deciding whether breathing is normal. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing. A lay rescuer does not delay CPR searching for a pulse. Unresponsive and not breathing normally means activate emergency services, obtain an AED and begin CPR. Use a phone on speaker if available. Assign specific helpers: one calls 911 and confirms, another brings the AED, another opens the correct access gate. Do not replace emergency activation with a message to a manager.

For an unresponsive person breathing normally, maintain an open airway, monitor continuously and obtain help. A recovery position can protect the airway when no trauma or other contraindication requires a different approach; if breathing becomes abnormal, place supine and start CPR. Avoid unnecessary movement after major injury, except for immediate safety or lifesaving care. Use gloves and available barriers without unnecessary delay, and perform hand hygiene afterward.

Worked case: a worker collapses beside a safely parked truck and gasps every few seconds. Treat those gasps as abnormal, call and start CPR/AED action. The same collapse inside a gas-alarmed tank requires emergency activation and capable rescue before first-aid access. Practice explaining the difference; the presence of a casualty does not remove the hazard to the rescuer.

## F02 — Adult CPR that actually moves blood

After emergency activation, place the adult supine on a firm surface where high-quality CPR can be performed safely without avoidable movement delays. Kneel beside the chest. Put the heel of one hand in the center of the chest on the lower half of the sternum and the other hand on top. Keep arms straight with shoulders above hands. Compress at 100–120 per minute, at least 5 cm(2 inches) deep while avoiding depth beyond 6 cm(2.4 inches) in an average adult. Release pressure fully between compressions without losing position. Do not lean on the chest. Minimize interruptions, aiming for pauses under ten seconds.

For a trained rescuer able and willing to give breaths, use 30 compressions then 2 breaths. Open the airway with head tilt–chin lift, use the trained barrier/mask seal, and give each breath over about one second, only enough for visible chest rise. Avoid forceful excessive ventilation. If the first breath does not raise the chest, reposition/open and try the second, then promptly resume compressions rather than repeatedly attempting breaths. Lay rescuers should prioritize opening the airway even when injury is suspected; advanced airway maneuvers are outside this course. If unable or unwilling to give breaths, perform compression-only CPR and follow dispatcher coaching; doing something effective is better than waiting.

When another trained rescuer is available, change compressors about every two minutes or sooner if tired, coordinating the change to minimize pause. Continue until the person shows signs of life/normal breathing, capable rescuers take over, the scene becomes unsafe or you physically cannot continue. A no-shock AED message alone is not a reason to stop.

Worked case:150 shallow compressions per minute are not better than 110 effective ones. On a feedback manikin, correct rate, depth, recoil and interruptions together. A learner who recites 100–120 but leans continuously has not demonstrated the skill. Practice two-minute cycles with recorded feedback, then a fresh independent run.

## F03 — AED use, clear checks and readiness

Send for the AED as soon as possible while CPR continues. Turn it on and follow its prompts. Expose the chest, prepare it as directed and apply pads according to their diagrams: commonly one on the upper right chest and the other on the left side below the armpit. Do not place pads over clothing, a medication patch or directly over an implanted device; follow device guidance and avoid needless delay. Remove a patch with protection and wipe the area if it interferes with placement. Dry a wet chest. Do not deliver a shock while the person is in water; move to a safe setting. Keep pads from touching each other.

While one rescuer prepares the AED, another can continue compressions until analysis is needed. Clearly announce and visually verify nobody touches the person during analysis and shock. Deliver a shock only as the device directs, then resume CPR promptly. If no shock is advised, resume CPR as prompted; the device is making a rhythm decision, not declaring recovery. Do not remove pads merely because no shock was given.

Use pediatric attenuation/pads if indicated and available for a young child or infant; if unavailable, an adult AED can still be used following its instructions. If pads would touch, use the manufacturer's front-and-back placement. Never cut pads or improvise reduced energy. Instructor demonstration uses the actual trainer's diagrams and prompts.

Worked case: an AED says no shock after an unresponsive gasping adult's rhythm is analyzed. The team immediately resumes CPR. A trainee who waits for the ambulance has confused treatment selection with recovery. Inspect AED readiness before emergencies: access, status indicator, batteries, pad dates and supplies. Training always uses a non-shocking trainer and manikin.

## F04 — Child and infant CPR distinctions

For this course, infant means under one year excluding newborn resuscitation; child means about one year to puberty. Use the adult sequence after puberty. Respiratory problems commonly precede pediatric arrest, so breaths with compressions are particularly important. If the infant/child is unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate help and start CPR; lay rescuers do not search for a pulse. With a phone, use speaker activation while beginning care. If alone without a phone and you must leave an unwitnessed pediatric collapse to call, give about two minutes of CPR before leaving to activate/get the AED. For a witnessed sudden collapse, promptly activate/get the AED. A second rescuer calls and retrieves the AED while the first provides care.

Use 100–120 compressions per minute and full recoil. Compress at least one-third the chest's front-to-back depth, approximately 4 cm(1.5 inches) for an infant and 5 cm(2 inches) for a child. For a child, one or two hands may be used to achieve effective depth. For an infant, the 2025 guidance uses the heel of one hand on the sternum or two thumbs with hands encircling the chest; do not teach the old two-finger compression technique as the current standard. Position on the lower half of the sternum while avoiding the tip and ribs. A single rescuer uses 30:2; two trained rescuers use 15:2 without an advanced airway.

Open the airway gently; do not overextend an infant's head. Give breaths over about one second with visible chest rise and avoid excessive volume. A small infant may require a mouth-to-mouth-and-nose seal or appropriate mask. Use a pediatric AED attenuator if available and do not delay necessary AED use when only an adult device is present. Keep pads apart using the approved placement.

Worked case: a small infant's chest is compressed using two fingers because the instructor's old poster shows it. Replace that practice with the current one-hand or two-thumb method and verify measured depth/recoil on an infant manikin. Do not assume adult hand force or adult breath volume is appropriate. Practice both single-rescuer and coordinated two-rescuer ratios with infant and child trainers.

## F05 — Choking: effective cough versus severe obstruction

A person who can cough forcefully, speak or breathe has some air movement. Encourage coughing and watch for worsening; do not automatically strike or thrust someone with an effective cough. Severe obstruction includes inability to speak/cry or breathe effectively, weak or absent cough and deterioration. Activate emergency help. For a responsive adult or child with severe obstruction, give repeated cycles of five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive. Bend/support the person forward for back blows between the shoulder blades. For abdominal thrusts, position the fist above the navel and below the breastbone, grasp it and give separate inward/upward thrusts. A qualified instructor demonstrates on a choking trainer, not by force on a classmate.

If the abdomen cannot be encircled or the adult is in late pregnancy, use five back blows followed by five chest thrusts instead. For an infant, support head/jaw without compressing the throat, hold face-down with head lower than chest and deliver five back blows; turn supported face-up, still head lower, and deliver five chest thrusts using the heel of one hand under current guidance. Never use abdominal thrusts on an infant or shake/hang the infant by the feet.

If the person becomes unresponsive, lower safely and start CPR beginning with compressions. Each time the airway is opened for breaths, look for an object and remove it only if visible and readily accessible; never perform a blind finger sweep. Continue CPR/AED and emergency help. Someone treated for severe choking should receive medical follow-up for possible injury or retained material.

Worked case: an adult initially coughs loudly, then becomes silent and cannot breathe. The response changes from encouraging cough to severe-obstruction care. If the adult collapses, it changes again to CPR. The learner must respond to the condition rather than finish a memorized set after circumstances change.

## F06 — Life-threatening bleeding and shock

Look for rapid pooling, continuous heavy flow, spurting or bleeding accompanied by collapse or worsening condition. Activate EMS and use gloves/barriers. Apply firm direct pressure with gauze or a clean dressing at the source. Maintain effective pressure rather than repeatedly lifting the dressing to inspect. For life-threatening limb bleeding not controlled by pressure, apply a commercial tourniquet according to training and its instructions. Place it above the wound toward the torso, typically 2–3 inches away and not over a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops, secure it and record the time. Do not periodically loosen or remove it; hand management to emergency clinicians. If bleeding continues, follow the trained device/protocol response, including a second appropriate tourniquet if needed, rather than accepting ineffective control.

For a deep external wound suitable for packing, expose the bleeding site while maintaining pressure. A trained provider presses gauze against the bleeding source and fills the wound firmly with successive portions of gauze, rather than laying a loose pad across the opening. Once filled, apply sustained firm pressure over the packing; follow the hemostatic product's timing instructions and continue pressure until bleeding is controlled and care is transferred. Do not repeatedly lift the packing to look. Reassess for continued bleeding and use the trained escalation response if control fails. Do not pack chest or abdominal cavities, the eye or an unassessed wound, and do not remove an embedded object. A tourniquet is not used around the neck or torso. Training uses dedicated wound/limb simulators; no forceful application on people. The instructor demonstrates packing against the simulated source, filling the cavity and maintaining pressure before independent assessment.

Shock can follow blood loss or other emergencies. Watch for weakness, pale/cool clammy skin, rapid breathing and altered responsiveness; do not wait for every sign. Control the cause when possible, activate help, protect from heat loss, monitor breathing and avoid food/drink in a seriously injured person. Keep a position that supports breathing and minimizes further injury; do not force a breathless person flat.

Worked case: heavy forearm bleeding continues despite correct pressure. A trained responder uses the tourniquet, confirms bleeding stops and records 14:12. A coworker proposes loosening it at 14:20 to improve comfort. Do not loosen; report time and response to EMS. Practice achieving actual simulated bleeding control, not merely attaching a device.

## F07 — Wounds, fractures, head and spinal concerns

After life threats are addressed, protect minor wounds, clean with appropriate clean water and cover with a clean dressing. Seek medical advice for deep, dirty, puncture, bite or infected wounds and tetanus concerns. Do not remove an embedded object; stabilize around it without pressing it deeper. Severe bleeding still requires appropriate control around the object and urgent help. For open chest wounds, activate EMS; do not improvise an airtight seal. Current first-aid options include leaving exposed, a clean nonocclusive dressing or a purpose-made vented seal used by trained personnel. Monitor breathing and follow emergency guidance.

Suspected fracture or major joint injury needs support in the position found and professional assessment; do not straighten a deformed limb or push exposed bone back. Check circulation/sensation before and after any trained support without delaying urgent care. Remove rings or tight items early if safe before swelling. Cold packs wrapped to protect skin can help some closed minor injuries, but do not let comfort measures delay assessment of serious trauma.

For possible head, neck or spinal injury, ask the person to remain still, support as trained and summon help. Do not routinely apply a rigid cervical collar or force a position. Move only for immediate danger or lifesaving airway/CPR needs. After a head impact, altered responsiveness, worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure or neurologic changes require urgent evaluation. Do not return someone to hazardous work or sport just because they initially feel better.

Worked case: a person falls and has leg deformity plus gasping/unresponsiveness. The breathing emergency takes priority over making a splint. Another person with normal breathing and a painful deformity is protected from unnecessary movement and monitored pending help. Practice selecting priorities, then safe support on a trainer.

## F08 — Thermal, chemical and electrical burns

Stop the burning process only when safe. Cool a thermal burn promptly with clean running water; current first-aid guidance supports a 5–20 minute cooling interval. Monitor for hypothermia, especially children or large burns, and keep unburned areas warm. Do not apply grease, break blisters or place ice directly on damaged tissue. Remove jewelry and constricting items if safe, but do not pull off clothing stuck to the burn. Cover appropriately with a clean nonadherent dressing after cooling. Deep burns, substantial partial-thickness burns or burns involving important areas need prompt medical assessment; smoke inhalation signs or breathing difficulty require EMS.

Electrical contact can injure the rescuer. Do not touch the casualty until the source is made safe by appropriate personnel. High-voltage scenes require specialist control. After access is safe, assess breathing, activate EMS and provide CPR/AED if needed. A small skin mark can hide serious internal injury; do not dismiss it.

For chemical exposure, protect yourself, remove contaminated material/clothing safely and follow chemical-specific emergency guidance. Brush off dry material carefully when appropriate before irrigation, avoiding further exposure. For ordinary eye splashes, immediately use abundant room-temperature running water for 15–20 minutes; remove contact lenses if readily possible, hold eyelids gently open and encourage blinking. Avoid a forceful stream and runoff into the other eye. For ordinary skin spills, remove contaminated clothing and rinse promptly with abundant room-temperature running water for at least 15 minutes. Substance-specific instructions may require a different or longer response; contact Poison Control or EMS and follow those instructions. Do not improvise neutralization or delay known appropriate irrigation while searching for paperwork. Severe symptoms require emergency activation while a helper continues decontamination.

Worked case: a splash reaches a worker's eye and another person proposes acid/base neutralization. Reject the improvised reaction, initiate the appropriate eyewash response and obtain emergency/substance guidance. Practice locating/activating a trainer eyewash and communicating product information without putting a chemical in anyone's eye.

## F09 — Chest pain, stroke, seizures and low blood sugar

New nontraumatic chest discomfort can be serious even if it feels like indigestion. Activate EMS rather than have the person drive. Keep at rest, monitor and be ready for CPR/AED. An alert adult may chew/swallow 162–325 mg aspirin while awaiting EMS unless allergic or advised not to by a clinician; if uncertain about contraindications, wait for professional guidance. Do not give aspirin to an unresponsive person or routinely to a suspected stroke patient.

Use FAST to recognize possible adult stroke: facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, time to call. Other sudden neurologic changes also matter. Record last-known-well time, not merely discovery time. Even resolving symptoms need urgent assessment. Do not give food/drink to someone with impaired swallowing or delay for an employer approval.

During a seizure, protect from surrounding hazards, cushion the head if practical and time it. Do not restrain or put anything in the mouth. Afterward assess breathing and protect the airway. Activate EMS for a first seizure, duration beyond five minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, water-related event, injury/breathing problem, pregnancy, very young infant or delayed return to usual mental state. Call whenever serious uncertainty or deterioration exists.

For suspected low blood sugar, an awake person able to swallow can take oral glucose or an appropriate simple sugar source. Do not give anything by mouth if not awake or unable to swallow. Activate EMS for inability to swallow, seizure or lack of improvement within ten minutes after oral glucose. Follow product/professional guidance; do not give insulin as first aid. Worked case: witness says speech was normal 09:10 but change found 09:25. Record 09:10 last-known-well and call rather than assume low sugar or wait for resolution.

## F10 — Breathing emergencies, allergy and opioid overdose

Severe breathlessness, inability to speak normally, blue/gray appearance or deteriorating consciousness requires EMS. Help a person with asthma use their prescribed reliever inhaler if assistance is needed and within training; use their device instructions and spacer when prescribed. Do not offer another person's medication or treat an unknown breathing emergency as definitely asthma. Let a conscious person use a position that eases breathing.

Anaphylaxis may involve swelling, throat tightness, breathing difficulty, widespread skin signs, vomiting or faintness after an exposure. It can progress rapidly and may occur without obvious rash. Activate EMS and assist with the person's epinephrine autoinjector when available. Use the device instructions on the outer thigh; trainer devices only in class. Antihistamines do not replace epinephrine for a life-threatening reaction. If there is no response and EMS arrival will exceed 5–10 minutes, a repeat dose may be considered following available device/professional guidance. Do not delay emergency activation to see whether the first dose works.

For the EpiPen model described in the March 2026 US instructions, keep fingers away from the orange needle end, twist off the safety cap, press the orange end firmly against the middle outer thigh until activation and hold for three seconds before removing. Stabilize a young child's leg. The labeled second dose uses a new device starting five minutes after the first if symptoms fail to improve or worsen; more than two sequential doses require direct medical supervision. Older models and other brands can have different cap actions: identify the actual device and use its own instructions, not a remembered color mnemonic. Practice only with its matching inert trainer. In this workplace course summon EMS promptly for suspected anaphylaxis and continue monitoring.

Suspected opioid overdose with unresponsiveness and abnormal breathing requires emergency activation and CPR with breaths when able, plus naloxone if available. Do not delay CPR/AED while searching for naloxone or wait passively for it to work. For a NARCAN 4 mg nasal device, position the person on their back, support the head tilted back, hold the nozzle between two fingers with thumb at the plunger, insert gently into one nostril until fingers meet the nose, and press the plunger fully. Do not prime or test the single-use device. Remove it; if needed give a new device in the other nostril after two to three minutes, continuing emergency care. Use matching inert trainers on a manikin only. If normal breathing returns, monitor closely, use an appropriate recovery position and maintain emergency help; overdose can recur. Avoid unnecessary handling of drugs or needles.

Worked case: an unresponsive person has slow gasps and a naloxone device nearby. A helper administers according to training while another calls and the first rescuer provides CPR/AED care. The team does not assume overdose means cardiac arrest cannot occur. Practice with a manikin and medication trainers, never live medication.

## F11 — Heat, cold and safe environmental care

Heat illness can become urgent when mental status changes, collapse or severe weakness appears. Move to a safer/cooler place if possible, activate EMS for suspected heatstroke and start active cooling promptly. Cold-water immersion can be effective where trained helpers can support the airway and monitor safely; otherwise use available active cooling such as wetting and fanning while following emergency guidance. Do not delay cooling for a temperature measurement or assume sweating rules out heatstroke. An impaired person must not be forced to drink.

For less severe heat symptoms in an alert person able to swallow, stop exertion, cool, rest and provide appropriate fluids while monitoring. Escalate for worsening or failure to improve. Do not send the person straight back into the same heat load because they report slight improvement.

In cold exposure, move gently to shelter, remove wet clothing when feasible and insulate from ground/wind with dry layers. Altered responsiveness or severe hypothermia needs EMS and careful handling. Do not use a hot shower/bath for a person with reduced responsiveness. Use warming devices according to instructions with a barrier and skin checks; avoid burns. For frostbite, protect the area, avoid rubbing/direct heat and obtain medical care. Do not thaw if refreezing is likely. Watch breathing and provide CPR if unresponsive and not breathing normally.

Worked case: a worker becomes confused while still sweating in a protective suit. Begin emergency response/cooling rather than offering more water and waiting. Another shivering worker is alert after rain: shelter, dry insulation and monitoring are appropriate while assessing severity. Practice distinguishing the mental-status emergency from uncomplicated discomfort.

## F12 — Poisoning, bites and incident prevention

Do not enter an unsafe atmosphere to reach a poisoned person. If safely accessible, stop exposure without contaminating yourself, call EMS for severe symptoms and contact the regional poison center for substance-specific guidance. In the US, Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222;911 is the emergency route for collapse, breathing difficulty or other severe danger. Preserve product/label information safely. Do not induce vomiting, give improvised neutralizers or give food/drink unless directed for the actual substance. Eye/skin exposures need appropriate immediate decontamination as taught in F08.

For a suspected venomous snakebite, move away from the snake, keep the person calm with unnecessary movement minimized, remove constricting jewelry and seek urgent medical care. Do not cut, suck, apply ice or a tourniquet to the bite, and do not try to catch the snake. A safely obtained description may help, but photographs must not delay care or create another bite. For animal/human bites, control bleeding, clean appropriately and seek advice about infection, tetanus and rabies as relevant.

Remove an attached tick promptly with fine-tipped tweezers near the skin, pulling steadily upward; do not burn it or coat it with chemicals. Clean the area and monitor/report illness. Insect stings can trigger anaphylaxis; remove an accessible stinger promptly without delaying emergency care for severe allergy. The urgent decision follows the person's condition, not the small size of the puncture.

Worked case: a worker swallows an unidentified liquid and a coworker proposes making them vomit. Contact poison guidance and assess emergency symptoms; do not induce vomiting. Record substance, amount if known, time and symptoms without guessing. Practice reading a label and giving a concise report rather than treating every exposure with the same home remedy.

## F13 — Drowning and rescue limits

A would-be rescuer can become another casualty in water. Activate emergency help and use a safe reach/throw aid from a stable location where possible. Do not enter hazardous water without the appropriate rescue capability, equipment and training. Once the person is out and accessible safely, assess responsiveness/breathing and begin trained CPR with breaths if unresponsive and not breathing normally. Breaths are especially important after drowning; do not delay ventilation/CPR to try to drain water from the lungs or perform abdominal thrusts for water removal.

Use the AED when available after safe removal and appropriate chest preparation, with CPR continuing around device analysis/shock pauses. Keep rescuers clear as directed. Do not use a shock while the person is in water. If vomiting occurs, clear the airway safely and resume care promptly. Trained rescuers may begin drowning CPR with rescue breaths followed by compressions; do not wait for ventilation equipment and thereby delay compressions. CPR with both breaths and compressions is the central skill here. Follow dispatcher coaching; this course does not qualify in-water ventilation, lifeguarding, swiftwater or ice rescue.

Worked case: a child is recovered from a pool by a capable rescuer and is unresponsive with abnormal breathing. Another person calls and gets the AED while the trained first aider starts CPR with breaths. Trying to hang the child upside-down wastes time and risks injury. Medical evaluation remains important after a drowning event even if the person improves.

Practice: use a floor manikin, dry AED trainer and a rope throw prop from a safe stable position. Explain rescue limits, respiratory priority and why the aquatic environment does not remove AED usefulness after safe access. No live-water rescue is required or credited by this course.

## F14 — Handover, exposure reporting and durable readiness

Give incoming clinicians concise facts: event, responsiveness/breathing, important times, care provided, AED prompts/shocks, bleeding-control placement/time, medication assistance and response. Include known allergies/history only as relevant and available. Do not substitute a guessed diagnosis for observations. Continue assigned care until relieved and confirm transfer rather than assuming someone else has taken over.

Afterward, manage blood/body-fluid exposure promptly through the employer's process, wash appropriately, report needlestick/splash and obtain medical advice. Replenish equipment, replace used AED supplies, restore readiness and document without unnecessary circulation of private health information. Offer support to responders; stress reactions are not a reason to hide an incident. Never make a false emergency call during a drill; use a simulated dispatcher or coordinated exercise arrangement.

Worked case: two certified first aiders are both at a remote site while the workshop is occupied and the ambulance gate is locked. Certificates alone have not created coverage. Assign coverage, accessible equipment, clear location details and a tested access route. Verify the correction in a drill, not just in a meeting record.

Practice: complete an event handover with unknown times marked unknown, then inspect a trainer AED readiness checklist. Completion of this material does not show that you can compress, ventilate, defibrillate or control bleeding effectively. Those require observed individual performance, with correction and fresh reassessment. VNDRLY's credential records its own assessed scope and does not falsely issue another organization's card.

# VUC016 formative checks

Attempt before viewing explanations. After errors reread the namedFchapter,explain the consequence and perform a changed example safely.

## F01-L1

Are occasional gasps normal breathing in an unresponsive adult?

**Explained answer:** No; activate emergency help and begin CPR/AED action.

**Remediation:** Do not delay for a lay pulse search.

## F01-L2

Does first-aid training authorize entry to a gas-alarmed tank?

**Explained answer:** No; activate capable rescue and maintain safety.

**Remediation:** A casualty does not remove the atmospheric hazard.

## F02-L1

What four compression qualities must work together?

**Explained answer:** Rate 100–120,appropriate depth,full recoil and minimal interruption.

**Remediation:** Correct rate alone cannot compensate for shallow compressions or leaning.

## F02-L2

How are adult breaths delivered in trained 30:2 CPR?

**Explained answer:** Two breaths about one second each, enough for visible rise without excess.

**Remediation:** Open the airway and avoid prolonged ventilation pauses.

## F03-L1

Does no shock advised prove recovery?

**Explained answer:** No; promptly resume CPR as directed.

**Remediation:** AED rhythm advice is not a declaration of normal circulation.

## F03-L2

When must everyone be clear?

**Explained answer:** During rhythm analysis and shock.

**Remediation:** Announce and visually verify clearance.

## F04-L1

What replaces the old two-finger infant compression method here?

**Explained answer:** Heel of one hand or two-thumb encircling technique under 2025 guidance.

**Remediation:** Demonstrate actual depth/recoil on an infant trainer.

## F04-L2

What ratios apply to pediatric CPR without advanced airway?

**Explained answer:** Single rescuer 30:2;two trained rescuers 15:2.

**Remediation:** Practice both rather than transfer adult team ratio automatically.

## F05-L1

What if the person still coughs strongly?

**Explained answer:** Encourage cough and observe for deterioration.

**Remediation:** Severe obstruction requires different action.

## F05-L2

Which thrust must never be used for an infant?

**Explained answer:** Abdominal thrusts.

**Remediation:** Use the taught five-back/five-chest sequence and CPR if unresponsive.

## F06-L1

What is the goal of tightening a correctly indicated tourniquet?

**Explained answer:** Stopping life-threatening limb bleeding.

**Remediation:** Attachment alone is not effective control; follow actual device training.

## F06-L2

Should it be periodically loosened for comfort?

**Explained answer:** No; record time and hand over to clinicians.

**Remediation:** Intermittent loosening can restart dangerous bleeding.

## F07-L1

Should an embedded object be pulled out?

**Explained answer:** No; stabilize around it and seek appropriate help.

**Remediation:** Removal can worsen bleeding/injury.

## F07-L2

Does splinting take priority over abnormal breathing?

**Explained answer:** No; lifesaving airway/CPR priorities come first.

**Remediation:** Avoid delaying critical care for a secondary injury.

## F08-L1

What is the initial thermal-burn cooling approach?

**Explained answer:** Prompt clean running water,5–20 minutes as appropriate while avoiding hypothermia.

**Remediation:** Do not use grease or direct ice on damaged tissue.

## F08-L2

Why reject improvised acid/base neutralization on skin or eyes?

**Explained answer:** The reaction can worsen injury; use appropriate chemical-specific irrigation/guidance.

**Remediation:** Protect responder and initiate the correct exposure response.

## F09-L1

Which stroke time matters besides discovery?

**Explained answer:** Last known well.

**Remediation:** Record witness evidence rather than guess onset.

## F09-L2

Can oral glucose be given to an unresponsive person?

**Explained answer:** No; EMS and airway/breathing care are needed.

**Remediation:** Impaired swallowing creates aspiration risk.

## F10-L1

Can antihistamine replace epinephrine for severe anaphylaxis?

**Explained answer:** No; activate EMS and assist appropriate epinephrine use.

**Remediation:** Do not delay emergency care to see whether a weaker measure works.

## F10-L2

Should naloxone delay indicated CPR?

**Explained answer:** No; CPR with breaths/AED and emergency activation proceed, naloxone when available.

**Remediation:** Medication is not a substitute for resuscitation.

## F11-L1

Does sweating exclude heatstroke?

**Explained answer:** No; altered mental state in heat requires urgent cooling/EMS.

**Remediation:** Do not rely on dry skin as a required sign.

## F11-L2

Should an impaired hypothermic person be put in a hot bath?

**Explained answer:** No; gentle handling, shelter/insulation and EMS.

**Remediation:** Avoid unsafe rewarming and drowning/fall risks.

## F12-L1

Should vomiting be induced after an unknown ingestion?

**Explained answer:** No; poison guidance and EMS for severe symptoms.

**Remediation:** Do not apply home remedies indiscriminately.

## F12-L2

Should a suspected venomous snake be captured for identification?

**Explained answer:** No; avoid further bites and seek urgent care.

**Remediation:** Description only when safe, no delay.

## F13-L1

Why are breaths especially important after drowning?

**Explained answer:** Oxygen deprivation commonly drives the arrest.

**Remediation:** Provide trained CPR with breaths after safe access.

## F13-L2

Should water be drained by hanging the casualty upside down?

**Explained answer:** No; that delays lifesaving care and risks harm.

**Remediation:** Prioritize safe access, breathing/CPR and emergency help.

## F14-L1

What belongs in a clinical handover?

**Explained answer:** Observed condition,event/times,care,AED/bleeding/medication details and changes.

**Remediation:** Separate observations from diagnosis guesses.

## F14-L2

Can two current first-aid cards guarantee site coverage?

**Explained answer:** No; location,availability,equipment and access must work.

**Remediation:** Test the response arrangements.

# VUC016 clinical basis and coverage

Reviewed 2026-11-17. The following current primary guidance informs original instruction; no proprietary teaching illustrations, examination banks or provider branding are copied.

- [AHA2025 Adult Basic Life Support](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-basic-life-support): recognition, activation, compression/ventilation quality, AED and updated choking sequences, including late-pregnancy/inability-to-encircle modifications.
- [AHA/AAP2025 Pediatric Basic Life Support](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/pediatric-basic-life-support): infant/child CPR, hand technique, depth, ratios, AED and obstruction. The old two-finger infant compression technique is not taught as current. Newborn resuscitation is outside this lay-rescuer scope.
- [AHA/Red Cross 2024 First Aid](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/2024-first-aid-guidelines): first-aid medical, traumatic, environmental and toxicological conditions. Specific sections checked included bleeding/tourniquets, chest wounds, burns/cooling, hypoglycemia, anaphylaxis, seizure escalation, heat/cold and snakebite.
- [AHA2025 special circumstances, drowning](https://cpr.heart.org/en/resuscitation-science/cpr-and-ecc-guidelines/adult-and-pediatric-special-circumstances-of-resuscitation): CPR with breaths after safe access, possible breaths-first initiation for trained rescuers without equipment delay, AED and specialized rescue boundaries.
- [Poison Control immediate assistance](https://www.poison.org/need-immediate-assistance): poison-center contact, emergency escalation and avoidance of induced vomiting.
- [Red Cross child/baby CPR](https://shop.redcross.org/take-a-class/cpr/performing-cpr/child-baby-cpr) and [AHA child/infant call sequencing reference](https://cpr.heart.org/en/-/media/CPR-Files/Training-Programs/Community-Programs/Infant-CPR/KJ1424_How_to_Perform_ChildInf_CPR_231120.pdf?sc_lang=en): phone/no-phone activation context. The older AHA illustration's two-finger method is superseded by the 2025 pediatric guidance and is not reused.
- [University of Florida emergency management bleeding guidance](https://emergency.ufl.edu/take-action/medical-emergency-stop-the-bleed/): placement context for trained tourniquet use. Actual device instructions and qualified practice govern application.
