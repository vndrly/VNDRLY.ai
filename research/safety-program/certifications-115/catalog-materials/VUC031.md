# VNDRLY/U Pipeline Safety — Operator Qualification

VUC031 · Original training and evaluation materials · Revision 2026-11-17

## What this program teaches

Operator Qualification, or OQ, concerns an individual's ability to perform an assigned covered task and recognize and react to abnormal operating conditions. It is not one universal government-issued pipeline card. The pipeline operator must have and follow its written qualification program. A contractor's certificate, experience or training vendor can provide evidence, but cannot replace the operator's task determination and acceptance.

This course teaches workers, crew leads and qualification coordinators how to make and document that determination, prepare for task evaluation, apply safe task controls and maintain qualification. It includes fully worked training simulations for representative inspection, measurement, valve, damage-prevention and pigging-readiness decisions. Those simulations teach transferable skills without pretending that one generic valve sequence works on every pipeline. Actual covered-task qualification must identify the real task, equipment, approved procedure, applicable abnormal conditions and accepted performance evaluation. The package includes a controlled method and forms for that operational adoption; a blank operator-specific field is an unresolved authorization, not an earned qualification.

Learners need basic safety orientation, ability to read the assigned procedure with permitted assistance, and the numeracy needed for the measurement tasks. No live pipeline operation is part of this generic classroom delivery. Use inert equipment, synthetic readings and a controlled simulated facility. Real excavation, energized electrical work, pressure testing, line breaking, welding, fusion, confined-space entry and control-room operation require their own training, authorization and safeguards. This course does not issue those specialist qualifications.

Learning outcomes: classify covered tasks using the four-part test; verify qualification scope before assignment; use controlled procedures and communications; recognize and react to abnormal conditions; demonstrate the supplied task simulations; distinguish teaching from independent evaluation; manage contractor acceptance, direction/observation, changes, records and reevaluation; and produce a defensible qualification decision without inventing evidence.

## D01 — Covered tasks and the operator's responsibility

For gas pipelines, the OQ requirements are in 49 CFR Part 192 Subpart N; for hazardous-liquid pipelines, Part 195 Subpart G. The four-part test asks whether an activity is on a pipeline facility, is an operations or maintenance task, is required by the applicable part, and affects operation or integrity. All four criteria matter. Being inside a fence does not make every activity covered; a maintenance task does not become exempt merely because it is routine or missing from a purchased task list.

Describe the actual activity before classifying it. “Pipeline support” is too vague. “Inspect and operate the identified maintenance valve under procedure V-04” permits analysis of the facility, duty, applicable requirement and integrity effect. An activity integral to satisfying a performance-based requirement can be covered even if the regulation does not spell out its common trade name. The operator documents the determination and updates its list when its work changes.

**Worked example:** Delivering sealed office supplies to a laydown area is not automatically an OQ covered task. Launching a cleaning pig as part of regulated maintenance is a different activity. PHMSA's pigging interpretation explains why failure to list that task in an industry catalog does not remove the operator's obligation. Calling both workers “helpers” cannot decide their qualifications.

Apply the four questions to the task itself, record the relevant requirement and identify the operator decision-maker. A “no” requires a reason, not a preference to avoid training. An unresolved criterion goes back to the operator before independent performance. Separate safety training can still be required for a task that does not meet the OQ definition.

**Exercise:** Compare a delivery, a covered-valve inspection and maintenance pigging. Write the actual task statement and the evidence needed for each criterion. Correct the common error of treating a broad job title as the covered-task list.

## D02 — Translate a task into observable performance

A usable task definition identifies the asset family, starting condition, tools, sequence reference, allowable operating envelope, expected result, hazards, abnormal conditions and closeout evidence. The qualification scope must be narrow enough that an evaluator can tell whether the individual actually demonstrated it. “All pipeline work” cannot be established by a ten-question general safety quiz.

Read the controlling procedure and verify its revision against the work packet. Match equipment identifiers to the drawing and physical label. Resolve discrepancies before work. Identify hold points where another person's verification or control-room authorization is needed. Determine which measurements require calibrated equipment, which actions are prohibited and which changes require a new briefing. The worker should be able to explain why the steps matter, not only repeat their order.

Break a task into observable criteria: correctly identify the asset; select and check the tool; confirm prerequisites; perform the authorized action; monitor the specified indications; react to a supplied abnormal cue; and record the result. Keep a separate list of critical errors that cannot be averaged away. Critical criteria depend on the task, such as not opening a pressurized closure or not operating the wrong valve.

**Worked example:** Procedure V-04 names valve 24, but the drawing in the work packet points to valve 42. The learner stops the setup and requests controlled clarification. Guessing the asset from its proximity to the crew truck would make every later step unreliable. An evaluator who ignores the discrepancy is evaluating the wrong task.

**Exercise:** Turn the simulated valve-inspection task into five observable criteria. State the exact evidence for each. Replace “understands valves” with a behavior an assessor can see and record.

## D03 — Pipeline hazards and the normal operating picture

Pipeline systems can contain flammable, toxic, asphyxiating or environmentally harmful materials, stored pressure and electrical or mechanical energy. Gas can migrate into structures and ignition can occur away from the initial release. Liquids can reach drains or waterways. Carbon dioxide releases can create asphyxiation hazards and collect in low areas. Odor is not a reliable universal detector. Learn the actual product, exposure hazards and emergency actions from the operator's information and safety data.

Establish the normal picture before manipulating anything: identified equipment, expected pressure/temperature/flow or position, approved work boundaries, current operating mode, communication contacts and nearby activities. A normal-looking gauge on the wrong asset proves nothing. A valve position indicator may be inconsistent with actual position. Treat contradictory indications as a condition to resolve rather than choosing the convenient one.

A job briefing connects the operation to the surroundings: public traffic, excavation, weather, flooding, ignition sources, overhead lines, access/egress, terrain, simultaneous work and emergency routes. Controls should prevent the hazard where practicable and define how workers will recognize control failure. PPE supports those controls; it does not make an unverified pressure boundary safe.

**Worked example:** A crew expects a depressurized isolated training section, but a mock gauge shows pressure and the isolation record is unsigned. The safe conclusion is that prerequisites are not established. The worker does not “crack a fitting to see” or assume the gauge is wrong. They hold and contact the responsible operator.

**Exercise:** Prepare a normal-condition brief for the fictional facility: product, identified task, normal indications, boundaries and emergency contact. Then add a conflicting indication and explain which assumption is no longer valid.

## D04 — Recognize and react to abnormal operating conditions

An abnormal operating condition, or AOC, is an operator-identified condition suggesting malfunction or deviation that may exceed design limits or create a hazard to people, property or the environment. Qualification requires both recognition and appropriate reaction. Recognizing a dangerous cue and then improvising a recovery is not a passing performance.

Use a four-column decision aid: normal expectation, observable abnormal cue, possible consequence and authorized response. Task-specific examples may include unexpected pressure, a release, failure to obtain a valid instrument response, an unresponsive valve, a missing protective device, damaged coating or equipment identity conflict. Broader conditions such as fire, third-party contact or loss of communication may affect several tasks. The operator defines what the individual can reasonably encounter and how the individual responds within their role.

The first reaction may be to stop the task, maintain or move to a safe position, warn exposed people and notify control or emergency responders. It is not universally “close the nearest valve.” An uncoordinated valve action can worsen a transient or affect another crew. Use the actual emergency or abnormal-operation procedure. If a task instruction is unavailable or contradictory, do not replace it with a remembered sequence from another system.

**Worked example:** During a simulated valve inspection, the learner sees a mock release cue. They identify the unsafe condition, move to the scenario's safe point, prevent approach within their authority and notify the simulated controller with asset and location. They do not attempt an unauthorized valve cycle to “seat it better.”

**Exercise:** For a pressure disagreement, a failed detector check and a release cue, state recognition evidence, immediate safe action, authority boundary and information to report. Practise a changed cue so the response is not memorized from one question.

## D05 — Procedures, communication and work control

The operator's approved procedure must match the work. A contractor can propose a method, but it needs the operator's review and approval before use. An old procedure on a personal phone is not automatically current because the worker has used it for years. Verify revision, scope, asset and associated permits or work controls at the job briefing.

Use closed-loop communication for consequential instructions: identify yourself, location and asset; receive the instruction; read back the critical details; obtain confirmation; perform only the authorized action; and report the result. If communications fail at a required hold point, stop at the prescribed safe state and resolve the failure. Do not treat silence as approval. Establish who has operational control and who can authorize restart after an interruption.

Isolation and energy-control requirements must be established before intrusive work. A shut valve may not prove isolation, a zero gauge may be blocked or defective, and trapped sections can retain pressure. The actual procedure determines verification and control methods. This course's inert simulations do not authorize opening live piping or defeating an interlock. Technical steps cannot be made generic merely to shorten a training packet.

**Worked example:** A controller directs an inspection of V-24, while the worker hears V-42. A read-back catches the difference before any manipulation. If the worker cannot reach control after discovering the conflict, they hold rather than acting on the most plausible number.

**Exercise:** Role-play an instruction containing a transposed identifier and a later loss of radio contact. Record the correction, hold point and restart authorization. Explain why a completed timesheet is not evidence that the control was followed.

## D06 — Training, evaluation and the qualification decision

Training builds capability; evaluation determines whether the individual meets the operator's acceptance criteria. A training completion record, an exam score and an operator qualification decision are related but different records. A person may finish instruction and still need task evaluation or remediation. Do not label those stages interchangeable to make a dashboard green.

The regulations permit written or oral examinations, performance-history review, observation during job performance, training or simulation, and other assessment methods. The selected combination must establish task capability and AOC recognition/reaction. Performance history alone is not an acceptable sole method under the current rule, and observation of on-the-job performance alone is also prohibited as the sole method. A credible evaluation tests knowledge and performance appropriate to the task instead of simply watching a familiar worker finish a job.

Before evaluation, identify the task and equipment scope, current procedure, evaluator, criteria, allowed references, critical failures and evidence to retain. During an independent attempt, do not silently coach a missing critical action. If intervention is needed, protect safety, record the intervention and treat the attempt under the remediation process. Training can resume, but the coached result is not an independent pass.

**Worked example:** A learner answers valve questions correctly but attempts the wrong asset in simulation. The written result cannot erase the performance failure. The instructor reteaches identification and then uses a changed asset arrangement for reassessment. The qualification decision remains pending until the applicable evidence passes.

**Exercise:** Review an evaluation record containing only “experienced — pass.” Identify the missing task, method, criteria and evidence. Rewrite it as a defensible blank evaluation form without inventing a performance that was not observed.

## D07 — Contractors and directed/observed work

The receiving operator remains responsible for ensuring that everyone performing covered tasks meets its program, including contractor personnel and mutual-aid workers. A recognizable vendor logo or current provider card is not enough. Compare the individual's identity, exact covered tasks, dates, methods, equipment/procedure applicability and any restrictions with the receiving operator's requirements. Record acceptance or the unresolved gap.

A nonqualified individual may perform a covered task only under the operator's permitted direction-and-observation arrangement with a qualified individual. The arrangement must allow effective direction, observation and correction. A supervisor somewhere on the site or available by phone is not automatically sufficient. The number and placement of trainees, complexity, visibility, communication and ability to intervene matter. Follow the operator's established restrictions rather than inventing a universal trainee ratio.

The observer must be qualified for the task, understand the procedure and remain able to perform the required role. If they leave or lose observation at a critical point, the trainee's authority does not continue unchanged. Stop safely and restore the authorized arrangement. Participation as a trainee does not itself create independent qualification.

**Worked example:** Operator North accepted a worker on one valve type. Operator South uses a different actuator and procedure. South may reuse evidence where its program permits, but must resolve the differences before acceptance. Renaming North's task in a spreadsheet does not establish South's required capability.

**Exercise:** Build a receiving-operator review with one matching item, one expired evaluation, one different equipment variant and one missing AOC assessment. Decide which evidence can be accepted and which needs review without requiring unrelated retraining by default.

## D08 — Records, changes and reevaluation

Qualification records must identify the individual, covered tasks, current qualification dates and methods. The operator retains records supporting current qualification while the individual performs the task; prior qualification records and those for individuals no longer performing covered tasks are retained for five years under the OQ provisions. Do not delete failed attempts or superseded records to make a current record look cleaner. Restrict personal data and retain an auditable history.

The operator establishes reevaluation intervals by task. There is no single universal annual OQ expiration for every task. An unexpired date also does not remove the need to evaluate when the operator has reason to believe performance contributed to an incident/accident or that the individual is no longer qualified. Manage restrictions, remediation and evaluation according to the program; do not wait for the printed expiration when a competence concern is already known.

Changes affecting covered tasks must be communicated to the people performing them. Evaluate whether new equipment, revised procedures, changed limits or a different AOC requires instruction and evaluation before further work. A cosmetic document correction differs from a new critical operating step. Record the change-impact decision. Significant program modifications have regulator-notification requirements under the applicable rule; the responsible program owner handles those through the current official route.

**Worked example:** A new actuator changes the emergency local-control procedure. The coordinator identifies affected task holders and restricts the changed scope until required communication/training/evaluation is complete. They preserve unaffected qualifications and historical dates rather than resetting every course for every worker.

**Exercise:** Write a change-impact record for that actuator: affected task and people, nature of change, required instruction/evaluation, interim restrictions, decision-maker and completion evidence. Do not pre-fill a successful result.

## D09 — Patrol, condition observation and damage reporting

A patrol or condition-observation task starts with the assigned route, coverage method, equipment, expected conditions and reporting criteria. Regulatory inspection intervals and permitted methods depend on the system and location; the operator's task procedure controls the assignment. A generic course must not substitute one calendar interval for all gas and liquid pipelines.

Look for conditions the task requires: unauthorized excavation, erosion or washout, exposed or unsupported pipe, damaged markers, encroachment, signs of leakage and changes near crossings. Record the actual location and condition using approved references. Pipeline markers warn of presence but do not establish exact horizontal position or depth. Do not direct an excavator to dig from a marker's position alone.

If a suspected release or damage appears, move to the safe location and notify through the procedure; do not approach merely to get a better photograph. Avoid ignition sources and protect others within your authority. Do not attempt to uncover damage, touch a suspected energized line or apply a field repair outside your qualification. The integrity decision belongs to the authorized technical process.

**Worked example:** A patrol finds fresh excavation near a marker and an exposed pipe with a possible coating scrape. The observer stops their routine route at a safe point, reports the exact location and observed condition, and requests operator response. “No leak seen” is not a clearance to cover the pipe; mechanical/coating damage can matter before a leak exists.

**Exercise:** Use the supplied fictional map to create a patrol record with one completed segment, one inaccessible segment and one abnormal finding. An inaccessible segment stays unresolved rather than being recorded as inspected.

## D10 — Locating and excavation-interface decisions

Damage prevention requires accurate coordination before ground disturbance. Establish the applicable one-call notification, current ticket scope and validity, responses, markings and operator instructions. State rules differ; the course provides no universal waiting period or tolerance-zone width. Obtain the rule and procedure applicable to the site. A ticket is not permission to disregard contradictory field evidence.

Locating involves the correct line or conductor, suitable equipment, equipment checks, interpretation and required verification. Signals can couple onto nearby utilities; parallel facilities, poor grounding or broken tracer wire can mislead. Do not force an uncertain result into a confident line on the ground. Record limitations, recheck using approved methods and escalate unresolved identity or location. Existing drawings and markers support, but do not replace, the required locating/verification process.

At the excavation interface, confirm the protected zone, allowed excavation method, required exposure/verification, observer roles, support and communication. If an unknown facility appears, stop the affected work safely and notify. Damage, including a scrape without leakage, must be handled through the operator's procedure; do not hide it beneath backfill. This course does not qualify a learner to operate excavation equipment or enter an unprotected trench.

**Worked example:** Marks show a line on one side of a proposed excavation, but a locator produces a second strong response. The worker reports the ambiguity and holds the affected excavation for proper verification. Choosing the more convenient response because the schedule is tight does not resolve the risk.

**Exercise:** Review a mock ticket that covers an earlier work area, a sketch of new excavation and a conflicting locator result. Identify three separate gaps and the required escalation, without inventing an exact digging depth.

## D11 — Instrument readiness and defensible readings

A measurement task is only as reliable as its instrument, method and interpretation. Verify the instrument identity, correct sensor/range, service status, calibration requirements, battery, physical condition and manufacturer/operator checks. A calibration label and a functional response check serve different purposes. One does not necessarily replace the other. Perform the required checks in the safe training environment before relying on a reading.

Understand units and what the instrument measures. Percent lower explosive limit is not the same as percent gas by volume; oxygen readings do not establish absence of toxic gas. Sensors can be product-specific, affected by environment or unable to measure certain hazards. A zero display can reflect clean air, failed sensing, wrong mode or an unrecognized limitation. Use the appropriate method and account for sample transport/response time where applicable.

Record actual readings with units, location, time and instrument identity. Do not round an out-of-limit result into compliance. Compare against the supplied approved action criteria, not a remembered threshold from another product. If an instrument fails a required check, remove it from service for that task and obtain a verified suitable replacement; do not continue because its display turns on.

**Worked example:** The training detector powers on but fails its prescribed functional response check. The worker labels the instrument unavailable, informs the lead and obtains a suitable checked replacement. A later zero reading from the failed instrument is not accepted as evidence of a safe atmosphere.

**Exercise:** Convert a mock pressure reading of 0.60 MPa to 600 kPa, then compare it with a fictional 550 kPa investigation threshold. State that it exceeds the threshold by 50 kPa. The numbers are simulation data, not a permitted operating pressure for any real pipeline.

## D12 — Valve inspection and controlled actuation

Valve tasks can include external inspection, maintenance, position verification and operation. These are not automatically the same qualification. Start with the exact asset, valve/actuator type, approved procedure and required operational coordination. Inspect accessibility, identification, visible damage, leakage cues and the specified indicators without placing yourself in a release or moving-equipment hazard.

For an authorized actuation, confirm prerequisites and the command, use the designated control method, monitor required indications and stop at defined abnormal conditions. Do not force a resistant valve, add an unapproved cheater bar or assume the same number of turns applies to a different valve. A position indicator and the expected system response should be consistent under the procedure; a disagreement requires resolution, not a guessed “probably open” record.

Closing or opening a valve changes the system. It can affect pressure, flow, surge, connected equipment and other work. Therefore, do not operate an unidentified or unassigned valve merely because it seems helpful in an emergency. Follow the task and emergency authority actually provided. Inspection-only authorization is not permission to cycle the valve.

**Worked example:** During an inert training operation, the indicator moves to the commanded position but the simulated system feedback contradicts it. The worker reports the discrepancy, holds at the prescribed point and does not report successful completion. The evaluator records the actual decision and the AOC response.

**Exercise:** Perform the supplied inert-valve station using its identification, read-back, stop-condition and closeout instructions. Repeat with the asset label changed. Explain which action would exceed inspection-only scope.

## D13 — Pressure protection and test boundaries

Pressure-control and protective devices help keep equipment within its operating limits, but qualification to inspect one component does not authorize changing its setpoint or bypassing it. Identify the device, function, approved limits, test method and required coordination. Distinguish an instrument used to observe pressure from a device that limits pressure. A readable gauge does not itself provide overpressure protection.

A maintenance or test procedure must define isolation, test medium, equipment ratings, barriers, communications, acceptance criteria and restoration. Stored energy can be severe, especially with compressed gas. Never substitute an improvised pressure test for the approved method. This course uses static readings and an unpressurized simulator only. Do not connect real pressure sources to perform its classroom scenarios.

Inspect for the conditions assigned in the procedure: damage, blocked or impaired discharge/vent paths, missing protection, incorrect identification, unexplained seals or inconsistent readings. Do not adjust a protective device to make a failed test pass. Record the as-found result and escalate. Restoration needs the required verification so a temporary test configuration does not remain in operation.

**Worked example:** A mock test record lists a protective-device acceptance band, but the observed response falls outside it. The learner records failure and requests authorized action. Changing the acceptance band on the form is falsification; changing the setpoint without authority is an operational deviation.

**Exercise:** Compare three synthetic results with the supplied band and identify pass, fail and indeterminate due to missing units. Explain why the indeterminate record cannot be counted as a pass.

## D14 — Corrosion and coating observation

Corrosion control includes coatings, cathodic protection, monitoring and evaluation appropriate to the pipeline. A coating defect can expose metal to the environment; a cathodic-protection reading must be taken and interpreted using the applicable method and criterion. Do not treat a single generic voltage as the universal pass criterion for every system, reference electrode or measurement condition.

For a defined measurement task, identify the test point, reference system, meter setup, connection, required environmental conditions and method. Inspect equipment and leads, record units/polarity and preserve the actual reading. Reference electrode condition, contact, interference and voltage-drop considerations can affect meaning. If the method or equipment is wrong, the number may be precise but not useful. Qualified technical personnel resolve criteria and remedial decisions.

When assigned to observe exposed pipe or coating, document the location, extent and visible condition without unapproved scraping, grinding or repair. Report gouges, dents, corrosion or coating damage through the procedure. Backfilling before required examination can conceal evidence and prevent corrective action. Welding, coating application and specialized corrosion evaluation need their own task qualification where applicable.

**Worked example:** The learner obtains a mock negative millivolt reading but records it as positive volts. The evaluator asks them to verify connections, units and the original display. The correct response is to correct the record transparently and reassess the reading, not declare protection adequate from the magnitude alone.

**Exercise:** Build a measurement record that includes test point, reference, instrument, method, time, units/polarity and condition. Identify which missing element would prevent comparison with the assigned criterion.

## D15 — Pigging readiness and trapped-pressure hazards

Pig launching and receiving can be covered tasks for regulated operation and maintenance, including cleaning and integrity-related work. A pig, launcher, receiver and their connected piping form a system whose configuration and condition must be understood. The operator's exact procedure controls the sequence; this course does not supply a universal live-valve lineup.

The principal readiness questions include correct equipment and tool compatibility, confirmed communications, isolation/depressurization arrangements, pressure indication, relief capability, closure safety, exclusion zone and the expected tool position. Trapped pressure or a blocked indication can persist despite an apparently reassuring gauge. Never open or loosen a closure to test whether pressure remains. Required devices and verification steps exist to establish that condition safely before opening.

The gas and liquid rules address safe pressure relief and indication or prevention arrangements at launchers/receivers. Do not defeat an interlock, bypass a pressure-warning device or stand in the closure's potential line of fire. If expected tool arrival is not confirmed, pressure indications disagree or a protective device is missing, hold and notify the authorized lead. An overdue pig does not justify improvising a live intervention.

**Worked example:** The training receiver shows a zero gauge but its relief path is not verified and the closure warning device is marked defective. The learner declares opening prerequisites unmet. They explain that one zero indication cannot establish the safety of a system with unresolved safeguards.

**Exercise:** Review the inert receiver readiness sheet. Identify the pressure-relief, verification, closure and communication hold points. Respond to a simulated missing-arrival indication without inventing a retrieval procedure.

## D16 — Repair, joining and restoration interfaces

Repair work combines qualification boundaries: the person identifying damage, the person approving a repair, the joiner/welder, the inspector and the person authorizing return to service may have different responsibilities. One OQ course completion cannot establish all of them. Use the approved repair design/procedure, material compatibility and separately required qualifications. Never infer that a successful classroom quiz authorizes welding or plastic fusion.

Before work, verify identity and scope, correct material and component documentation, necessary isolation/work permits and the required inspection hold points. During the work, preserve traceability and record deviations. If a material cannot be identified or the procedure does not match its grade/type, stop that step and obtain a controlled decision. Do not conceal a substitution to keep the job moving.

Restoration is a controlled activity. Verify required inspection/test acceptance, removal or disposition of temporary equipment, correct final configuration, communications and authorized return to service. A repair crew leaving the site does not mean the operator has accepted restoration. As-built changes and outstanding restrictions must reach the people operating the system.

**Worked example:** A replacement component fits physically but its documentation does not match the approved specification. The worker holds installation and reports the mismatch. A supervisor's casual “looks the same” does not supply the missing engineering and procedure basis.

**Exercise:** Review a fictional closeout packet with an unsigned inspection hold point and an unresolved temporary bypass. Explain why the return-to-service decision remains pending and what evidence the responsible operator needs.

## D17 — Emergency actions and defensible reporting

A pipeline release may threaten workers, the public and the environment. Recognize credible cues such as unexpected vapor, liquid, sound, vegetation effects or instrument indications in context; no single cue is required in every event. Move to safety using the site procedure, avoid ignition sources and contact the operator/emergency services from a safe location. Do not approach a suspected release to confirm it with smell or touch.

The actual emergency plan assigns operational isolation, public protection, responder coordination and external reporting. Frontline workers must promptly communicate facts and follow their role. They should not delay internal notification while trying to determine whether a legal reporting threshold is met. The operator's designated personnel apply those requirements. Do not make an unauthorized valve intervention simply because action feels faster than communication.

A useful report includes who is calling, location, asset if reliably identified, observed condition, time, people exposed, safe actions taken and uncertainties. Preserve evidence without entering danger or interfering with responders. After the event, performance concerns may trigger qualification evaluation. That is a reason to preserve accurate records, not to rewrite the worker's task history.

**Worked example:** A patrol hears an unusual release-like sound near a crossing and sees disturbed vegetation. The worker moves away to a safe location and reports the observations, not a guessed leak size. They do not wait for a second worker to approach and “prove” it before notifying control.

**Exercise:** Deliver a 60-second simulated report, then a changed update. Distinguish directly observed facts from another person's account and identify who authorizes further operational actions.

## D18 — Qualification review and continuous improvement

Bring the evidence together without expanding its meaning. For each person/task, review identity, applicable operator, equipment/procedure scope, training, evaluation methods, knowledge/performance/AOC results, dates, restrictions and acceptance. An incomplete packet stays incomplete. The right corrective action is targeted: missing operator acceptance differs from failed physical performance, and neither automatically requires repeating every unrelated safety course.

Check the evaluation system as well as the learner. Repeated failures on the same step may indicate unclear instruction, a poor procedure or an unsuitable simulation. An evaluator who gives every person identical comments without observed evidence may not be applying criteria consistently. Review examples together, calibrate scoring and improve instruction without lowering critical standards to achieve a completion target.

Qualification is maintained through actual performance, change communication, reevaluation and appropriate records. Course authors can provide complete instruction and assessment tools, but cannot truthfully pre-populate a real worker's successful task demonstration. The certificate for this package states the course and simulations completed. Operator-specific qualification is recorded separately only after its actual requirements and acceptance are met.

**Worked example:** A worker has strong evidence for an older equipment variant and a successful new knowledge check, but has not demonstrated the changed critical operation. The review accepts the valid prior evidence within scope and identifies the remaining practical evaluation. It does not discard everything, and it does not declare the new task complete prematurely.

**Exercise:** Complete the supplied capstone qualification matrix for three fictional workers. Write one accepted scope, one restricted/pending scope and one remediation plan, each tied to actual evidence. Explain why neither a green badge nor an impressive course title can replace that reasoning.

# VUC031 delivery, practical evaluation and operator adoption

## Instructor plan

Plan 24 instructional hours excluding breaks: D01–D08 instruction/cases 6 hours; D09–D18 instruction/cases 6 hours; eighteen stations 8 hours; two final papers 2 hours; remediation review and capstone discussion 2 hours. Extend individual practice when needed. This is a VNDRLY course schedule, not a federal minimum or a promise that 24 hours qualifies someone on every pipeline task.

Instructor competence must cover the OQ rules, training/evaluation distinction and the supplied task simulations. Technical station assessors need the relevant task knowledge and ability to recognize unsafe performance. Before actual operator adoption, its responsible authority approves the procedure/equipment match and evaluator criteria. This materials package does not name or certify a real evaluator by itself. An instructor who cannot judge a technical station must obtain an appropriately competent assessor rather than mark it passed from attendance.

Use a classroom and inert practice area: no hydrocarbons, pressure sources, energized plant, excavation, live control connections or real emergency calls. Equipment includes fictional task/procedure records, mock asset labels, an isolated hand-operated training valve with no process connection, a static pressure display, a detector training unit or clearly labeled result cards, a low-energy educational meter simulator, marked floor map, inert receiver diagram, work-control forms and protected examiner papers. Remove pinch hazards and establish a stop signal. Any real equipment demonstration requires its own safety assessment and authorization outside these inert exercises.

For each chapter: establish the normal case, explain the consequence of a missed step, demonstrate the safe decision, have each learner perform it and then change one condition. Reading assistance and interpretation are allowed if they preserve the capability being assessed. Record accommodations. Never collect real confidential operator plans into a public class packet; use the fictional documents below. Learners can consult approved references, but must independently recognize the condition and choose the safe response.

## Fully specified fictional training system

**Operator:** Prairie Training Pipeline, a fictitious organization. **Controller:** Chen, simulated radio Control-1. **Qualification lead:** Rivera. **Training assets:** V-24 (inert hand-operated valve), V-42 (a label only), gauge G-1 (static display), detector D-1 (training result cards), test point CP-7 (low-energy display only), receiver R-3 (diagram only). No device connects to a real pipeline.

**Map:** Grid A1 is reception/safe assembly. A2 is the valve pad. B2 is the receiver diagram area. C2 is the patrol crossing. C3 is an uninspected route segment. The exercise boundary is taped on the floor. The emergency response is to stop, move to A1, notify simulated Control-1 and keep others from approaching the affected exercise area. Learners do not telephone real emergency services.

**Procedure PTP-01 revision 1 — inert valve task:** Verify V-24 against the work order and physical label. Inspect the inert device and confirm no process/energy connection. Contact Control-1, read back V-24 and the requested training movement, and receive confirmation. Move only the assigned inert control through its ordinary free range; no added force or tools. Stop immediately for resistance, label conflict, conflicting indication, simulated release cue or lost required communication. Record initial indication, command, final indication, any mismatch and closeout acknowledgment. Inspection-only work orders do not permit movement. These instructions apply only to this inert model.

**Procedure PTP-02 revision 1 — reading exercise:** Identify G-1 and its units. Confirm the supplied readiness card says valid for the exercise. Read the static value, convert only if requested, record both original and converted units. The fictional investigation trigger is greater than 550 kPa; a triggered reading means stop the exercise and notify Control-1, not adjust equipment. Unknown units or invalid readiness make the reading indeterminate. The trigger is not a real operating limit.

**Procedure PTP-03 revision 1 — detector readiness:** Check D-1 identity, supplied sensor/range card, physical-condition card and manufacturer-check result card. A failed functional-check card means unavailable for the assigned task; label it in the exercise log and request a verified replacement. Never use a later zero display to overrule the failed check. No actual gas is used.

**Procedure PTP-04 revision 1 — CP recording:** At CP-7, confirm the mock meter's DC millivolt mode and marked reference/polarity diagram. Record the supplied signed reading, units, time, reference identifier and method. The exercise tests accurate measurement documentation only; it contains no cathodic-protection acceptance criterion. An absent method or inconsistent sign means hold for clarification, not declare adequate protection.

**Procedure PTP-05 revision 1 — receiver review:** R-3 is a diagram, never a physical pressure closure. Before any simulated opening decision, require an identified approved task, controller confirmation, documented isolation/depressurization, verified relief path, required pressure indication/safeguard and closure arrangement, tool-position status and exclusion plan. If any are missing or contradictory, record NOT READY and contact Control-1. A zero gauge alone cannot override missing safeguards. No learner performs a live lineup or opening.

**Procedure PTP-06 revision 1 — record and acceptance:** Rivera reviews actual identity/task/method/date/scope evidence and records accepted, restricted or pending. Provider evidence is not automatically accepted. Unqualified trainees require the documented qualified director/observer to remain able to observe and correct; otherwise stop the affected exercise. No invented expiry interval is used. Change cases specify the applicable training record dates and restrictions.

## Scoring and critical failures

Each station has five observable criteria: 0 absent/incorrect/unsafe, 1 safe but incomplete or prompted, 2 independent and complete. Require 8/10 at every station and 2 on each starred criterion. Stop an unsafe attempt and document it. Reteach, then use the stated changed case. Do not erase the original result or coach an answer and record it as independent.

Critical failures across all stations: wrong-asset manipulation; unapproved live work; bypassing a failed prerequisite or safeguard; opening a potentially pressurized boundary; unsafe approach to a release; fabricated records or qualification; unqualified independent performance; abandoning required direction/observation; unauthorized restart or recovery. Any such response blocks completion until corrected and independently reassessed. A high exam total cannot compensate for a critical failure.

# VUC031 formative checks

Attempt before reading the response. Correct each error using the named chapter, explain why the unsafe alternative fails and solve a changed example before continuing.

## D01-L1

What four elements define a covered task?

**Answer:** Pipeline facility, operations/maintenance, required by the applicable part, and effect on operation or integrity.

**Reason/remediation:** Apply all elements to the actual activity, not only the worker’s job title.

## D01-L2

Does absence from a purchased task list prove an activity is not covered?

**Answer:** No. The operator must determine coverage using the regulatory test.

**Reason/remediation:** Revisit maintenance pigging and identify its actual regulatory purpose.

## D02-L1

What makes a performance criterion observable?

**Answer:** It states an action or result an evaluator can see and judge against the procedure.

**Reason/remediation:** Replace vague understanding with identification, verification, execution and response evidence.

## D02-L2

The drawing and work order name different valves. What next?

**Answer:** Hold the affected task and resolve the controlled-document/asset conflict.

**Reason/remediation:** A correct sequence on the wrong asset is not competent performance.

## D03-L1

Can smell alone establish whether a pipeline atmosphere is safe?

**Answer:** No. Product characteristics and suitable approved detection/control methods matter.

**Reason/remediation:** Not all hazards have reliable odor warnings.

## D03-L2

Does a closed valve by itself establish safe isolation for intrusive work?

**Answer:** No. The actual procedure must establish and verify required energy control.

**Reason/remediation:** A position alone does not prove absence of pressure or trapped energy.

## D04-L1

What two abilities are required regarding an AOC?

**Answer:** Recognize it and react appropriately within the operator’s procedure and authority.

**Reason/remediation:** Naming a hazard without a safe response is incomplete.

## D04-L2

Is closing the nearest valve a universal AOC response?

**Answer:** No. Follow the applicable task/emergency procedure and operational authority.

**Reason/remediation:** Uncoordinated manipulation can worsen the event.

## D05-L1

Why read back an asset identifier?

**Answer:** To detect misunderstanding before a consequential action.

**Reason/remediation:** Closed-loop communication verifies what will actually be done.

## D05-L2

What if required communication fails at a hold point?

**Answer:** Stop at the prescribed safe state and restore authorized communication before proceeding.

**Reason/remediation:** Silence is not permission.

## D06-L1

Does attendance establish qualification?

**Answer:** No. Evaluation must establish the assigned task capability and AOC recognition/reaction under the operator’s program.

**Reason/remediation:** Training, evaluation and acceptance are separate facts.

## D06-L2

May current OQ rely only on performance history or only on observing on-the-job performance?

**Answer:** No. The current rules prohibit each as the sole evaluation method.

**Reason/remediation:** Use the operator’s appropriate combination of accepted evaluation methods.

## D07-L1

Does another operator’s acceptance transfer automatically?

**Answer:** No. The receiving operator must verify scope and acceptance under its program.

**Reason/remediation:** Compare tasks, equipment, procedures, methods and evidence.

## D07-L2

Can a trainee continue independently when the required observer leaves?

**Answer:** No. Stop safely and restore the permitted direction/observation arrangement.

**Reason/remediation:** A phone number does not guarantee effective observation and correction.

## D08-L1

What is the retention rule for prior qualification records under OQ?

**Answer:** Five years; supporting current qualification records remain while the person performs the covered task.

**Reason/remediation:** Do not confuse current evidence retention with disposal at certificate expiry.

## D08-L2

Does an unexpired date override evidence that a person may no longer be qualified?

**Answer:** No. Apply required evaluation and appropriate restrictions under the program.

**Reason/remediation:** Competence concerns are not postponed to a calendar expiration.

## D09-L1

Does a marker identify exact pipe depth?

**Answer:** No. Use required locating and verification methods.

**Reason/remediation:** A marker warns of pipeline presence, not a complete excavation survey.

## D09-L2

How should an inaccessible patrol segment be recorded?

**Answer:** As not inspected/unresolved, with follow-up through the procedure.

**Reason/remediation:** Do not manufacture complete route coverage.

## D10-L1

Does a valid one-call ticket resolve every unexpected field condition?

**Answer:** No. Conflicts, unknown facilities and scope changes require appropriate verification and action.

**Reason/remediation:** A ticket does not erase contradictory evidence.

## D10-L2

Should a scrape without visible leakage be hidden by backfill?

**Answer:** No. Report damage and preserve required inspection opportunity.

**Reason/remediation:** Integrity concerns can exist before leakage.

## D11-L1

Does a calibration label replace every required functional check?

**Answer:** No. Follow the instrument’s specified checks; calibration and functional readiness are distinct.

**Reason/remediation:** A powered display can still be unreliable for the task.

## D11-L2

Convert 0.60 MPa to kPa and compare with the fictional 550 kPa trigger.

**Answer:** 600 kPa, which is 50 kPa above the trigger.

**Reason/remediation:** Preserve units and use the scenario criteria, not an invented real operating limit.

## D12-L1

May an inspection-only worker cycle a valve to see whether it works?

**Answer:** Only if the actual authorization/procedure includes that operation; inspection-only scope does not.

**Reason/remediation:** Operating changes the system and can require separate control.

## D12-L2

What if the inert valve resists ordinary movement?

**Answer:** Stop under PTP-01 and report; do not add force or tools.

**Reason/remediation:** Resistance is a defined stop condition, not an invitation to improvise.

## D13-L1

Is a gauge automatically an overpressure protective device?

**Answer:** No. Observing pressure and limiting pressure are different functions.

**Reason/remediation:** Identify the actual device function before deciding what a test proves.

## D13-L2

Should acceptance limits be edited after an unfavorable test result?

**Answer:** No. Record the as-found result and obtain authorized action.

**Reason/remediation:** Changing the criterion to fit the result defeats evaluation.

## D14-L1

Why record polarity and reference for a CP reading?

**Answer:** They are part of the measurement’s meaning and necessary interpretation.

**Reason/remediation:** Magnitude alone cannot establish adequacy.

## D14-L2

Does −875 mV alone prove adequate cathodic protection?

**Answer:** No. The method, reference, conditions and applicable criterion must be established.

**Reason/remediation:** This course does not assign one universal voltage criterion.

## D15-L1

Can a zero gauge alone authorize opening a receiver with unresolved safeguards?

**Answer:** No. All required relief, verification, isolation and closure conditions must be satisfied.

**Reason/remediation:** A faulty/blocked indication or trapped pressure can defeat that assumption.

## D15-L2

Can an overdue pig justify an improvised live retrieval?

**Answer:** No. Hold and follow the operator’s authorized abnormal-condition procedure.

**Reason/remediation:** Schedule pressure does not create technical authority.

## D16-L1

Does physical fit prove a replacement component is approved?

**Answer:** No. Verify specification, compatibility and the approved procedure/material evidence.

**Reason/remediation:** A fitting part can still be unsuitable.

## D16-L2

Does crew departure establish return-to-service acceptance?

**Answer:** No. Required inspections, configuration, records and operator authorization must be complete.

**Reason/remediation:** Restoration is a controlled decision.

## D17-L1

Should a worker approach a suspected leak to confirm it by smell?

**Answer:** No. Move to safety and report credible observations through the emergency procedure.

**Reason/remediation:** Confirmation must not create exposure.

## D17-L2

Should internal notification wait for a frontline worker to decide a legal reporting threshold?

**Answer:** No. Promptly notify the responsible operator; designated personnel determine external obligations.

**Reason/remediation:** Do not delay protective action while guessing a legal classification.

## D18-L1

Can valid evidence be reused after a procedure change?

**Answer:** Only within the accepted unchanged scope and after the responsible change-impact review.

**Reason/remediation:** Targeted reuse avoids both false completion and needless unrelated retraining.

## D18-L2

What does a complete training package prove about a real worker?

**Answer:** Only that materials exist; actual training/evaluation/acceptance evidence is still needed for that person.

**Reason/remediation:** Never pre-populate successful performance from authored content.

## Domain coverage and evidence

Each D01–D18 has two explained formative checks, three distinct controlled final questions, a changed three-part retest and one five-criterion observed station. The public package includes teaching, worked examples, learner exercises and blank evaluation forms. Protected final answers are kept outside it.

The technical chapters cover normal-condition definition, hazard cues, work-control boundaries and representative skills. They do not supply a universal live valve lineup, pressure-test method, cathodic-protection criterion, excavation tolerance distance or pigging sequence. The supplied PTP procedures fully define **inert training tasks** so those assessments can actually be performed. Real system adoption must replace the synthetic values and equipment scope through controlled operator approval, without presenting unperformed live tasks as completed.

Welding/fusion, hot tapping, specialized repair design, control-room operation, electrical work, pressure testing and other specialist tasks retain their applicable separate technical qualification requirements. A course certificate does not erase them. Operators must determine their complete covered-task list and add any task instruction/evaluation not supported by the actual adopted materials; no finite purchased catalog is a regulatory exemption. This is the legally necessary difference between a complete OQ training/framework package and a claim of universal all-task qualification.
