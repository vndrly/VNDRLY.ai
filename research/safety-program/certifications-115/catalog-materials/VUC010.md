# VUC010 — General Industry Supervisor

VNDRLY/U Safety Leadership. Material version 1.0, 17 November 2026. Audience: supervisors responsible for shop, warehouse or facility work. Prerequisite: ability to explain basic workplace hazards and participate in the delivery language; the embedded worker foundation is required even when a learner has prior experience. Provide language and accessibility support without answering assessed questions.

This is VNDRLY's own instructional and assessment program. It is not an OSHA Outreach 30-hour course, does not issue an OSHA card, and does not qualify an electrical worker, industrial hygienist, lift-truck operator, authorized servicing employee or emergency responder. The counterpart supplies a subject-scope benchmark, not permission to award its credential. No attendance, demonstration or credential has occurred merely because these materials exist.

## Required contents and sequence

Teach G01–G10 in worker-foundation.md, then M11–M16 in supervisor-decision-lessons.md and M17–M20 in supervisor-technical-lessons.md. These are embedded instruction, not reading-list substitutes. Worker chapter labels are retained for assessment traceability. This guide supersedes any worker-only completion statement in embedded material.

The learner must explain an exposure, select controls within their authority, preserve specialist boundaries, assign resources and responsibilities, and verify the resulting protection. A list of policies or training attendance is not evidence that a control works. Require the actual written or orally recorded decisions from every case, including unresolved facts and stop conditions.

## Preparation and safety of training

Instructor must be competent to teach general-industry safety management, explain the supplied calculations and boundaries, and evaluate reasoned controls rather than slogans. Read all cases and protected instruments beforehand. Arrange a suitable specialist for real employer-specific technical instruction; do not improvise authorizations through this course.

Supply printed/digital lessons, calculator, paper or accessible drawing tool, inert PPE and fictional diagrams supplied in the cases. No live electrical work, actual isolation manipulation, truck operation, hazardous substances, working at height, fire or rescue drill is required. Stop an activity if it would expose a participant. Local emergency/contact familiarization may be added, but keep synthetic case records separate from operational reports. Do not send notifications or create live incidents for training.

## Assessment and remediation

Forty formative responses: two per G01–G10 and M11–M20. Learners attempt first, then review explanations and correct reasoning. These are learning checks, not the controlled final.

The controlled final has 80 original constructed-response questions, four per domain, worth two points each. Nominal time 180 minutes, delivered in two supervised sittings if necessary with paper custody maintained; documented accommodations may extend time. Calculator and blank paper permitted; no completed exercises, answer key or coaching. Score two for required correct findings and reasoning, one for materially correct but incomplete without unsafe assertion, zero for incorrect, absent or unsafe. Pass 128/160 overall AND at least 6/8 in each of twenty domains. A critical unsafe authorization requires remediation and independent reassessment regardless of numerical score.

Independent practicals: six foundation stations A–F and eight supervisor stations S1–S8. Each has five criteria scored 0/1/2; pass 8/10 with every critical criterion scoring two. Use first cases for coaching and the supplied changed-condition variants for independent assessment. If the learner has already seen the variant's solution, prepare an equivalent-demand fresh variant before assessing. Preserve exact inputs and assessor findings. Reading a rubric is not a practical pass.

Failed components remain failed while the learner receives teaching and fresh practice. Protected reassessment cards support an equivalent-demand new instrument; retain four questions per failed domain and the 6/8 floor. Retain passing domain scores only when version, identity and controlled conditions are unchanged; recompute the overall 128/160. If not, reassess the full final. Two unsuccessful retakes require a documented learning plan before another full failed-component assessment. Do not erase earlier attempts or immediately retest revealed answers.

# VNDRLY/U Safety Foundations — General Industry Worker

VUC009, original learner coursebook, materials version1.0. This program teaches recognition, avoidance, reporting and basic control decisions in shops, warehouses and facilities. It does not issue an OSHA10-hour card or qualify electrical work, forklift operation, lockout service work, confined-space entry or emergency rescue. All people, facilities and incidents in exercises are fictional.

## G01 — Worker rights, employer responsibilities and useful reports

Safety begins with the work arrangement, not a worker's willingness to accept danger. Employers must provide the protections required for the work, communicate hazards and supply necessary training. Workers should understand the assigned task, follow the applicable controls, report injuries and hazards promptly and ask when information is missing. A signature on an orientation sheet does not make a defective machine safe or transfer the employer's duties to a new employee.

OSHA is the federal occupational safety and health agency; approved state plans cover some workplaces under their own programs. Standards apply according to the activity and jurisdiction. The name “general industry” does not mean every employer or every task follows identical rules. A warehouse expansion involving construction may introduce different requirements from its normal storage work. Ask the responsible safety person which procedures and authority apply rather than selecting whichever rule seems easier.

Workers can raise safety concerns, seek required information and training, and contact OSHA about workplace hazards. Retaliation for protected activity is prohibited. A hazard complaint and a retaliation complaint are distinct: filing one does not automatically file the other. For federal OSH Act retaliation, OSHA identifies a30-day filing period; other laws have different deadlines. Seek current official guidance promptly rather than waiting for an internal dispute to finish. [OSHA worker rights](https://www.osha.gov/workers), [complaint routes](https://www.osha.gov/workers/file-complaint).

Before reporting, move to a safe position. State the location, task, observed condition, exposed people, immediate precautions and what help is needed. Distinguish observation from diagnosis. “The conveyor guard is missing at the north packing station and workers are feeding cartons beside the exposed roller” is more useful than “management is careless.” Do not enter a hazardous area to obtain a better photograph. A quiet electronic report is insufficient when someone needs an immediate warning.

**Worked case:** Elena reports a cracked platform step. Her supervisor says the job must continue because nobody has fallen. Elena identifies the exact step, avoids the unsafe access, warns the approaching coworker and uses the site's escalation route. She records her report and the response accurately. The absence of an injury does not prove acceptable access. She does not repair the step with an unreviewed board or imply she is authorized to inspect structural capacity.

An injury report documents what happened and helps obtain care and correction. An employer's OSHA300 log, where required, is a separate recordkeeping instrument; not every internal first-aid report necessarily becomes a recordable case. Workers report honestly rather than classifying an injury themselves to protect a department's statistics. Do not suppress a report because someone promises it will be minor. Protect coworkers' medical privacy; never circulate a medical record to strengthen an argument.

**Practice:** A fictional log has three entries: “oil on aisle4,” “worker slipped at10:10, knee hurt,” and “worker says hours were cut after reporting.” Sort them. **Explanation:** first is a hazard observation, second an injury report requiring appropriate care/reporting, third a potential retaliation concern with its own prompt route. Preserve facts and chronology; do not automatically decide motive or legal outcome.

**Resource exercise:** Locate the official complaint page and explain how to describe a hazard without sending an actual complaint during training. Record the relevant state/federal contact for the fictional facility. A training exercise must not accidentally create a live allegation.

Workers can request an OSHA inspection for a serious hazard or suspected violation and can ask that their identity not be revealed to the employer. They also have rights to relevant training and certain exposure, medical and injury information under applicable requirements. Ask through the appropriate route; an employer safety poster or the official worker page helps identify resources. An inspection may include worker participation and private discussion with inspectors; report actual conditions rather than rehearsed answers.

A company stop-work policy and the legal right to refuse dangerous work are not identical. OSHA describes conditions involving good-faith belief in imminent serious danger, a reasonable basis, insufficient time for normal enforcement and asking for correction where possible. It recommends requesting correction or other work and communicating the refusal, rather than assuming that filing a complaint automatically permits leaving the site. Follow emergency evacuation when needed; consult the current official guidance for a particular concern. [Right-to-refuse guidance](https://www.osha.gov/workers/right-to-refuse).

**Incident-learning example:** A fictional worker is struck when a stacked item falls during picking. The report identifies unstable storage, exposure below the load and the absence of a controlled retrieval method. The lesson is to correct that exposure path, not conclude that every picker needs to be more alert. Use incident accounts to improve controls without exposing a real worker's private details or treating a fictional event as an OSHA investigation.

## G02 — Walking surfaces, ladders and fall exposure

A walking route connects people to work, exits and equipment. Assess its entire path: floor condition, drainage, level changes, openings, lighting, doorways and interaction with vehicles. A small obstruction can become serious when someone carries a load, steps backward or evacuates. Cleaning only the visible puddle does not control a leak that immediately recreates it. OSHA's general surface requirements address maintenance, inspection and correcting or guarding hazards before reuse. [1910.22](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22/).

Use the hierarchy of controls. Remove unnecessary floor hoses where possible; reroute or protect necessary lines with an appropriate arrangement; segregate traffic; maintain the surface. A sign warns but does not physically prevent an unexpected step into an opening. Interim isolation must actually keep people from the hazard and leave an acceptable alternate route. Do not redirect pedestrians into a forklift lane to make the original aisle look clear.

At an elevated edge or opening, identify the required fall protection before approaching. Guardrails, covers and personal systems have distinct functions and design requirements. A loose sheet of plywood is not a verified cover. A harness alone is not a complete fall-protection system: suitable anchorage, compatibility, clearance, training and rescue arrangements matter. Do not attach to an available pipe or rail just because it is nearby. This course recognizes missing protection; it does not train or authorize system design.

For covered general-industry unprotected sides/edges, four feet above a lower level is a key protection threshold; other situations, including dangerous equipment and holes, have specific provisions and can need protection below that height. Do not apply a construction threshold or assume anything under four feet is harmless. The responsible person determines the applicable arrangement, including falling-object protection and exclusion below overhead work. [1910.28](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.28).

Select a ladder for the task and environment; a platform or different access method may be safer for prolonged or forceful work. Inspect before use, identify damaged rails/rungs/feet and remove defective equipment through the site's process. Set it on a stable appropriate support, secure it as needed, keep the climbing area clear, face the ladder and maintain a secure grip. Carry tools so they do not compromise climbing. Do not stand on prohibited top steps, overreach, move an occupied ladder or improvise height with boxes. Follow the manufacturer's configuration and the site's ladder rules. [1910.23](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.23).

**Worked case:** A worker needs to reach a shelf beyond a stepladder's side. Leaning farther moves the body away from stable support. The correct response is to stop, descend and reposition or select appropriate access. Asking a coworker to hold the ladder does not make excessive reach acceptable. If the floor is oily, resolve the support and access condition first.

**Practice:** A floor opening is covered by an unmarked panel that shifts under a cart. Describe the safe decision. **Explanation:** keep people and traffic away, report and obtain a verified secured cover/guarding arrangement suitable for the loads and applicable requirements. Do not step on the panel to test it. Reopening requires verification of the corrected arrangement, not merely a photograph from above.

**Application:** Draw a route from workbench to exit with a pallet, spill and vehicle crossing. Mark an interim safe route and explain why it does not transfer exposure. Compare your route with the supplied practical case and revise it if it crosses the delivery lane.

## G03 — Exit routes, emergency actions and fire prevention

Learn the local alarms, emergency reporting method, evacuation routes, assembly point, accountability process and assigned emergency roles before a problem occurs. Identify an alternate route if the normal one becomes unsafe. A route must remain usable during the emergency, not merely be drawn on a posted plan. Storage, locked or obstructed doors, smoke and vehicle traffic can defeat it. An emergency plan identifies responsibilities for reporting, evacuation, accounting and designated special duties. [1910.38](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.38).

On an alarm, follow the applicable plan, move away from the hazard, warn others if safe and report to the designated accountability location. Do not detour for property or return because the alarm stops. If a coworker is missing, give the emergency controller their last known location and relevant observed facts. Do not become an untrained rescuer. A worker collapsing in a tank or suspect atmosphere may indicate a hazard that will also incapacitate the person entering after them.

Fire needs fuel, an oxidizing environment and an ignition source. Prevention reduces the chance they meet: control combustible accumulation, maintain equipment, store compatible materials appropriately and follow hot-work controls. A welding screen does not stop sparks from igniting material on the other side of a wall. A permit is not proof that the listed precautions remain effective after conditions change.

Know the employer's extinguisher policy. Some workplaces direct employees to evacuate; others assign and train particular employees for limited response. Having an extinguisher within reach does not establish permission or competence to fight a fire. OSHA distinguishes employee education and designated-user training requirements. [1910.157](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.157). Never let a fire, smoke or a closing escape path trap you while attempting control.

Fire classes distinguish fuels: ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, energized electrical equipment, combustible metals and cooking oils require appropriate extinguishing approaches. A letter on an extinguisher indicates its listed applications, not that any fire of that class is safe to approach. Water can be dangerous around energized equipment or incompatible materials. This course's practical is a decision exercise, not live-fire qualification.

**Worked case:** Smoke appears near a motor; a nearby carton pile burns. The new employee has no designated firefighting role. They raise the alarm through the plan, withdraw by a safe route and provide the location to responders. They do not unplug the smoking equipment by reaching into the affected area or test whether an extinguisher is adequate.

**Practice:** The primary exit corridor is blocked by smoke and the alternate route shown in the mock plan is clear. A supervisor has not arrived. **Explanation:** use the safe alternate route and accountability process; do not wait in danger for routine permission. Report the blocked route. If actual routes differ from the plan, report the discrepancy before normal work starts.

**Communication drill:** “Smoke at west motor room; I am outside at assembly pointB; one coworker's last known location was packing bay; I have not re-entered.” This separates known location from uncertainty. Do not report everyone accounted for based on a casual glance.

## G04 — Electrical hazards and unqualified-worker boundaries

Electrical energy can cause shock, burns, fire and arc-related injury. A small tool can expose a worker through damaged insulation, wet conditions or an unsuitable supply. A larger cabinet can contain hazards even if the connected machine is not moving. Treat missing covers, damaged cords, exposed parts, unusual heating or repeated protective-device trips as conditions requiring a safe response, not troubleshooting by trial and error.

Do not open electrical enclosures, probe conductors or test absence of voltage unless qualified and authorized for that work. Deenergization and verification are defined work practices, not equivalent to pressing a stop button. OSHA generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work, subject to specified exceptions; those exceptions are not a worker's permission to improvise energized repairs. A qualified person performs required electrical verification. [1910.333](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.333).

Inspect portable equipment visually within your trained role before use. Report damage, remove it from service by the applicable safe process and obtain approved replacement or repair. Do not tape a damaged lead and call it repaired, remove a grounding pin, force an incompatible plug or route a cord where vehicles crush it. Suitability includes the environment: moisture, chemicals, combustible atmospheres and physical abuse may require specific equipment.

Grounding and protective devices reduce particular risks; they do not make deliberate contact with exposed conductors safe. A repeatedly tripping device may indicate a fault. Resetting it again to finish a job can reintroduce the hazard. Obtain the responsible qualified evaluation. Maintain required access and working space around electrical equipment; a storage pallet can obstruct both safe operation and emergency response.

**Worked case:** A cleaner sees a split extension cord beside standing water. The worker does not pick it up to see whether copper is exposed. They keep out of the affected area, warn others and contact the responsible person for safe isolation and correction. Cleanup waits until the electrical exposure is controlled. Rubber-soled shoes do not establish electrical protection.

**Practice:** A conveyor is stopped, but the motor cabinet remains energized. A coworker asks you to remove its cover to read a label. **Explanation:** do not open it as an unqualified worker. Obtain the information from accessible records or the qualified/authorized person using the proper procedure. A stopped conveyor is not evidence of an electrically safe cabinet.

**Recognition drill:** Sort four mock photographs: intact labeled tool, crushed plug, open panel and blocked access. For each defect, give a safe location/report and permitted next step; do not specify live repair methods. Explain why a promise to “be quick” does not change the exposure.

## G05 — PPE selection, fit, use and limitations

PPE stands between a person and a remaining exposure; it does not eliminate the source. Begin with the task's hazard assessment and prescribed controls. A worker should know which body part needs protection, from what agent or energy, for how long and under which conditions. Different gloves, eyewear, footwear and clothing are not interchangeable because their color or thickness looks similar. OSHA's PPE framework includes hazard assessment, selection, fit and training. [1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

Check the supplied equipment against the task instructions and manufacturer information. Inspect its condition, adjustment and compatibility with other items. A face shield may supplement appropriate eye protection rather than replace it. Chemical gloves need suitable compatibility and service life; an intact glove of the wrong material may still fail. Loose clothing, jewelry or inappropriate gloves can introduce entanglement near rotating equipment. Resolve the task-specific choice with the responsible person before starting.

Demonstrate putting on, adjusting and removing the assigned PPE without creating exposure. Keep clean and contaminated sides separate. Follow cleaning, storage, inspection and replacement instructions. Do not share an item in a way that transfers contamination or compromises fit. Report discomfort that causes poor use; silently removing protection midtask defeats the planned control and hides the problem.

Respiratory protection has additional requirements. Required respirator use is part of an employer program with appropriate selection, medical evaluation and fit testing where applicable, plus training and use requirements. A seal check at use does not replace required fit testing. An air-purifying device does not supply oxygen and is not a universal solution for an unknown atmosphere. [1910.134](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134). This course does not perform a medical evaluation, fit test or respiratory qualification.

**Worked case:** A worker is issued a different glove brand for the same chemical task. The package does not identify compatibility with that chemical. “It is thicker” is not a supported selection. Hold the exposed task, obtain the correct compatibility information and approved PPE, then check fit and the other controls. Do not test resistance by putting a drop of chemical on a worn glove.

**Practice:** Safety glasses are badly scratched, and the worker lifts them to inspect a rotating operation. **Explanation:** stop safely and obtain suitable serviceable eye protection or another controlled inspection method. Poor visibility is a defect affecting safe use, not justification for unprotected exposure.

**Application:** Using inert sample PPE and the fictional task card, explain what each item protects, inspect it, demonstrate adjustment and identify one limitation. Do not enter an actual hazard to demonstrate confidence. The assessor records observed use, not just recognition of a product name.

## G06 — Chemical labels, SDS and safe work information

Hazard communication connects a chemical's identity to its hazards and the controls used where it is handled. The workplace program includes labels, safety data sheets and training; a worker must be able to find and understand the information for the actual product. [1910.1200](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1200). Do not identify a substance by color, smell, container shape or a coworker's guess.

For a shipped-container label, locate product identification, signal word, hazard statements, pictograms, precautions and supplier information. Workplace labeling arrangements may differ under the applicable program, but the worker still needs reliable identity and hazard information. An unlabeled secondary container that someone left overnight should not be treated as an immediate-use exception by another worker. Hold its use and resolve identity through the responsible process.

The SDS is organized information, not a universal job procedure. Start with product identity and hazards, then look for first aid, fire/spill response, handling/storage, exposure controls/PPE and incompatibilities as relevant. Match the product and revision to the actual container. If information conflicts or is unavailable, obtain clarification rather than selecting the least restrictive statement. Job-specific ventilation, quantity, temperature and exposure can change the control requirements.

Read pictograms together with the words. The flame concerns flammability and related fire hazards; flame-over-circle identifies oxidizing hazards; a cylinder concerns pressurized contents; corrosion warns of corrosive effects; exploding-bomb concerns explosive/reactive hazards. Skull-and-crossbones indicates severe acute toxicity; the exclamation mark can indicate irritation or other specified harmful effects; the person/starburst symbol covers specified serious health hazards. The environmental symbol concerns aquatic hazards and is not mandatory under OSHA's HCS. One symbol can represent several classifications; it is not a complete control instruction. “Danger” and “Warning” distinguish relative severity within the labeling system, not a safe-versus-unsafe product choice. [OSHA pictogram guide,2024](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA3491QuickCardPictogram.pdf).

Use the SDS section numbers to find information quickly:1identification,2hazards,3composition,4first aid,5firefighting,6accidental release,7handling/storage,8exposure controls/PPE,9properties,10stability/reactivity,11toxicology,12ecology,13disposal,14transport,15regulatory and16other information/revision. The format includes all16; OSHA does not enforce content in12–15 because other agencies cover those areas. This format guide is not a chemical-specific instruction. [OSHA SDS guide](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/OSHA3514.pdf).

**Fictional training extract — ClearSolv-T, not a real product:** flammable liquid; eye irritation hazard; keep from ignition; use only with specified ventilation; protect eyes with the task-approved equipment; avoid incompatible oxidizers; spill response reserved for designated personnel. In the exercise, transfer is proposed beside a spark-producing grinder with local exhaust unavailable. The correct decision is to hold transfer and correct the ignition/ventilation arrangement. Merely adding goggles does not address either condition.

Never mix chemicals to improve cleaning without an approved procedure. Incompatible mixtures can generate heat or hazardous gases. Keep containers closed and stored under the applicable segregation plan. For a release, alert and isolate from a safe location, follow the emergency plan and avoid untrained cleanup. A small quantity does not automatically mean a harmless exposure, particularly in a poorly ventilated area.

**Worked case:** A bottle labeled only “cleaner” is found at the sink. A worker wants to combine it with another product. Identity and compatibility are unresolved; do not use or mix it. Notify the designated person for safe identification/disposition. Do not open and sniff it to choose the right SDS.

**Practice:** The online SDS system is inaccessible during the task, and nobody can obtain the required information. **Explanation:** do not continue the affected chemical operation on memory or generic assumptions. Use the employer's available access arrangement or resolve the information gap. Record that lack of access is the problem; do not fabricate a document match.

**Application:** Use the mock extract to identify hazard, exposure, controls and emergency boundary. Then explain which missing information would require the complete SDS and workplace procedure. This teaches use of information rather than memorization of pictogram names alone.

## G07 — Hazardous energy and affected-worker lockout awareness

Unexpected startup and stored energy can injure during servicing even when a machine appears quiet. Energy includes electrical supply, gravity, springs, compressed air, hydraulic pressure, heat and moving parts that coast. Identify the task, possible motion/release and every relevant source. A control button, software command or emergency stop does not necessarily physically isolate energy.

OSHA's hazardous-energy standard distinguishes authorized servicing personnel from affected employees and other employees in the area. The employer's energy-control program supplies procedures and training. [1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147). This course teaches an affected worker to recognize the boundary, cooperate with notifications and avoid restart/interference; it does not grant authorized-person qualification.

At awareness level, understand the logic: prepare and notify; shut down appropriately; isolate the applicable sources; apply the prescribed locks/tags; control stored energy; verify the isolated condition through the authorized procedure; conduct the work; and restore only through the controlled return-to-service process. Each step addresses a different failure. A lock on one disconnect does not control a suspended load or trapped pressure elsewhere.

Do not remove another person's lock, defeat a tag, restart locked equipment or assume that a colleague's absence authorizes restoration. Exceptional removal, group work and shift changes require the employer's specific program; this lesson does not provide a shortcut. Ask the authorized person when the work will be ready and follow the notification process. If your task changes from normal operation to clearing a jam or removing a guard, stop and obtain the applicable servicing decision before reaching into danger.

**Worked case:** A carton is stuck under a conveyor roller. The operator presses stop and reaches for it. The stopped belt does not establish isolation of motor energy, stored tension or possible remote startup. Keep out of the danger area and obtain the authorized jam-clearing method. A short task can still expose a hand before anyone reacts.

**Practice:** A maintenance lock remains on a machine after shift change. A production worker knows how to use the start switch and is behind schedule. **Explanation:** do not bypass or remove the protection. Contact the responsible authorized person and follow the documented handover/restoration process. Production urgency is not evidence that workers, tools and energy hazards are clear.

**Model exercise:** On an inert drawing mark electrical, pneumatic and gravity sources. Explain which remain after the electrical disconnect is opened. The answer includes residual air pressure and a raised component unless they are separately controlled. Do not manipulate actual equipment during this awareness exercise.

## G08 — Machine guarding, hand tools and changing tasks

Machine hazards occur where material is worked, where parts rotate, where surfaces draw objects inward and where chips or sparks leave the process. Consider the operator and nearby people. A guard should prevent the relevant exposure without itself creating a hazard. Feeding tools can assist safe handling but are not automatically substitutes for required guarding. [1910.212](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.212).

Before normal use, confirm the machine is the correct one for the task, required guarding is present and the work arrangement follows the trained procedure. Do not reach around, over or through protection to save time. A switch that prevents operation when a door opens must not be bypassed to watch the process. Stop and report missing, damaged or ineffective protection; a warning sign alone does not replace a guard.

Hand and powered tools also require the correct type, compatible accessories, condition and method. Inspect handles, leads, guards and attachments within your trained role. An accessory's size and rating must suit the tool and task; visual fit alone is insufficient. Support the work appropriately and keep hands outside the hazard path. Control flying material and bystanders. Do not use a damaged tool because the replacement is inconvenient.

**Worked case:** A grinder's guard is removed to fit an oversized wheel. The proposed adjustment changes both guarding and compatibility. The correct decision is to stop, remove the defective setup from use through the site's process and obtain the suitable tool/accessory. A face shield does not make the altered setup acceptable.

Cleaning, adjustment and troubleshooting often expose hazards absent from normal feeding. A machine that can be safely operated with guards in place may require hazardous-energy control for maintenance. Identify the transition explicitly. “It is only cleaning” is not a hazard assessment. Do not use compressed air, hands or improvised sticks to clear material where the method is not approved and the hazard remains.

**Practice:** A coworker tapes down an interlock so a cover can remain open. **Explanation:** do not operate the altered machine. Warn affected people, report and obtain correction by the responsible authorized personnel. The fact that the coworker is experienced does not change the defeated function.

**Application:** In a staged image, mark a rotating shaft, nip point, point of operation and chip path. For each, identify who could be exposed and the control function required. Then explain why a photograph alone cannot verify the machine is safe to service. Physical state and energy-control verification are separate evidence.

## G09 — Materials handling, storage and powered-truck interfaces

Handling hazards arise from load weight, shape, stability, height, reach, route and interaction with people. Plan the movement before lifting. Clear the destination and route, assess whether mechanical assistance or a different method is needed and keep fingers from pinch zones. There is no universal weight that makes every manual lift safe: a lighter awkward load at long reach can still cause injury. Do not twist or hurry to compensate for a poor layout.

Storage must remain stable and appropriate to the rack, floor and material. A leaning stack, damaged pallet or struck rack requires controlled evaluation; do not pull an item from the bottom to make the pile appear straight. Keep aisles, exits and emergency access clear. Stored cylinders, chemicals and other special materials require their specific arrangements; ordinary shelving practice is not automatically enough.

Powered industrial trucks require their own training and evaluation for the equipment and workplace. This course does not qualify a forklift driver. OSHA's truck standard addresses operator training and operating controls. [1910.178](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.178). A general safety certificate is not a license to borrow a truck for one pallet.

As a pedestrian, use designated routes, maintain separation and follow the agreed crossing/communication procedure. Do not assume eye contact, a horn or a high-visibility vest proves the operator can safely accommodate you. Loads can obscure view; turning creates a changing envelope; dock areas add edge and trailer movement hazards. Keep out from beneath elevated loads and do not ride on forks or unauthorized passenger positions.

**Worked case:** A warehouse aisle is blocked, and the alternative passes behind a reversing forklift. The solution is not to hurry through the blind area. Stop the conflicting movement through the site's control arrangement and obtain a safe route. If an interim route is established, verify it remains protected as deliveries change.

**Practice:** A pallet is leaning from a damaged rack. You are not a truck operator. **Explanation:** keep people out of the collapse zone and report for authorized stabilization/correction. Do not push it upright, climb the rack or operate the truck yourself. A photo taken from a safe position can document the condition; entering beneath it cannot be justified by documentation.

**Planning exercise:** A fictional package weighs18kg, has no reliable handles and must be moved from floor to a shoulder-height shelf through a narrow aisle. Explain why18kg alone is insufficient to approve a solo lift. Consider reach, height, grip, route, frequency and assistance. A better layout or mechanical aid may remove the awkward lift altogether.

## G10 — Industrial hygiene, ergonomics and exposure prevention

Some harms develop without an immediate injury. Dust, fumes, vapors, noise, temperature and repetitive load can accumulate risk before a person feels unwell. Recognize the source, how exposure reaches the worker and which control interrupts it. Remove or substitute the hazard where feasible, use effective engineering controls, organize work appropriately and use properly selected PPE for remaining exposure. A mask or earplug is not evidence that the source has been controlled.

An industrial-hygiene assessment identifies agents, tasks, exposure patterns and suitable measurement methods. A worker should report failed ventilation, changed products, unusual dust or symptoms, but should not invent a safe exposure level from appearance or smell. Clear air can still contain a harmful gas; visible dust does not identify its composition. Sampling and interpretation require the appropriate competent process.

Noise can damage hearing. Difficulty communicating may signal a need for assessment; a phone reading alone does not establish the employer's exposure determination. Use the prescribed protection correctly and report poor fit or damaged equipment. Source control and exposure duration remain relevant. [OSHA noise overview](https://www.osha.gov/noise).

Heat risk depends on workload, conditions, clothing and acclimatization as well as air temperature. Follow the heat-prevention plan, access water/rest/cooling and look out for coworkers. Confusion, collapse or severe heat-illness signs require emergency response; do not leave the person alone or tell them to work through it. Use the site's trained response and emergency services. [OSHA heat guidance](https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure). A universal “safe temperature” is not supplied by this orientation.

Ergonomics improves how the task fits the person. Repetition, force, awkward posture and long static work can interact. Adjust height/reach, use suitable tools or aids, change the method and report early discomfort. Rotating jobs helps only if it actually changes the exposure; alternating two equally demanding tasks may not. Do not blame a worker for symptoms while leaving the same poorly arranged task unchanged.

**Worked case:** Dust extraction stops during bag handling. The worker sees no large cloud yet. Continuing until a cloud appears is not a valid verification method. Hold the affected operation, report the failed control and obtain safe correction and any needed exposure assessment. A different disposable mask chosen without the respiratory program does not replace the failed system.

**Practice:** A repetitive bench task causes shoulder discomfort. The reach is long and the work surface cannot currently be adjusted. **Explanation:** report the symptoms and task conditions; seek changes to layout, tools, assistance or work organization rather than simply stretching while continuing unchanged. Appropriate clinical evaluation may be needed, but the worker need not diagnose an injury to report a concern.

**Integrated decision:** A worker encounters a blocked exit, failed exhaust and pressure to finish. Identify the exposure and immediate safe boundary, communicate facts and require verified correction. Avoid exchanging one hazard for another. Record the actual changed condition and who owns follow-up; a checkmark is not proof that the work is ready.

# VUC010 — supervisor decision instruction

These six original VNDRLY lessons are embedded from the previously authored supervisor material, reviewed for this package. They are not an external curriculum. The VUC010 guide governs completion.

## M11 — Turn a safety objective into owned work

A supervisor needs a mechanism that converts a concern into an effective correction. A statement such as “improve yard safety” lacks an accountable action, a due date and a way to judge success. Define the exposure, the desired change, the responsible person, resources, interim protection and verification. The person assigned to act must have the authority and information needed to perform the correction.

In a fictional warehouse, three reports describe pedestrians crossing a loading lane. Management assigns another toolbox talk. The talk may clarify expectations, but it does not answer why workers cross the lane. A review finds that the only parts cabinet is on the opposite side. The supervisor convenes workers and logistics staff to consider relocating the cabinet or establishing a protected route. The chosen control is checked against deliveries, emergency access and routine restocking.

Write a specific action: “Relocate the frequently used supplies to the pedestrian side after checking storage compatibility and access; verify with the receiving and maintenance crews during two representative shifts.” The two-shift observation is an exercise choice, not a universal regulatory requirement. Explain why that observation tests the conditions that created the exposure. Assign interim arrangements until the permanent solution is verified.

A manager should not close the action merely because money was spent or a sign installed. Closure needs evidence that the proposed control exists and works in the intended conditions. Record limitations and unresolved work. If conditions change, reopen the decision rather than protecting the original completion metric.

Exercise and rubric: Given a fictional backlog of six hazard reports, choose an urgent exposure for immediate control and write an actionable correction. Passing requires a responsible owner, interim arrangement, realistic resource decision and a verification method tied to the hazard. An answer that delegates everything to “safety” without an accountable owner is incomplete.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-prevention); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/worker-participation)

## M12 — Risk ranking, uncertainty and critical controls

A risk ranking helps a team organize attention; it does not turn uncertain observations into measured probabilities. Define the event, the consequence being considered and the conditions that make it possible. Different team members may be ranking different events without realizing it. A small spill, ignition of released vapor and exposure during cleanup are related but distinct scenarios.

For a fictional transfer task, the team uses a five-level likelihood scale and a five-level consequence scale. A score of 3 times 4 equals 12 under that local model. Another scenario scores 2 times 5 equals 10. That arithmetic does not prove the second is acceptable or that its potentially catastrophic outcome can be ignored. Ordinal categories are decision aids, not precise physical measurements. Apply the organization's escalation rules and consider uncertainty and critical-control failure.

A critical control should have an observable performance expectation. “Isolation is in place” is weaker than a task-specific isolation arrangement that identifies energy sources, verification method, responsible roles and failure response. The supervisor does not need to personally perform every specialist task, but must know what evidence supports the work decision and when expertise is required.

Exercise: A team rates an activity as low risk because it has been done many times without injury. Ask what exposure and control evidence they used. Repetition without an incident can coexist with an unrecognized high-consequence failure path. Review current conditions, near misses, equipment changes and the effectiveness of barriers.

Assessment: The learner identifies the event being ranked, explains assumptions, distinguishes score from certainty and names a control whose failure would stop or change the job. Passing requires identifying unresolved information rather than reducing a likelihood category simply to fit a preferred schedule. This lesson teaches decision reasoning; it is not a substitute for a required engineering or process-hazard analysis.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-prevention)

## M13 — Exposure information and industrial-hygiene decisions

Some hazards are not reliably detected by sight, smell or immediate symptoms. Exposure assessment connects a substance or physical agent to a task, route, duration and affected group. A supervisor should recognize when information is insufficient and arrange a competent assessment. Do not infer that a worker is safe because they have not complained or because a general-area measurement was low.

Consider a fictional shop where solvent wiping occurs for short periods throughout a shift. A measurement taken in an empty area during lunch does not characterize the worker's breathing-zone exposure during wiping. The assessment plan needs to represent the task and relevant conditions. Record materials, quantities, ventilation, work pattern and control changes so results can be interpreted. Health information must be handled through appropriate confidential channels.

A simple arithmetic exercise illustrates time weighting, not a real exposure limit. Suppose a fictional measurement is 20 units for two hours and 5 units for six hours. The eight-hour weighted average is (20×2 + 5×6)/8 = 8.75 units. That calculation alone does not address a short-term peak, a ceiling criterion, skin exposure, measurement uncertainty or a different applicable averaging period. Do not compare arbitrary units with a real limit.

Supervisors should connect findings to controls and follow-up. If a process substitution reduces one exposure but introduces a flammable material, the change needs broader review. PPE selection should follow the evaluated hazard and applicable program, not availability in the storeroom. Respiratory protection involves additional medical, fit, use and maintenance requirements when applicable.

Assessment: Ask the learner to calculate the fictional average, identify at least two limitations and state what task information an evaluator needs. Passing requires correct units and arithmetic, acknowledgement of peak and route limitations, and a plan to verify controls under representative conditions. This exercise does not authorize exposure-limit selection or replace an industrial hygienist's assessment.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/chemical-hazards); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.134)

## M14 — Investigate events without stopping at blame

An investigation should explain how the event became possible and what can prevent recurrence. “The worker was careless” does not describe the conditions that shaped the action or identify a testable correction. Preserve facts, arrange appropriate care and scene protection, and collect accounts without coaching witnesses toward a preferred explanation. Separate established facts, plausible hypotheses and unanswered questions.

In a fictional workshop, a worker reaches around a guard to clear a jam. The immediate action matters, but the review also asks why jams occurred, whether the normal clearing method was workable, whether isolation instructions matched the machine and whether production pressure discouraged stopping. Finding these conditions does not erase individual responsibilities; it improves the chance of fixing the system that made recurrence likely.

Construct a chronology using independent sources where available. Compare what was expected with what happened. A log entry created after the event should not be treated as contemporaneous observation without saying so. Protect personal and medical information. Do not destroy or modify equipment that may be needed for a formal investigation; follow the applicable preservation instructions.

Select corrections that address the identified mechanisms. If frequent jams are caused by a worn guide, a repeated warning alone leaves the initiating condition intact. If the isolation instruction is ambiguous, merely recording that the worker attended training does not show they could apply it correctly. Verify the repair, instruction and observed task performance as appropriate.

Exercise and rubric: Given a short fictional chronology, the learner identifies two facts, one hypothesis and two additional evidence needs. They propose a correction linked to an identified failure mechanism and a way to verify it. Passing requires avoiding unsupported blame and avoiding automatic conclusions that all workers need the same retraining. The investigation should produce targeted learning and effective changes.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/education-training)

## M15 — Contractors, changes and competing work

A job can be well planned in isolation and become unsafe when another team starts nearby. Supervisors need to coordinate interfaces: shared access, energy sources, lifting areas, chemical releases, noise, emergency routes and authority to stop conflicting work. A contractor's general orientation does not establish that the current work package has been coordinated with other activities.

In a fictional facility, one crew prepares hot work while another plans a solvent transfer nearby. Both hold completed task checklists. The checklists do not prove the combined arrangement is safe. The responsible supervisors must exchange the relevant scope and timing information, evaluate the interaction and establish a compatible sequence or separation before work proceeds. Do not assume each contractor can see the other's plans.

A change review should identify what changed and what assumptions it invalidates. Substituting equipment, moving the work location, losing ventilation or replacing a key role can alter controls. Record the decision, communicate it to affected people and verify understanding. A hurried signature after work begins does not establish that the change was assessed before exposure.

Exercise: A night crew receives an isolation handover that identifies an old equipment tag. The machine has been replaced with a different configuration. A satisfactory decision is to resolve the identity and isolation scope before relying on the record. It is not enough to copy the prior crew's initials or assume the equipment is functionally identical.

Practical assessment can use a tabletop schedule with two fictional contractors, a shared access route and a late equipment change. The learner identifies conflicting activities, assigns coordination responsibility and defines a stop/review trigger. Passing requires communicating a workable revised sequence and documenting which assumptions were checked. This is a coordination assessment, not permission to approve specialist technical procedures outside the learner's competence.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/hazard-identification); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/worker-participation)

## M16 — Measure improvement and demonstrate supervisory competence

A dashboard can make a weak program look strong if its measures reward easy closures rather than reduced exposure. Training completion, inspection counts and days without an injury are useful facts, but none alone proves control effectiveness. Pair activity measures with evidence that the important controls work. Ask whether high-risk actions are overdue, whether observations represent real work and whether workers can report problems without penalty.

In a fictional company, 98 percent of courses are complete while three critical isolation corrections remain overdue. The training percentage should not hide those corrections. Another team reports a sudden increase in near misses after a new reporting channel opens. The increase may reflect improved reporting rather than a sudden deterioration in physical conditions. Examine event details, reporting changes and exposure before drawing a conclusion.

Use a balanced review: identify the hazard-control objective, select a measure that tests it, examine the data's limits and decide a corrective action. Avoid interpreting a small sample as a precise trend. Show counts and relevant denominators. A comparison of two incidents versus one is incomplete without knowing the work exposure and reporting conditions.

Final exercise: Prepare a short supervisor review for a fictional maintenance campaign. Include the most important exposure, the control owner, one unresolved information need, the worker consultation method, the verification evidence and the next review trigger. Explain which decisions require specialist support. Describe what would make you stop or change the work even if the schedule and training dashboard are green.

Assessment rubric: a competent response connects measures to real controls, preserves uncertainty, assigns follow-up and communicates the decision clearly. Repeating a favorable score without addressing contradictory evidence is not a pass. The VNDRLY bundle assesses the knowledge taught in its courses. Official OSHA 30-hour Outreach recognition separately requires authorized delivery, required topics and contact time; a completion percentage does not supply those missing delivery records.

Sources: [Reference 1](https://www.osha.gov/safety-management/education-training); [Reference 2](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/OTP_General_Industry_Procedures_2024.pdf)

# VUC010 — supervisor technical decisions

These original chapters extend the worker foundation and six supervisor decision lessons. They teach oversight, planning and verification. They do not qualify the learner to perform electrical work, approve engineered safeguards, drive industrial trucks or lead specialist emergency response without the separate required competence and authority.

## M17 — Verify energy control and safeguarding through the whole task

A supervisor must understand the work boundary well enough to obtain competent decisions. “Maintenance is handling it” is not a complete coordination plan if production can still operate connected equipment. Identify the exact asset, task, affected people, connected sources and who controls each decision. Distinguish normal operation, setup, cleaning, fault finding and servicing. Different steps can expose different hazards even during one work order.

Start the planning discussion with the authorized servicer and affected workers. Ask them to explain the equipment identity and applicable controlled procedure; identify electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, gravity, thermal and stored mechanical sources. Ask what verifies the safe state and what would require reverification. The supervisor does not substitute personal confidence for the qualified person's electrical testing or the authorized person's energy-control steps. Record who supplies the evidence and which operations remain prohibited.

Electrical isolation and machine energy control overlap but are not identical. A motor circuit can be deenergized while a connected pneumatic cylinder remains capable of movement. Conversely, a mechanically restrained machine may still have an energized cabinet. Electrical verification requires the applicable qualified work practice; pressing stop or observing no motion is not equivalent. Refer to the embedded G04/G07 sources and instruction for the separate boundaries.

**Worked case:** Packing lineP7 and feederF2 share a control system. The work order says “repairP7” and the diagram shows a separate disconnect, but compressed air and a gravity-fed chute remain connected. Production proposes runningF2 during repair. The supervisor holds the conflicting plan until competent personnel establish the actual isolation and interface. The question is not merely whetherF2 can run; it is whether its operation can defeat the protected state or introduce material/energy intoP7. A separate asset name does not prove independence.

Group work and shift changes need continuity. Identify how each worker remains protected, how status is communicated and how the employer's authorized procedure transfers control. Do not instruct a replacement worker to copy initials or remove an absent worker's lock. Document the unresolved state honestly and obtain the designated process. During temporary testing or repositioning, the applicable procedure governs clearing people/tools, changing the energy state and restoring protection. A supervisor's deadline is not an alternate procedure.

Guarding review begins with the exposure path: point of operation, rotating or nip points, thrown material and bystanders. When production changes feedstock, tool, speed or access, ask whether the existing safeguard still performs its intended function. A signed inspection from last week is not proof for a modified setup. Controls must remain effective during foreseeable access/cleaning as well as normal feeding. Specialist design or validation belongs with qualified personnel.

**Practice:** An interlocked access door causes frequent stoppages. A supervisor is asked to authorize temporary defeat until the new switch arrives. **Explained answer:** reject the bypassed operating state. Determine and correct the cause through authorized maintenance and an approved safe alternative if one exists. Assess production/work arrangements so employees are not pressured to improvise. “Temporary” does not define a protective function.

**Verification exercise:** Write a restart evidence list forP7: correct asset/configuration, completed authorized work, required guards restored/verified, energy-control restoration by the responsible personnel, affected-worker notification and supported safe production conditions. If one item is unknown, record it as unknown with an owner; do not sign a blanket ready status.

## M18 — Plan emergencies and PPE for changing work

Emergency planning must remain usable during maintenance, night shifts and contractor work. Identify how people receive an alarm, how they leave or shelter under the applicable plan, how they are counted and which designated people perform special duties. Verify that temporary work has not blocked routes, changed assembly access or made the alarm ineffective for part of the workforce. Include visitors, temporary workers and people needing an accessible arrangement. Do not assume a posted map proves everyone can use it.

**Worked case:** A packaging facility temporarily closes corridorN during construction. Day staff use an alternate route, but the night cleaning contractor still has the old map. The supervisor coordinates a revised route, communicates it in an understandable format, verifies the physical path and rehearses a tabletop alarm/accountability. Updating the map without telling the contractor leaves the actual failure in place. The contractor must also communicate hazards its cleaning work introduces.

Fire prevention examines combustible loading, ignition sources, storage and work interactions. A hot-work request near a flammable-material transfer cannot be accepted by reviewing two permits independently. Identify common airspaces, spark travel, ventilation, nearby openings and changing conditions; obtain the competent task-specific decision. This course does not supply a universal hot-work distance or override the site program. A fire-watch assignment without training, equipment, authority and the required period of coverage is not a complete control.

Do not silently expand employee firefighting roles. If the employer's policy is evacuation, a supervisor should not direct an untrained person to try an extinguisher because the incident is embarrassing or production is important. If designated response is part of the actual program, establish required competence, equipment, safe conditions and escape. Exercise accountability and emergency communications without a live fire. Preserve failed drill observations and correct their causes rather than recording everyone as successful.

PPE oversight starts with the task hazard assessment, then appropriate selection, fit, condition, training and compatibility. A purchase order is not proof that the PPE works for the task. Substituting a chemical, glove, face protection or work method may invalidate the previous selection. Ensure affected workers understand when protection is needed, how to use it, its limitations and care/replacement. Verify actual use in a safe demonstration rather than only asking whether a training box is checked.

**Practice:** A new eyewear model fits under a face shield but interferes with required hearing protection. **Explained answer:** review the combined arrangement and actual hazard requirements; obtain compatible protection without silently dropping one function. A supervisor cannot resolve the conflict by telling workers to choose whichever is most comfortable while exposed.

**Drill review exercise:** An alarm exercise shows a contractor missing from the list and a worker waiting at the old assembly area. Treat both as system findings. Revise roster ownership and route communication, then verify with the affected groups. Do not punish reporting or rewrite the first drill as a clean pass. A successful retest does not erase the original evidence.

## M19 — Design handling and traffic arrangements around real work

A supervisor should examine the reason people and loads meet, not just order them to pay more attention. Map receiving, storage, picking, replenishment, waste removal and exits. Observe different shifts and peak deliveries. Identify crossing frequency, blind areas, unstable loads, dock interfaces and tasks that require awkward force or reach. Include workers' knowledge of shortcuts; a repeated shortcut may reveal that the designated method is impractical or unavailable.

Prefer removing unnecessary crossings and awkward handling. Relocating a frequently used supply point can reduce exposure more directly than repeating a warning. Check the change for storage compatibility, fire access, floor/rack capacity and replenishment. Physical separation is useful only if it remains effective when doors open, pallets accumulate or another contractor arrives. A gate left open all shift may defeat a carefully drawn pedestrian route.

Truck operators need appropriate equipment/workplace training and evaluation; supervisors verify evidence rather than treat a general certificate as a universal license. A new truck type, attachment, route or task can require additional evaluation under the employer's program. This orientation does not teach or assess driving. If operator competence or equipment condition is uncertain, hold the operation and obtain the responsible qualified decision. Do not borrow an unqualified employee to clear a problem caused by poor planning.

Dock and trailer work adds interfaces: vehicle movement, platform/leveler condition, load stability, edge exposure and communication between driver and dock team. The site's approved method defines restraint/chocking, capacity and verification as applicable. A supervisor should know who confirms readiness and what stops the transfer. Do not substitute a generic checklist for the actual vehicle/equipment arrangement.

**Worked layout:** Workers retrieve cartons40times per shift across a delivery lane. Moving the supply point reduces planned crossings to8. That is32fewer, or80percent reduction in this fictional frequency. It does not make the remaining crossings safe or account for new restocking trips. Observe the actual revised work and apply a controlled crossing arrangement for the residual exposure. Counts are evidence of frequency, not a calculated injury probability.

Storage changes need competent review. A struck rack, new load shape or higher stack can invalidate prior assumptions. Keep people out of the threatened zone; obtain authorized inspection/stabilization and required technical assessment. A supervisor should not instruct workers to climb, push or unload an unstable arrangement without a supported method. Document disposition and verification before reopening the area.

**Practice:** A more compact storage layout reduces walking distance but forces workers to lift18kgpackages from floor to shoulder height with no grips. **Explained answer:** evaluate the whole task—reach, height, grip, force, repetition, route and assistance. Consider changing shelf height, packaging, access or mechanical aid. Reduced walking alone does not establish lower overall risk.

**Worker consultation exercise:** Ask two fictional shift representatives why crossings occur, show the proposed plan, record one objection and revise it. A valid objection might identify that night restocking uses the new pedestrian area. The supervisor must resolve that interaction, not dismiss it because day-shift observations looked good.

## M20 — Approve chemical, charging and ergonomic changes with evidence

A change can reduce one hazard while introducing another. Replacing a cleaner may reduce odor but increase flammability or affect gloves, ventilation, storage and waste handling. Replacing a manual task with powered equipment may change traffic, charging and maintenance needs. Before approval, identify the old assumptions, new exposures, affected people and required competent reviews. Set a trial/verification plan and explicit stop conditions; do not discover incompatibility by exposing workers first.

For a chemical substitution, obtain actual product identity/SDS and use conditions, not only a supplier's marketing description. Review quantity, transfer method, temperature, incompatibilities, ventilation, ignition, exposure routes, PPE and emergency response. Confirm labels, information access and worker training before the change is used. A less hazardous classification in one category does not prove the whole task is safer. Industrial-hygiene support may be needed to assess representative exposure and control effectiveness.

**Worked case:** CleanerA uses an existing exhaust arrangement and glove selection. CleanerB is proposed because it has less odor. Its fictional safety information identifies a new flammability concern, and compatibility with the old gloves is unknown. Hold approval until the relevant exposure/ignition/PPE/storage review is complete. Do not ask a worker to test a small amount during production. Record what evidence supports the final choice and what remains prohibited.

Powered-truck charging or fueling is a distinct work activity. It can introduce electrical, chemical, fire, ventilation and manual-handling hazards. Identify the actual battery/fuel technology and manufacturer's requirements; do not apply instructions for one chemistry to another. Keep the charging arrangement within the employer's assessed program, with designated trained people, suitable equipment and emergency provisions. A spare outlet in a convenient aisle is not a charging-site approval.

**Charging exercise:** A proposed truck charger occupies the emergency route and uses an unverified electrical supply. The operator says the plug fits. The supervisor rejects the location/supply assumption and obtains competent electrical and operational review. Verify access, ventilation and technology-specific requirements before use. No worker is instructed to modify wiring or open a battery during this course.

Ergonomic change review compares actual exposures before and after. Moving a container closer may reduce reach but obstruct an exit or create a pinch point. A powered aid may reduce lifting while introducing pushing, crushing or traffic exposure. Include representative workers, trial the approved arrangement safely and record task frequency, force/reach observations and worker feedback. Symptoms require appropriate reporting/care; a supervisor should not diagnose or dismiss them.

**Practice:** Two jobs alternate every hour, but both require the same wrist force and shoulder reach. **Explained answer:** rotation has not necessarily reduced the relevant exposure. Change the task geometry, tools, assistance or work pattern so the exposure actually changes, then verify. Do not rely on the word “rotation” as evidence.

**Change-release exercise:** Complete five statements: what changed; hazards and assumptions affected; who reviewed each specialist issue; what evidence verifies readiness; and what condition triggers stop/review. For CleanerB, include the unresolved glove evidence and ignition conflict as hold points. For the charging area, include the blocked route and supply review. A decision is complete only when its actual evidence and restrictions are clear.

A protective device trips repeatedly. What is the worker-level response?

A glove is thicker than the old model. Is that enough to approve it for a chemical?

An unlabeled bottle left by another shift smells familiar. Can it be used?

**Correction/remediation:** Identify actual sources in the machine rather than assuming electricity is the only one.

## G07-L2

What should an affected employee do when a maintenance lock remains after shift change?

**Explained answer:** Do not remove/bypass/start; contact responsible authorized personnel and follow the documented handover/restoration process.

**Correction/remediation:** A shift boundary does not erase protection.

## G08-L1

A guard is removed to fit a larger accessory. What two issues need correction?

**Explained answer:** Missing guarding and tool/accessory compatibility; stop the altered setup and obtain suitable authorized correction.

**Correction/remediation:** PPE does not restore either function.

## G08-L2

Why can normal operation and jam clearing require different controls?

**Explained answer:** Clearing may expose moving/stored energy or require entering the danger zone; servicing decisions must address that changed task.

**Correction/remediation:** Do not assume a brief task remains normal operation.

## G09-L1

Why does a general safety course not authorize forklift driving?

**Explained answer:** Truck operation requires its own equipment/workplace training and evaluation; this course is pedestrian/recognition orientation.

**Correction/remediation:** Credential scope must match what was actually assessed.

## G09-L2

Why is package weight alone insufficient to decide whether a lift is safe?

**Explained answer:** Grip, reach, height, route, frequency, posture and assistance also affect exposure.

**Correction/remediation:** Evaluate the complete handling task.

## G10-L1

Ventilation fails but the air looks clear. What should guide the decision?

**Explained answer:** The prescribed control is unavailable; hold affected exposure and obtain correction/assessment rather than rely on appearance.

**Correction/remediation:** Invisible hazards remain possible.

## G10-L2

A hot worker becomes confused. What is the appropriate priority?

**Explained answer:** Activate emergency response and trained cooling/care, stay with the person and do not continue work or leave them alone.

**Correction/remediation:** Severe symptoms are an emergency, not a fitness test.

# VUC010 supervisor formative checks

Attempt first, then explain and correct any error. The twenty G-domain checks are embedded separately.

## M11-L1

What turns “improve traffic safety” into accountable work?

**Explained answer:** Define exposure, interim control, responsible owner, resources, due point and verification under real conditions.

**Correction:** A slogan or assignment to safety alone does not establish an effective correction.

## M11-L2

Why is purchasing a barrier not sufficient action closure?

**Explained answer:** Installation and effective protection under intended work must be verified, including new hazards and limitations.

**Correction:** Verify the exposure, not spending.

## M12-L1

**Explained answer:** No; consequence, critical controls, uncertainty and escalation rules still govern.

**Correction:** Ordinal arithmetic does not create permission.

## M12-L2

What is missing from “isolation checked” as a critical-control statement?

**Correction:** Make performance observable rather than a checkbox.

## M13-L1

Why does “carelessness” fail as the entire explanation of a jam-clearing injury?

**Correction:** Investigate facts and contributing conditions without erasing individual actions.

## M14-L2

How should a log written after an incident be identified?

**Explained answer:** As a later account, with its actual time/source, not a contemporaneous observation.

**Correction:** Preserve chronology and uncertainty.

**Explained answer:** No; evaluate the interaction, shared space/routes and coordinated timing/controls.

**Correction:** Independent plans may conflict.

## M15-L2

**Explained answer:** Resolve actual identity/configuration and applicable isolation evidence before proceeding.

**Correction:** Copied initials do not reconcile changed equipment.

## M16-L1

Training completion is98percent but critical isolation defects remain. What should the review prioritize?

**Explained answer:** The unresolved exposure and effective controls, with owners and verified disposition.

**Correction:** A favorable activity metric cannot hide critical-control failure.

## M16-L2

Why can more near-miss reports follow improvement?

**Explained answer:** A new reporting channel or greater trust may reveal previously unreported events.

**Correction:** Review conditions and reporting changes before inferring worsening safety.

## M17-L1

What must be checked when a connected feeder is proposed to run during conveyor service?

**Correction:** Different asset names do not establish independence.

## M17-L2

Why can a guard inspection before a feedstock change be insufficient afterward?

**Correction:** Review the changed task and competent safeguard evidence.

## M18-L1

**Correction:** Correct the actual affected group’s knowledge and route.

## M18-L2

Two required PPE items interfere. What should a supervisor verify?

**Explained answer:** Compatible protection for all relevant hazards, actual fit/use and supported task selection.

**Correction:** Do not silently drop one protective function.

## M19-L1

**Correction:** Frequency reduction is not injury-probability proof.

A smaller layout creates high awkward lifts. What should the review include?

**Explained answer:** Reach, height, grip, repetition, force, route and aids, not just walking distance.

**Correction:** Evaluate total task exposure.

**Correction:** Odor is not a complete hazard comparison.

Does rotating between two equally demanding wrist tasks necessarily reduce exposure?

**Explained answer:** No; the relevant force/posture/repetition may remain unchanged.

**Correction:** Verify actual exposure change and worker feedback.
