# VNDRLY/U Drilling Site Safety Orientation

VUC004 · Version 1.0 · 17 November 2026

## Program definition

This VNDRLY-owned program covers the safety-orientation subject represented by IADC RigPass. It is not IADC-accredited and does not issue a RigPass card or Basin United endorsement. The program combines ten fully written core chapters, eight offshore chapters, and six rig-specific chapters, all included in its coursebook. It includes onshore and offshore awareness so learners can understand their assigned environment; it does not qualify them to operate rig equipment or perform specialist work.

Audience: new or returning drilling and service personnel, contractors, and support workers. No prior course credential is required. Instruction must be understandable in the learner's language, with appropriate reading, hearing, visual, and participation accommodations. Actual deployment may require separate operator credentials, medical fitness, practical training, or regulatory authorization. Those requirements are not waived by this course.

Purpose: enable a worker to recognize exposure pathways, verify the protective arrangements required for their role, communicate reliably, stop when conditions change, and respond within assigned limits. Objectives are stated in all 24 chapters and mapped to assessment in the coverage document. The core addresses responsibilities, task risk, permits and energy, fatal hazards, chemicals, health, driving, tools/PPE, emergencies, and organizational readiness. Offshore instruction addresses preparation, access, transport, arrival, SEMS, marine environmental responsibility, and accountability. Rig chapters apply those foundations to the operating phases and interfaces of a drilling location.

## Delivery and facilitation

Teach the core first, then R01–R06, then the offshore chapters. Use the full instruction, worked cases, and learner practice responses. Plan multiple sessions when needed rather than compressing the material to a provider's advertised duration. Preserve where each learner stopped. A facilitator asks each learner to identify the hazard, affected person, control, stop condition, and responsible next contact. An outline or a video played without comprehension checks does not complete the instruction.

Use the five supplied application stations after the relevant teaching. These require spoken or written decisions, not just selecting an answer letter. All are conducted with fictional documents or in a safe classroom; no live drilling, lifting, pressure release, entry rescue, excavation entry, or water-survival activity occurs. The instructor must understand rig safety interfaces and the boundaries of the exercises. Bring a current site plan only when authorized to use it, and distinguish it from the fictional examples.

For every wrong learning-check answer, explain the failed assumption and revisit the corresponding chapter. Have the learner apply the corrected rule to a changed example. Record initial and corrected responses separately. Do not award a final-exam point for remediation of a lesson question. The package includes 97 formative checks: 61 core, 24 offshore, and 12 rig-specific.

## Assessment and credential rules

The protected final bank contains 117 questions. Form A selects 40: two per core chapter (20), one per offshore chapter (8), and two per rig chapter (12). A pass requires 32/40. One correct answer earns one point; no partial or negative credit. Use the controlled form without its key, confirm learner identity, and avoid coaching. Allow approximately 60 minutes, with reasonable accommodations. Language support must preserve the question rather than reveal the answer. Question order or a fresh balanced selection may change on retake, with its key checked before administration.

After a failed final, provide targeted instruction and practice in the missed domains. Preserve the original attempt. Three unsuccessful attempts trigger a documented instructor-led retraining plan before another attempt. Do not erase failures or count merely viewing questions as completing an exam. A compromised key requires a new reviewed form.

Earn the **VNDRLY/U Drilling Site Safety Orientation** credential by completing all instruction and required remediation, passing the final, and passing all five classroom application stations. Critical unsafe responses require teaching and a changed retry before station completion. The credential documents orientation knowledge and decisions, not hands-on specialist competence. Practical task qualifications, fit tests, licensed qualifications, site induction, and work authorizations remain separate.

No universal legal expiry is asserted. Refresh the relevant instruction after equipment, responsibility, site procedure, or regulatory changes; after an incident or demonstrated gap; and when an employer/operator requires it. Record any employer-imposed interval separately. Do not copy an external provider's renewal policy and label it a VNDRLY legal requirement.

## Records, sources, and maintenance

Keep learner identity, program/version, instruction dates, instructor qualifications, accommodations, learning-check remediation, final form and attempt scores, station observations, credential date, and limitations. Store the protected final key separately from learner materials. Review sources and site applicability before administration. The 2020 public IADC topic document is a dated benchmark, not proof of current accreditation. Preserve the included core and offshore source mappings alongside the additional rig coverage. Do not claim outside approval without an actual approval process.

# Part I — core instruction

# ESC01: Worker responsibilities, speaking up, and reliable communication

## Learning objectives
- Explain the difference between employer duties, worker responsibilities, and site stop-work arrangements.
- Use a specific stop-work message, protect people, and confirm conditions before an authorized restart.
- Report injuries, near misses, and uncertainty without guessing at causes or assigning blame.
- Recognize human performance pressures and use closed-loop communication during handoffs.

Purpose and boundaries
This lesson teaches the decisions every worker can make before specialized work begins. Your employer must establish the procedures, training, equipment, and supervision for your actual assignment. Know who controls your work, who operates the facility, how to contact emergency assistance, and where to assemble. If these answers are missing, resolve them before entering the work area. 

Employers and workers have different responsibilities
OSHA's worker-rights guidance describes employer responsibilities for a safe workplace, understandable training, and required protective equipment. Workers contribute by following applicable procedures, using provided protection correctly, reporting hazards promptly, and staying within their training and authorization. Do not accept a hazardous shortcut simply because a contractor, customer, or experienced coworker requests it. Multiple employers may be present; identify both your own supervisor and the person coordinating the site. General industry requirements in 29 CFR 1910 and construction requirements in 29 CFR 1926 depend on the work and circumstances. A worker should raise a concern rather than try to resolve legal applicability at the point of exposure.

Speak up with observable facts
A useful concern has four parts: the location, what you observed, who could be exposed, and what needs to pause. For example: “Stop the lift. A delivery driver is inside the marked landing area behind the east skid. Keep the load stationary in a safe condition and clear that area before the lift supervisor authorizes movement.” Compare this with “Be careful over there,” which does not identify an action or a person. If you cannot safely approach, use the established radio channel or alarm method. Do not step into the hazard to deliver a better explanation. Ask for acknowledgment and confirm that the message reached the people controlling the work.

Stop-work is a process
Learn the site's stop-work policy before the shift. When danger or uncertainty appears, pause your own exposed activity, alert those affected, and move to a safe position without creating another exposure. A sudden shutdown may itself release energy or remove ventilation; use the applicable emergency or controlled-stop procedure and trained operator. State what you know and what you do not know. Notify the responsible supervisor, preserve the area where safe, and have the controlling person evaluate the condition. Restart requires the hazard to be corrected or controlled, affected workers briefed, and required permits or authorizations renewed. The person who first spoke up should hear how the concern was resolved. Silence after a pause is not restart permission.

Understand the legal limit separately
A company's stop-work policy can be broader than the legal protection for refusing dangerous work. OSHA's right-to-refuse guidance describes specific conditions: a good-faith belief of imminent danger, a reasonable person's agreement that serious injury or death is a real danger, insufficient time for normal enforcement channels, and, where possible, a request that the employer correct the condition. OSHA recommends seeking correction, requesting other work, and remaining at the worksite unless ordered to leave. Do not promise that every refusal under every circumstance has identical legal protection. Workers may report hazards to OSHA and raise retaliation concerns; federal OSH Act section 11(c) complaints generally have a 30-day filing period. State-plan processes and other laws can differ. Contact OSHA promptly about the applicable process rather than delaying while seeking certainty from coworkers.

Human performance affects good people
A capable person can make a mistake when the task is unfamiliar, interruptions are frequent, labels are similar, or the schedule discourages questions. Fatigue can slow recognition and increase reliance on habit. Familiarity can make a missing barrier seem normal. A production target can turn “just this once” into an accepted routine. Treat these as signals to strengthen the work process, not as proof that a worker is careless. Before a consequential action, stop, identify the exact equipment, compare it with the instruction, and confirm the expected result. If the instruction does not match the field, do not improvise a correction from memory. Obtain clarification and update the work plan through the responsible person.

Behavior-based safety and human performance
Behavior-based safety uses agreed observations of work, respectful feedback, and follow-up to reinforce safe actions and identify obstacles. For example, observing that workers step over a hose should prompt discussion of the route, staging space, and practical alternatives, not a competition to count unsafe people. Human performance also examines how task design, information, fatigue, competing goals, and organizational conditions shape actions. Together these approaches can reveal why a method is difficult to follow and support stronger defenses. Neither makes observation a replacement for engineering controls, nor makes the nearest worker the automatic cause of an incident. Use observations to ask questions, correct immediate exposure, and improve the work system.

Communicate so the receiver can verify
Use equipment identifiers and locations rather than “that valve” or “the usual pump.” In closed-loop communication, the sender gives the instruction, the receiver repeats the critical details, and the sender confirms or corrects them. For example, “Hold vehicle entry at Gate Two” is repeated as “Gate Two entry held,” followed by confirmation. Poor reception, language differences, hearing protection, and simultaneous radio traffic can defeat a message. Stop movement if a critical instruction is uncertain. Agree on the communication method and loss-of-communication response before work. During shift handover, transfer outstanding hazards, isolation status, incomplete permits, abnormal conditions, and who currently holds responsibility. A signature alone does not demonstrate understanding.

Report to learn and protect
Report an injury or illness through the employer's process promptly, including symptoms that appear later. Obtain needed medical attention first. Report a near miss even when no equipment is damaged: the event can reveal a failed barrier before the next person is harmed. Separate observations from assumptions. Record the time, location, task, actual sequence, conditions, and witnesses; describe uncertainty honestly. Do not alter equipment or clean a scene if that would interfere with emergency control or the employer's investigation. A learning-focused discussion asks why the action made sense at the time, what information was available, and which safeguards failed. This supports accountability for correcting conditions without declaring blame before evidence exists.

Worked scenario: interrupted handoff
Nia arrives to relieve a contractor at a compressor facility. The departing worker says, “Everything is normal; maintenance is almost done.” Nia sees an open barricade and a maintenance tag but cannot identify the equipment on the tag from her position. A radio call asks her to let a delivery truck use the access lane. The tempting response is to rely on the departing worker's reassurance and clear the truck quickly. Instead, Nia holds access from a safe location and asks for the current isolation boundary, the maintenance lead, and the designated route. She repeats the lane closure over the radio and gets acknowledgment from dispatch. The maintenance lead explains that a hose crosses the lane behind the skid and the barricade was displaced. The crew restores the boundary and selects another delivery route. Nia's handoff record now identifies the remaining maintenance and route restriction. Restarting vehicle entry on the alternate route follows the site coordinator's confirmation. The lesson is that “normal” is an opinion; a handoff needs verifiable conditions.

Worked scenario: a near miss without damage
Luis sees a suspended fitting swing close to a coworker's shoulder when a tag line catches on scrap. Nobody is struck. A coworker suggests skipping the report because the lift succeeded. Luis first confirms the load is in a stable safe condition and keeps people out of the potential swing path. He tells the lift supervisor the observed sequence, including the scrap location, without claiming that the operator caused it. The team discovers that the landing area inspection covered the load's final footprint but omitted the tag-line travel path. They remove obstructions using an approved method, revise the briefing, and confirm responsibilities before another lift. The near-miss report records the barrier gap and corrective action. It does not claim that luck proves the method was acceptable. This report helps another crew recognize the same exposure during its next job.

IOGP Life-Saving Rules: purpose and field application
IOGP's nine-rule framework targets recurring fatal exposures. Its subjects are controlled safety-system overrides, confined spaces, driving, energy isolation, hot work, line of fire, mechanical lifting, work authorization, and work at height. Use these subjects as prompts before starting: which apply, which safeguards are present, and what must stop if a safeguard fails? For a pump repair beside a delivery lane, consider isolation, authorization, vehicle movement, ignition, lifting, and your position together. If a required safeguard cannot be met, pause and involve supervision. The framework complements competent people and site procedures; it does not replace them. This is VNDRLY's original educational explanation, not an official IOGP rule card, altered rule statement, or endorsement. Consult IOGP's official materials and the employer's implementation for the exact rules.

Personal preparation
Before starting, practice the exact words you would use to stop an unsafe action. Find the emergency contact method and assembly location. Ask how concerns reach both your supervisor and the host site. Confirm that training is understandable to you, including technical words and emergency signals. If you are pressured to hurry, name the unfinished safety decision rather than arguing about attitude. Reliable work begins when uncertainty becomes shared information that the team can resolve.

Source notes: OSHA Workers' Rights guidance; OSHA Workers' Right to Refuse Dangerous Work; OSH Act section 11(c), including its complaint timing. Human-performance examples and the two scenarios are original learning applications, not regulatory quotations or a guarantee about individual legal claims.

# ESC02: Hazard recognition, job analysis, and critical control verification

## Learning objectives
- Describe hazards as sources of harm, exposure paths, and possible consequences.
- Break a task into meaningful steps and identify changing and simultaneous hazards.
- Choose stronger controls using the hierarchy and verify critical controls before exposure.
- Reassess the job when people, equipment, environment, or scope changes.

Start with how harm could occur
A hazard is a source or situation with the potential to cause injury or illness. Exposure connects the hazard to a person. A moving truck can strike someone through a shared travel path; a chemical can reach a worker through breathing, skin contact, or ingestion; stored pressure can eject a fitting into a worker's position. Name the energy or material, the route to the person, and the credible consequence. “Watch your hands” is too vague to control a pinch point. “Keep hands out of the coupling gap while the suspended spool can rotate” identifies both exposure and the condition that creates it.

Look beyond what is visible
Walk the job from a safe location with people who understand the process. Look above for overhead services and falling objects, below for buried utilities and unstable ground, around for vehicles and other crews, and inside the task for pressure, electricity, chemicals, heat, and stored movement. Consider exposures that develop over time, such as noise, vibration, repetitive force, heat strain, or dust. A clean-looking area can contain an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, hazardous vapor, or energized equipment. Absence of odor, symptoms, or previous incidents is not evidence of control. Ask what credible failure could place someone in the path of harm, including during setup, testing, cleaning, troubleshooting, and departure.

Industrial hygiene: anticipate, recognize, evaluate, and control
Industrial hygiene prevents harmful exposures before illness appears. Anticipation asks what a new material, process, or work method could release. Recognition identifies sources and routes, such as respirable dust from cutting, vapor from cleaning, or noise at a compressor. Evaluation uses appropriate professional assessment and representative measurements to determine the nature and extent of exposure. Control changes the source, pathway, or work arrangement and verifies that protection works. For example, changing a cleaning task from wiping to spraying should trigger review of airborne exposure and nearby people before the change occurs. Workers provide accurate task details, report failed controls, and follow the resulting instructions. They do not calculate an exposure limit from odor or select a sampling strategy without competence. 

Build a usable job safety analysis
A JSA or JHA breaks work into steps, identifies hazards for each step, and assigns controls. The name and form vary by employer. Useful steps are specific enough to reveal changes: unload the skid, position it, connect piping, test the connection, and return the system to service. “Complete repair safely” hides all those exposures. Involve workers who will do the task and people affected by it. Identify the person responsible for each control and the point at which it must be checked. A JSA supports planning; it does not replace a required permit, equipment-specific isolation procedure, atmospheric assessment, lift plan, or specialist training. Read it against actual field conditions instead of signing it from the truck.

Use the hierarchy to improve the plan
Elimination removes the hazard, such as arranging inspection from outside a vessel. Substitution replaces the hazard with a less hazardous option, but the replacement must be evaluated for new risks. Engineering controls physically separate people or contain the hazard, such as fixed guarding or a designed remote connection. Administrative controls organize work through procedures, restricted access, scheduling, and training. PPE reduces particular exposure when properly selected and used. Lower levels often depend more heavily on perfect human behavior. A sign and gloves do not prevent a moving machine from crushing a hand. Several controls may be necessary; choose them for how they interrupt the exposure path rather than how easy they are to list.

Compare consequences before probability
People commonly rate familiar work as low risk because they have performed it many times. Repetition does not make an uncontrolled fatal exposure acceptable. First identify the credible severe outcome, then ask which barriers prevent it and what could defeat them. A risk matrix can support prioritization but cannot authorize bypassing a mandatory safeguard. Do not lower a risk score simply because everyone signs the form. For a pressure release, the key questions include who establishes a safe energy state, how that state is verified, and how workers remain out of the release path. The answer cannot be “experienced crew” alone.

Critical controls need evidence
A critical control is a safeguard whose absence or failure could permit a serious event in the particular task. Your employer defines its critical controls and verification process. Examples can include verified energy isolation, a suitable excavation protective system, a maintained exclusion zone, or functioning atmosphere monitoring. Convert each control into an observable question: Is the correct equipment identified? Has the authorized person completed the required verification? Is the physical barrier present throughout the exposed area? Who will monitor the condition? A completed box is a record, not the control itself. If you cannot verify a control within your role, obtain confirmation from the authorized person and do not enter the exposure while the answer is missing.

Recognize change as a new decision
Stop and reassess when equipment, personnel, sequence, location, weather, materials, or surrounding activities change. A substitute hose may have a different rating. A new worker may not know the agreed signal. Rain may affect ground support and electrical conditions. A delivery can block an escape route. A test may introduce pressure that was absent during installation. The significance is not whether the change seems small; it is whether assumptions or controls are affected. Tell the work leader what changed, pause affected exposure, revise the assessment and permits as required, brief everyone affected, and obtain the appropriate authorization before resuming.

Worked scenario: hose replacement beside a traffic route
A crew plans to replace a process hose at a production facility. The initial JSA lists gloves, eye protection, and “be aware of traffic.” During the walkdown, Sam notices that the hose connection faces the walkway and a forklift route passes behind the mechanic. A small gauge is visible, but Sam is not authorized to isolate or prove the pressure state. The crew identifies two different severe outcomes: fluid release toward the mechanic and vehicle impact from behind. They ask the authorized process operator to apply and verify the equipment-specific energy-control arrangement. They arrange a physical traffic separation and a controlled alternative pedestrian route. They consider whether the task can be repositioned or performed remotely to reduce time near the connection. PPE is selected for the actual material and remains supplementary. Before starting, Sam confirms the route barrier is in place and asks the work leader to explain the verified isolation boundary. When a forklift driver asks to move a barrier for one delivery, the crew pauses. The revised route must be approved and briefed before either activity proceeds. The original JSA was inadequate because it named precautions without interrupting both exposure paths.

Worked scenario: rain changes an excavation task
A cable crew has a plan for work near a shallow excavation. Overnight rain leaves pooled water, the access track is rutted, and spoil has shifted toward the edge. A worker suggests proceeding because the permit is still dated for today and the task will take ten minutes. Maya stays outside the excavation and reports the changed conditions. The responsible competent person must evaluate the excavation under the applicable construction requirements; Maya does not decide that the soil is stable by looking at it. Equipment positioning, edge loads, access, protective measures, and possible utility exposure are reviewed. The crew also reassesses the vehicle route so a reversing truck will not approach the edge. Water removal itself requires a controlled plan because pumps, hoses, and discharge can introduce hazards. The work resumes only after the required inspections and controls are in place and the crew understands the revised boundaries. The lesson is not that rain always closes every excavation. It is that weather invalidates assumptions and creates a defined need for reassessment by the responsible people.

Put the analysis into a conversation
At the briefing, each worker should be able to identify the task boundary, the worst credible exposure, their safe position, their responsibility, and the trigger for stopping. Ask a coworker to explain the plan in their own words. A language barrier is a planning issue, not a reason to collect a signature without understanding. Include nearby contractors whose work could affect the task. Repeat the briefing for arrivals and relief personnel. If production changes the sequence, the assessment must follow the work rather than the form remaining frozen.

Close the learning loop
After the task, note controls that were difficult to maintain, missing tools, unexpected conditions, and successful improvements. Feed these observations into the next planning cycle. A control that repeatedly requires workers to stretch across a hazard may need redesign. A recurring temporary barrier conflict may indicate that the work area or schedule is unsuitable. OSHA's safety-management guidance supports ongoing hazard identification and control; those recommended practices complement, rather than replace, specific enforceable standards. Learning from routine work is how a JSA becomes a useful prediction instead of a document written only after an incident.

Source notes: OSHA Recommended Practices, Hazard Identification and Assessment and Hazard Prevention and Control, especially their action items on inspections, changes, hierarchy, control plans, and effectiveness. OSHA Job Hazard Analysis publication 3071 supports step-based analysis. These planning examples are original and do not prescribe engineering designs or qualify a person to perform specialist inspections.

# ESC03: Permits, simultaneous operations, and hazardous energy boundaries

## Learning objectives
- Explain permit limits and changed-condition review.
- Recognize interacting operations and a controlling coordinator.
- Identify multiple hazardous energy sources.
- Distinguish affected-worker recognition, authorized energy control, and qualified electrical work.

The permit is a controlled agreement
A permit-to-work system records who may perform a defined activity, where and when it may occur, what conditions must exist, and who authorizes it. Permit names vary by employer: hot work, confined space, excavation, line opening, electrical work, and other high-risk activities may have separate controls. A permit does not create isolation, remove vapor, or make a damaged barrier effective. Read its scope against the actual equipment and work. Confirm the work location, duration, responsible persons, required safeguards, linked permits, and suspension conditions. If you do not understand a condition or cannot identify the work area, ask before beginning.

Authorization has limits
A permit for replacing one component does not automatically authorize testing, entry into connected equipment, or a different repair. A permit issued for daylight work may depend on visibility that disappears after dark. A permit that requires ventilation cannot remain a basis for entry after the fan fails. Report changes, make the work safe within your role, and have the responsible issuer reassess, suspend, cancel, or reissue as the site system requires. Do not alter a condition or extend an expiry yourself. At handback, account for people, tools, guards, open connections, and remaining restrictions through the established process. Work completion and readiness to energize are different decisions.

SIMOPS means interactions matter
Simultaneous operations occur when activities share space, equipment, utilities, access, atmosphere, or emergency arrangements. A welding crew and a transfer crew may each have a valid task plan while their combination creates ignition exposure. A crane can block an evacuation route without entering another crew's immediate workspace. A shutdown can remove ventilation needed by another permit. Identify who coordinates these interactions and which employers must communicate. Share the timing, energy changes, exclusion zones, route needs, and foreseeable emissions of your task. Scheduling activities separately can remove conflicts more reliably than asking both crews to be especially careful.

Controlled bypass: a managed exception, not a shortcut
A safety control may need a proposed temporary override for defined testing, maintenance, or fault diagnosis. Repeated trips, inconvenient access, or a production deadline do not authorize it. Keep the control effective while the responsible authority evaluates whether any bypass is legally and technically permissible. The employer's controlled process must identify the affected safeguard, reason, hazards, authorized decision maker, people exposed, and necessary compensating controls. It must define a limited scope and time, monitoring and communication, stop conditions, and responsibility for restoration. Record approval and the actual status so the next shift cannot assume normal protection. Only appropriately authorized personnel implement an approved change; a general worker must not operate the override. Before normal service, responsible personnel verify restoration and document closure. No internal approval can waive a required legal safeguard. If no permissible adequately controlled method exists, the task must be redesigned or remain stopped.

Recognize energy before touching
Electrical supply is only one energy source. A spring may move a mechanism after power is removed. Gravity can lower a raised component. A hydraulic accumulator can retain pressure. A hot line can burn after flow stops. Chemical reaction or trapped process pressure can continue in a closed section. Remote controls, automatic sequences, alternate feeds, and connected systems can reintroduce energy. The question is not merely whether the switch is off. Ask what sources could move, release, heat, energize, or otherwise harm someone at this point. An untrained worker reports these possibilities and stays outside the exposure; an authorized person follows the equipment-specific energy-control procedure.

Understand roles without claiming qualification
Where 1910.147 applies, an authorized employee performs the energy-control functions, an affected employee operates or uses equipment being serviced or works in the area, and other employees need instruction relevant to their exposure. An affected worker must not restart or defeat a locked or tagged condition. Do not borrow a key, interfere with another person's lock, operate an isolated control, or assume an unattended lock is abandoned. Exceptional lock-removal procedures belong to the employer's controlled program. Group work and shift changes need continuity of protection. This lesson does not teach a complete isolation sequence or authorize you to apply a personal lock without the required task-specific training.

Apply the correct legal framework
1910.147(a)(1)(ii) expressly excludes construction and oil and gas well drilling and servicing, among other activities. Electrical work on utilization installations is addressed separately under general industry Subpart S, including 1910.333. Construction electrical controls include 1926.417; additional construction requirements may also apply. The exclusion from one standard is not an exemption from controlling a serious energy hazard. Your employer must identify the applicable requirements and safe procedures for the actual operation. Do not transfer a general industry factory procedure to well servicing merely because both use pumps. Site programs may require robust isolation beyond a particular standard's minimum applicability.

What verification means to an affected worker
The authorized team must establish the required safe energy state and verify it using appropriate procedures. Seeing a lock, hearing a motor stop, or reading a single gauge does not independently prove every source is controlled. A stopped machine may still be connected to an automatic start signal. A pressure gauge may be isolated from the section being opened. Ask the work leader which equipment is covered, which sources are addressed, and how the authorized verification is documented or communicated. Do not loosen a fitting or reach into equipment to check for yourself. If the field identifier differs from the permit or isolation record, stop and resolve that mismatch before anyone is exposed.

Electrical recognition and nonqualified boundaries
Exposed conductors, broken covers, damaged plugs, wet electrical equipment, arcing, burning odor, and repeated protective-device trips require attention from the responsible qualified person. When electrical equipment is uncertain, stop the affected task and involve the responsible qualified person. Under 1910.333, equipment not properly deenergized and controlled must be treated as energized; qualified persons perform required electrical verification and specified energized work. Maintain the employer's established approach boundaries, respect barricades, and keep conductive tools or materials from entering them. Around overhead lines, do not estimate safety by eye or assume a line is insulated. Have the operation planned using the applicable voltage, equipment, and clearance requirements. If equipment contacts an electrical line, keep others away and activate the site emergency plan; do not touch the equipment or attempt an improvised rescue.

Worked scenario: a permit conflict during transfer
A maintenance crew has a hot-work permit for a bracket on the north side of a tank area. A contractor arrives to begin a previously scheduled transfer on an adjacent connection. The maintenance helper sees a hose being positioned and asks whether the permit coordinator knows. The transfer driver says their paperwork is valid, so the jobs should be independent. The helper pauses the affected hot work and reports the new transfer, location, and timing. The coordinator brings the responsible operators together and evaluates possible vapor release, ignition sources, shared access, and emergency routes. They decide to separate the activities in time and reassess the hot-work area before issuing the restart. Nobody treats one permit as permission to ignore the other crew. The resolution is an interaction decision made by responsible people; the helper's contribution is recognizing the changed condition and preventing exposure while it is resolved.

Worked scenario: the quiet pump
A contractor is asked to clear a jam at a pump. The operator has selected OFF at the local control and the machine is silent. A coworker proposes reaching through the removed guard because the task will be quick. Elena declines to enter the hazard area and tells the work leader that the proposed access exposes moving parts. The team identifies possible automatic control, stored pressure, and mechanical movement. An authorized person must establish the appropriate equipment-specific isolation under the applicable site program; a qualified electrical worker handles any required electrical work or testing. Elena also notices that the tag number on the permit differs by one digit from the pump plate. Work stays paused until the identification is reconciled. Only after the correct equipment and controls are verified and the task is authorized can trained personnel perform the repair. Silence was a machine condition, not proof of an isolated safe state.

Pressure systems and line opening
Pressure may be trapped between closures, retained in an accumulator, or reintroduced through connected equipment. A pinhole leak can inject fluid through skin; a small-looking wound after a pressure release needs immediate medical assessment under the emergency plan. Never search for a leak with a hand or try to tighten or disconnect a live leaking fitting. Hose identity, pressure and temperature rating, material compatibility, condition, connections, and required restraint arrangements must be verified by responsible competent personnel for the actual service. Appearance and outside diameter do not prove suitability. Line opening requires its specific authorization, isolation and verification arrangements, material information, release-path control, and protection for nearby people. A general worker's decision is to stay outside the possible jet, whip, ejection, and spray paths and report missing evidence. This lesson does not teach valve sequences, depressurization, pressure testing, or connection-breaking techniques.

A practical release question
Before returning equipment, ask who has authority to accept it and who could still be exposed. A shift change, absent coworker, or remote team is a reason to confirm accountability, not to assume everyone is clear. Communicate remaining defects and restrictions explicitly. The final handback should leave the next operator with an accurate equipment state, rather than a vague message that maintenance is finished.

The permit and SIMOPS examples are original planning applications and are not substitutes for a facility's written programs.

Supplemental pressure sources: BSEE Safety Alert 465 (June 29, 2023), hose application, compatibility, inspection, and barriers; UK HSE hydraulic-injection safety alert FOD 4-2014, injury recognition and immediate medical treatment. # ESC04: Fatal-risk recognition: spaces, ignition, falls, loads, and excavations

## Learning objectives
- Recognize fatal exposure paths and stay within authorization.
- Explain confined-space entry boundaries and prevent improvised rescue.
- Identify missing hot-work, fall, lifting, and excavation controls.
- Choose a safe pause and responsible specialist reassessment.

Recognize a fatal exposure early
Some tasks can cause multiple deaths after a single failed barrier. Confined-space entry, hot work around flammable materials, falls, suspended loads, excavations, and uncontrolled release paths deserve explicit decisions before a person enters the exposure. This lesson teaches recognition and escalation. It does not qualify you as an entrant, attendant, rescue worker, competent person, rigger, crane operator, scaffold inspector, or fall-protection designer. Know the employer's authorization process for your role. If a boundary or required specialist is missing, keep people outside the exposure and obtain a decision through the responsible supervisor.

Confined spaces are defined by configuration and hazards
Under general industry 1910.146(b), a confined space is large enough to bodily enter and work, has limited or restricted entry or exit, and is not designed for continuous occupancy. A permit-required space has specified serious hazards, including a hazardous atmosphere, engulfment potential, a trapping configuration, or another recognized serious hazard. An open top does not make a pit safe. Entry occurs when any body part crosses the opening into the permit space; reaching inside is not a harmless alternative. General industry 1910.146 and construction Subpart AA have different applicability and coordination provisions. The employer must evaluate and classify the actual space and establish entry controls. You cannot classify a vessel by its appearance or yesterday's readings.

Entry requires a complete system
Before authorized entry, the applicable program must address isolation, acceptable conditions, testing and monitoring, ventilation where appropriate, communication, entry supervision, attendant duties, and rescue arrangements. These are connected safeguards: a ventilation failure can change a previously acceptable atmosphere; an upstream process change can introduce material. Do not enter on the strength of a signed permit alone. If an entrant collapses, raise the alarm and follow the emergency plan from a safe position. Do not become an unprotected would-be rescuer. Rescue capability must be selected and arranged for the actual space and hazards; simply knowing an emergency telephone number does not establish that the needed rescue can be performed in time.

Hot work changes the ignition picture
Welding, cutting, grinding, and other spark- or heat-producing work can ignite vapor, deposits, insulation, or material on the opposite side of a surface. Look for drains, openings, connected lines, hidden combustible material, and nearby transfers. A vessel that once contained a flammable material can remain hazardous when apparently empty. The responsible persons must establish preparation, authorization, fire protection, and atmosphere controls for the actual work. General industry 1910.252(a) addresses fire prevention for welding and cutting, including restrictions where flammable atmospheres exist and safeguards for used containers. A fire watch, when required, must be trained, positioned, equipped, and maintained for the required conditions and duration. A convenient extinguisher is not permission to weld on an unknown tank.

Fall hazards are more than height
Edges, floor holes, fragile surfaces, poor access, falling objects, and a fall into dangerous equipment all require assessment. General industry 1910.28 and construction 1926.501 have different provisions; four feet and six feet are common thresholds for particular unprotected-edge situations, not universal safe-height rules. Special activities and hazards have other requirements. Ask which protection applies rather than deciding that anything below a remembered number is safe. Prefer avoiding elevated work or using suitable collective protection where feasible. Personal fall-arrest use requires suitable equipment, anchorage, clearance, compatibility, inspection, training, and rescue planning. A harness without an appropriate system does not prevent a fall. Do not select an anchor by appearance or attach to convenient piping.

Suspended loads and line of fire
Line of fire describes the path of moving equipment, falling or swinging loads, released pressure, snapping tensioned material, or objects ejected by stored energy. Consider where a load could travel if it shifts, where a hose could whip, and what could trap a person against a fixed surface. Stay outside the established lift and travel zones. Do not walk under a suspended load or stand between a moving load and a structure. Only designated, trained persons should rig, signal, or guide within the approved plan. Construction 1926.1425 restricts fall-zone exposure and addresses load routes; limited task-specific exceptions are not general permission to approach. If a barrier is breached or communication is lost, call the agreed stop and have the responsible lift team restore control.

Excavation recognition
A trench can collapse without a useful warning and can also contain hazardous atmospheres, utilities, water, and vehicle hazards. Under construction 1926.651, utility locations, access, edge conditions, water, and competent-person inspections are among the required considerations. Protective-system requirements are addressed by 1926.652. Do not enter an unassessed excavation because it looks shallow or the task is brief. A competent person's daily inspection and inspections after rain or other hazard-increasing events are important triggers for stopping and reassessing. Spoil, equipment, or vibration at the edge can add loading. Remain outside barricades and use designated crossings; never leap a trench with tools. If you see cracking, water entry, falling material, or a damaged protective system, alert the crew and keep out while responsible personnel act.

Worked scenario: a tool inside a tank
Owen drops a measuring device through a tank opening while working on an external platform. A coworker offers to lean through the manway and retrieve it because nobody will climb all the way in. Owen recognizes that crossing the opening with part of the body can be entry under the permit-space definition. He keeps clear, prevents others from reaching in, and contacts the responsible space authority. The tank's status is not established by its open hatch or the absence of odor. The authorized team reviews the space evaluation, connected process conditions, and whether retrieval can be completed from outside without creating another exposure. They select an appropriate external method under their procedure. If entry had been necessary, it would have required the applicable entry program and authorized personnel. The correct result is the recovered tool without an improvised entry. The critical decision occurred before anyone crossed the opening, not after a gas detector alarm.

Worked scenario: a lift over an excavation route
At a construction site, a crane is moving a pipe spool toward a rack. The planned pedestrian route passes beside an excavation, and a temporary barrier has been moved to make room for delivery. A helper sees two workers taking the shortcut while the load approaches. The helper uses the agreed stop signal and remains outside the load path. The lift team brings the operation to a controlled safe state. The site coordinator closes the shortcut, establishes a separated route, and asks the excavation competent person to assess changed edge loading and access conditions before anyone uses the area. The rigging team confirms its landing zone and communications before resuming. It would be wrong to solve only the lifting problem by diverting pedestrians through the excavation or to solve only the traffic problem by standing beneath the load to direct people. The team considers the combined exposures and verifies both boundaries.

Worked scenario: sparks beside a hidden drain
A mechanic has authorization to grind a support outside a process building. During the walkdown, Priya notices a grated drain behind the support and a hose connected to nearby equipment. She does not decide that outdoor air makes ignition impossible. She pauses the proposed grinding and identifies the drain and hose to the permit issuer. The useful observation was not a complete chemical diagnosis; it was recognizing a possible route between an ignition source and material outside the mechanic's immediate view.

Specialist work and different jurisdictions
Explosives and perforating systems, commercial diving, and electric-power generation or transmission work require their own specialist programs, competent personnel, equipment, and task authorization. Keep outside identified blast, dive-support, and electrical exclusion zones. Do not handle unfamiliar explosive devices, disturb dive equipment, or assist power work on the assumption that a general safety credential authorizes it. Marine transfers and work over water also require an assessment of drowning, access, weather, vessel movement, suitable flotation and recovery arrangements under the actual operator and regulatory requirements. Shipyard, marine-terminal, longshoring, offshore, agriculture, mining, and aviation duties can fall under different regimes. Identify that boundary before accepting the assignment. These statements provide a referral boundary only; they do not teach the specialist tasks, satisfy their training requirements, or grant a marine, diving, explosives, or power-work qualification.

Your decision sequence
Name the severe event, identify the physical boundary, and determine who controls authorization. Ask what evidence establishes that critical safeguards are in place now. Stay out when a required condition is unknown. Report the exact deficiency and keep others informed without entering to demonstrate it. If conditions change, use the same sequence again. 

Source notes: 1910.146(a), (b), (d), and (k); construction 1926.1203(a) and coordination provisions (h)-(i); 1910.252(a); 1910.28(b); 1926.501(b); 1926.1425; 1926.651(b), (c), (h), (j), and (k); and 1926.652(a). Applicability and exceptions must be resolved for the actual task by the employer. Scenarios are original decision practice.

Scaffold and cylinder recognition
A scaffold is a planned access structure, not an arrangement a user may alter to improve reach. Before access, use the site inspection and release system, check the designated access route and visible condition, and report missing decking, rails, ties, damaged components or an uncertain status. Stay out of an unreleased or suspect scaffold. Users need the actual scaffold hazard training; erection, alteration and inspection involve different competent or qualified roles. Adding a ladder or removing a brace can change the structure; report the proposed change before continuing.

Compressed-gas cylinders can create pressure, projectile, fire, oxygen-enrichment or asphyxiation hazards depending on their contents. Confirm identity from reliable labeling, use the approved secured storage and movement method, protect valves as required, and separate incompatible materials under the actual procedure. Do not identify a gas by cylinder color, use a damaged cylinder, improvise adapters, or put oil/grease on oxygen equipment. The actual regulator, connections, handling equipment and task require trained evaluation. An uncontrolled release needs the emergency response, not an untrained attempt to tighten a leaking connection.

# ESC05: Chemical information, H2S, atmosphere alarms, and respiratory boundaries

## Learning objectives
- Use labels and SDS sections to identify task-specific chemical questions.
- Recognize H2S and other atmosphere hazards without relying on odor.
- Explain detector limitations and follow alarm, escape, and reentry arrangements.
- Distinguish awareness from employer exposure evaluation, medical evaluation, fit testing, and respirator authorization.

Chemicals create several exposure paths
A chemical can harm through inhalation, skin or eye contact, ingestion from contaminated hands, or injection from a high-pressure release. Some effects are immediate; others develop after repeated exposure or a delay. A clear liquid may be corrosive or flammable. A familiar product can create a different hazard when heated, sprayed, mixed, or used in a poorly ventilated location. Begin by identifying the exact product and task. Do not substitute a similar product name or rely on container color. If a label is missing or does not match the work instruction, isolate the uncertainty through your supervisor before use.

Read a label as a decision aid
Under 1910.1200(f), shipped-container information includes product identification, signal word, hazard statements, pictograms, precautionary statements, and supplier information. Workplace labeling arrangements can differ under the written program. Learn the site's system and do not assume every workplace container uses the full shipped label. A pictogram groups hazards; it does not tell you every condition for safe use. “Danger” and “Warning” indicate different relative severity categories within the labeling system, not a complete ranking across every chemical. Read the actual hazard and precaution statements. Do not remove or obscure a label. An exception for certain portable containers under the immediate-use provision is narrow, not permission to leave unidentified chemicals for the next person.

Find information before the emergency
The employer's hazard communication program must address chemical information and training. Safety data sheets must be readily accessible during the shift; know the backup arrangement if an electronic system is unavailable. Construction 1926.59 incorporates the same hazard communication requirements as 1910.1200. An SDS supports a task assessment but does not itself authorize the work or select the complete site control system. Match its product identifier to the container and check that it is the relevant supplier document. Tell the responsible person if information is missing, conflicting, or difficult to understand.

Use the SDS sections efficiently
Section 1 identifies the product and supplier; Section 2 summarizes hazards. Section 4 addresses first aid, 5 firefighting, and 6 accidental release. Section 7 covers handling and storage; Section 8 addresses exposure controls and personal protection. Sections 9 and 10 help identify properties and dangerous reactions. Section 11 addresses toxicological information, and Section 16 includes preparation or revision information. The standardized format has 16 sections; OSHA does not enforce the content of Sections 12 through 15. Read the sections needed for the decision rather than searching only for a glove recommendation. For example, storage incompatibility can make a perfectly fitted glove irrelevant to a reaction between two materials.

Translate information into the actual task
Consider amount, concentration, temperature, duration, application method, ventilation, and nearby people. Brushing a small quantity outdoors and spraying the same product inside a tank are different exposures. Check that selected gloves resist the actual chemical for the expected contact, rather than choosing gloves because they look thick. Eye and face protection, clothing, ventilation, and hygiene must address the identified routes together. Do not mix products unless the authorized procedure explicitly calls for it. Keep food and drink out of contaminated work areas and wash before eating. A general worker should identify a discrepancy and obtain the employer's assessment instead of inventing compatibility or exposure limits.

Recognize hydrogen sulfide without using smell
Hydrogen sulfide, H2S, can occur in oil and gas operations, wastewater, and other processes. It is toxic and flammable and can accumulate in low or poorly ventilated areas. Odor is unreliable because smell can fatigue or be lost; at dangerous concentrations a person can be overcome rapidly. Never approach a release to smell-test it. Symptoms, absence of odor, and the fact that another person is still standing are not safe-air measurements. Learn the facility's alarm signals, escape routes, wind indicators, muster locations, and accountability process before entering a designated area. These arrangements must reflect the actual process and possible release paths.

Testing is a defined responsibility
The employer must evaluate atmospheric hazards and arrange suitable monitoring, controls, and trained personnel for the work. A personal detector is not a universal clearance instrument. Know which gases its sensors detect, the alarm response, where it must be worn, and the pre-use checks required by the manufacturer's instructions and employer procedure. Bump testing checks sensor and alarm response; calibration adjusts instrument response using the prescribed method. A powered display alone establishes neither. Only trained authorized personnel should interpret task-clearance measurements or change settings. Oxygen, flammable gas, and toxic contaminants are separate concerns; a reading for one does not establish the others. Unknown conditions require the prescribed safe response, not a guess at what the detector would have shown.

Respond to alarms and suspected releases
On an alarm or suspected hazardous atmosphere, stop exposed activity and follow the emergency plan to a safe location, avoiding the plume and low areas. Use the designated escape route and wind guidance, and report to muster for accountability. Warn others from safety and provide the location, observed conditions, and missing-person information to responders. Do not investigate the source or reenter because the alarm becomes quiet. Only the responsible team can authorize reentry after appropriate evaluation. If someone collapses near a suspected release, raise the alarm; do not enter without the required rescue capability and authorization. Escape equipment, where provided, is for its designated escape function and does not turn the wearer into a rescuer.

Respiratory protection is an employer program
Where required, 1910.134(c)-(f) requires a written, worksite-specific program administered by a qualified person, hazard-based respirator selection, a medical evaluation before required use or fit testing, and fit testing for tight-fitting facepieces before use, at least annually, and when specified changes occur. Required protection, training, and medical evaluations are provided without employee cost. A user seal check each donning is separate from a fit test. Facial hair cannot interfere with the seal or valve function. The employer must address maintenance, cartridge service life, training, and program effectiveness. An air-purifying respirator does not supply oxygen and is not suitable for an oxygen-deficient or unknown atmosphere. IDLH entry requires the specified atmosphere-supplying protection and arrangements. Construction 1926.103 incorporates 1910.134.

Jalen sets it down safely without opening it and reports the missing identification. # ESC06: Occupational health, exposure prevention, and worker hygiene

## Learning objectives
- Recognize delayed and immediate occupational health effects and routes of exposure.
- Select practical source controls for dust, noise, radiation, temperature, and ergonomic hazards.
- Explain hygiene, exposure reporting, and conditional medical-surveillance responsibilities.
- Respond safely to two realistic exposure situations without attempting specialist work.

### Health hazards can develop before symptoms
A worker can finish a shift feeling well while accumulating a harmful exposure. Silica-related lung disease, some cancers, hearing loss, and effects of lead may develop gradually. Acute illness can also occur rapidly from excessive heat, cold, a biological exposure, or concentrated chemicals. Risk depends on the substance or energy, concentration or intensity, route, duration, and worker circumstances. Smell, appearance, and a coworker's lack of symptoms do not establish safety. Your role is to recognize the exposure opportunity, use the established controls, report changes, and stop when those controls are missing.

Start the job by identifying what will be disturbed, heated, sprayed, handled, or released. Read the relevant safety data sheet and task procedure; ask about previous sampling and nearby operations. Inhalation, ingestion, skin or eye contact, and injection through punctures are distinct exposure routes. A glove that prevents skin contact does not clean breathing air. A disposable face covering does not establish respiratory protection. Use substitution, enclosure, ventilation, isolation, and improved work methods before relying solely on personal protection. Monitoring must represent the task and actual conditions; a reading from a different material or location may not answer today's question.

### Silica, asbestos, and lead
Sand handling, abrasive work, and cutting or grinding silica-containing material can generate respirable crystalline silica. The hazardous fraction can be too small to see. Keep transfer systems closed, use maintained dust collection or an approved wet method, and stay outside dust sources. Do not create a secondary exposure by dry sweeping contaminated material or blowing clothing clean. Report failed collection and changes in material flow. The general-industry silica standard is 1910.1053; construction uses 1926.1153. Their exposure-control and medical-surveillance provisions must be applied to the actual work. A supervisor must arrange the required assessment rather than asking a worker to judge the limit by visibility.

Asbestos may occur in older insulation, gaskets, or other installed materials. Intact material and material being cut or removed present different release opportunities. A weathered covering is not identified reliably by color or age alone. Stop before disturbing suspect material, isolate the area, and obtain identification and an authorized work plan. Do not take an improvised sample or remove a small amount as a test. General-industry asbestos provisions and construction asbestos provisions differ; 1910.1001 and 1926.1101 are relevant starting points. Awareness training does not qualify you to perform asbestos abatement.

Lead can be present in coatings or process materials. Grinding, burning, and abrasive removal can put it in dust or fumes; contaminated hands can transfer it to food. Obtain the coating assessment before disturbing an unknown surface. Use the specified containment and hygiene arrangements. Do not eat or smoke in contaminated areas or take dusty clothing home for ordinary laundering. Lead requirements differ between 1910.1025 and construction 1926.62. Exposure assessment determines applicable controls and medical provisions. Do not interpret a medical test yourself or assume that an absence of symptoms rules out exposure.

### Noise, radiation, and temperature
Noise can mask alarms and damage hearing permanently. A need to shout is a warning to seek assessment, not a sound-level measurement. Quiet equipment, separation, enclosures, and maintenance can reduce the source. Wear the hearing protection selected for the task and fit it correctly; lifting an earmuff seal for conversation defeats protection. Report ringing or changed hearing promptly. OSHA's 1910.95 includes a specific exception for paragraphs c through n for oil and gas well drilling and servicing. Do not turn general-industry hearing-conservation thresholds into a universal legal claim for every energy job. Employers still must evaluate applicable noise requirements and protect workers.

Naturally occurring radioactive material, or NORM, may concentrate in some scale, sludge, and equipment. Industrial radiography also introduces radiation sources that are entirely different from ordinary equipment. A radiation symbol, survey tag, or controlled boundary demands attention even when nothing is visible or smelled. Do not grind scale, open tagged equipment, move a source, or enter a radiography exclusion zone without the applicable authorization. Notify the responsible specialist. Follow their survey, contamination-control, time, distance, and shielding instructions. OSHA radiation standards and other agencies' radiation rules may apply.

Heat risk increases with workload, humidity, radiant heat, protective clothing, and lack of acclimatization. Arrange water, recovery breaks, shade or cooling, communication, and gradual adjustment for new or returning workers. Do not wait for thirst or rely on toughness. Dizziness, unusual weakness, nausea, or cramps require stopping and assistance. Confusion, collapse, or altered behavior can signal a medical emergency: activate help and begin the site's trained cooling response without leaving the person alone. Do not give drinks to an unconscious or confused person.

Cold exposure can impair judgment and hand function before a worker recognizes the danger. Wind and wet clothing accelerate cooling. Plan dry layers, warm recovery space, suitable gloves, and work-rest arrangements. Report uncontrolled shivering, numbness, clumsiness, or confusion. Move to safe shelter and obtain appropriate medical assistance; do not rub suspected frostbite or apply direct intense heat. Keep combustion heaters and engines from introducing carbon monoxide into enclosed recovery areas. A warm cabin is not safe if its ventilation is defeated.

### Biological hazards, ergonomics, and hygiene
Field work may involve insects, ticks, animal bites, contaminated water, sewage, mold, or rodent waste. Inspect cabins and storage areas before handling debris. Avoid contact with carcasses, nests, sharps, and unidentified waste. Use the employer's pest, sanitation, and cleanup arrangements; do not sweep a dusty contaminated enclosure as ordinary housekeeping. Cover cuts, wash hands, use appropriate protective clothing, and report bites or exposures promptly. Bloodborne exposures have a separate response addressed in the emergency lesson.

Reduce musculoskeletal risk by changing the task, not merely lifting more carefully. Estimate load, grip, reach, travel distance, repetition, vibration, and awkward posture. Move supplies closer, use handling equipment, split an approved load, or obtain a planned team lift. Avoid twisting while carrying and never use a team lift where workers cannot coordinate footing and release. Report tingling, pain, and loss of function early. Stretching cannot make an excessive load safe.

Use designated clean eating and drinking areas, potable water, and suitable washing facilities. Remove contamination as instructed before breaks, using methods that do not spread it. Keep clean and dirty items separate. Never use solvent or compressed air to clean skin. Occupational exposure and medical records are different from an incident log. Ask the employer how to request records under applicable access rules such as 1910.1020. Surveillance is conditional on the relevant standard and exposure facts; it is not a single annual examination required for everyone. Keep medical details private and provide work restrictions through the designated process.

The lesson is to correct the failed barrier and assess exposure, not issue an improvised mask and continue.

### Worked scenario: unfamiliar scale and an unwell helper
At a maintenance pad, Luis is asked to wire-brush scale from a tagged pipe spool. His new helper has been carrying parts in heavy protective clothing and becomes unsteady. Luis separates the problems: the unknown scale requires a material assessment, while the helper needs immediate care. ### Additional exposure decisions: radiation, infection, and coatings
TENORM means naturally occurring radioactive material whose concentration or accessibility has been increased by human activity. Oil and gas production can concentrate radionuclides in scale and sludge. External radiation and inhaling or ingesting contaminated particles are different exposure routes; long-term radiation exposure can increase cancer risk. Ordinary PPE does not shield every form of penetrating radiation. Keep tagged material undisturbed, prevent dust generation and hand-to-mouth transfer, and obtain specialist survey and disposal instructions. Do not assume all produced equipment is radioactive, or that an absence of a tag establishes that it is clean.

Infectious disease prevention also concerns person-to-person spread, not only bites and contaminated waste. Follow the employer's illness-reporting, cleaning, ventilation, and exposure arrangements. Wash hands before eating and after contamination; use the designated cleaning products as instructed. Cover coughs and avoid sharing contaminated personal items. If ill, seek guidance before joining close-contact work or shared transport. Do not conceal symptoms to avoid delaying a crew, and do not diagnose or exclude coworkers based on rumor. The particular infection and workplace determine additional precautions; bloodborne controls do not automatically control airborne transmission.

Welding stainless steel or disturbing some chromium-containing coatings can create hexavalent chromium exposure. Its serious effects include respiratory disease and cancer; skin or eye contact can also cause harm. Identify the material and process, obtain the required exposure assessment and ventilation or other controls, and follow respiratory and hygiene arrangements when applicable. Do not infer that an ordinary dust mask or outdoor location makes fume exposure safe. General-industry 1910.1026 and construction 1926.1126 have distinct applicability and conditional medical requirements.

For a lift remaining after elimination and handling-aid review, check the destination and clear the route before grasping the load. Test whether you can handle it without strain; do not jerk an unexpectedly heavy object. Bring the load close, establish balanced footing, bend through hips and knees as appropriate, and rise smoothly without twisting. Turn by moving your feet and set the load down with controlled hands and a clear landing place. Stop if grip, visibility, or footing is inadequate. Tool ergonomics also requires neutral wrist positions, suitable handle size, supported work height, reduced sustained grip, and management of repetition and vibration. Rotate tasks only as part of a real exposure-reduction plan, not to distribute an excessive hazard to more people.

# ESC07: Journey management, driving, and mobile-equipment interfaces

## Learning objectives
- Plan a journey around driver fitness, vehicle condition, route, weather, and communication.
- Explain pedestrian separation, spotting, backing, and load-interface controls.
- Distinguish driving permission from equipment-specific operator qualification.
- Work through fatigue and delivery scenarios using stop-and-reassess decisions.

### Treat transportation as part of the job
A trip between facilities can expose a worker to more risk than the task at the destination. Rural roads may combine darkness, dust, wildlife, narrow bridges, school traffic, heavy trucks, and drivers unfamiliar with the route. On a location, short vehicle movements can create fatal crushing hazards. A good journey plan addresses the driver, vehicle, route, load, timing, and receiving location together. It also establishes what to do when the plan becomes unsafe. Meeting a delivery time does not justify continuing with failed brakes, inadequate visibility, or fatigue.

Before departure, confirm the trip is necessary and choose the approved vehicle and route. Check weather, flooding, road closures, overhead clearance, surface conditions, available fuel or charging, and safe stopping places. Obtain precise access instructions and a destination contact; navigation software can direct a vehicle through a restricted entrance or unsuitable road. Know the check-in process and how an overdue journey will be escalated. Where communication coverage is poor, arrange an approved alternative. A journey plan is a working agreement, not permission to use a phone while driving.

### Fitness and vehicle readiness
Fatigue reduces attention, reaction, and judgment. Extended shifts, overnight work, long commutes, poor sleep, medications, and illness can combine. Warning signs include repeated yawning, wandering thoughts, missed turns, lane drift, and difficulty recalling the last miles. Do not wait for a near collision. Stop in a safe place and notify the designated contact to arrange rest, a relief driver, or another transport option. Loud music, an open window, and caffeine do not replace adequate sleep. Do not use an unsafe shoulder or park in an operating equipment route just to meet a check-in time.

Disclose work-relevant impairment through the employer's confidential fitness process. Prescription or nonprescription medicine may affect driving even when legally obtained. Do not diagnose yourself from a package label alone when symptoms or the task raise doubt; seek appropriate guidance. Alcohol, recreational drugs, and medication misuse are incompatible with safe operation. Employer rules and applicable transportation regulations may impose additional duties. This lesson does not invent a universal hours-of-service limit or establish commercial-driver qualification.

Inspect the vehicle using the applicable checklist: tires, lights, windows, mirrors, seat belts, fluid leaks, steering, brakes, and required emergency equipment. Walk around before moving and look beneath or behind where someone or something could be hidden. Confirm occupants have suitable seats and restraints. Secure tools and cargo so they cannot become projectiles during braking. A warning light, damaged restraint, or unsecured heavy item requires evaluation and correction; reporting it after the trip does not protect the current occupants. Remove an unsafe vehicle from service through the designated process.

### Driving with changing conditions
Wear the seat belt before movement and require passengers to do the same. Set navigation and communications before departure. Park safely before reading or composing messages; a dispatch request does not remove distraction. Adjust speed and following distance to visibility, traction, load, and stopping space. The posted limit is not a promise that it is safe in fog, dust, ice, or heavy rain. Leave room for a loaded vehicle's greater stopping demands and changing center of gravity. Avoid sudden steering and braking.

Do not drive through water where depth, current, road support, or escape is uncertain. Turn around safely and report the route problem. On lease roads, expect vehicles at blind curves and crests; follow designated right-of-way and radio arrangements without assuming the other driver heard you. Approach railroad crossings under the vehicle's applicable procedure and never stop on tracks. Plan clearance for trailers and long loads. If visibility collapses, find a safe place outside traffic and equipment paths rather than following taillights into an unknown hazard.

Parking is a task requiring controls. Use designated spaces away from well-control equipment, evacuation paths, fire equipment, and suspended-load areas. Follow the location's engine, ignition-source, and vehicle-orientation rules. Apply parking brakes and use required stabilization or wheel chocks for the specific operation. Exit facing the vehicle with stable handholds and footing; jumping from a cab can turn an otherwise safe journey into an injury. Check the walking route before stepping into traffic.

### Pedestrians and mobile equipment
Separate people and machines with planned lanes, barriers, controlled crossings, speed management, and clear communication. A high-visibility vest improves recognition but does not stop a truck. Stay out of blind spots, articulation areas, counterweight swing zones, and the space between a moving vehicle and a fixed object. Make positive contact with the operator through the agreed system before approaching. Eye contact alone may be impossible through glare, tinted windows, or distance; do not interpret a glance as permission to enter.

Avoid backing where layout can provide forward travel. When backing is necessary, use the site procedure and a designated trained spotter when required. Agree signals, travel route, safe spotter position, and a stop signal before movement. The spotter must remain visible and outside the vehicle's path with an escape route. If visual or communication contact is lost, the operator stops. Cameras, alarms, and sensors supplement the plan; they do not prove the path is clear. Anyone identifying an immediate danger should use the established stop signal.

Forklifts, loaders, telehandlers, cranes, and other mobile machines have different operating limits. A road driver's license or a previous job title does not authorize their use. Powered industrial truck operation under 1910.178 requires appropriate training and evaluation, including practical elements, and employer certification. Equipment type, attachments, terrain, and workplace conditions matter. Construction equipment may be subject to 1926 provisions such as 1926.602.

### Loading and unloading interfaces
A load can shift when restraints are released. Before unloading, the driver and receiving crew establish who controls movement, the exclusion zone, stable ground, capacity, load condition, and the sequence. Look for leaning cargo, damaged packaging, stressed straps, and stored energy. Do not stand where falling pipe, rolling material, a released strap, or moving forks could strike. Use suitable equipment and designated methods for restraints; never cut or release a suspect restraint from its line of fire.

Keep people away from suspended or elevated loads. Do not walk beneath raised forks, ride on a load, or use a pallet as an improvised personnel platform. Do not use your hands or body to steady material where you can be trapped. Rigging, signaling, and lifting qualifications must match the task. If a load has shifted, stop and have the unloading plan revised by qualified personnel. A convenient vacant area is unsuitable if its surface cannot support the equipment or it blocks emergency access.

During inspection, one pipe bundle appears to lean against a restraint. The crew does not simply release it because the loader is waiting. Qualified personnel revise the restraint-removal and stabilization sequence and keep workers out of the potential roll path. A trained spotter is positioned with visibility and escape; when dust hides that spotter, the operator stops until contact is restored. After unloading, the driver checks the empty vehicle and confirms the route is clear before departure. The outcome relies on coordination at each interface, not on one person's confidence.

# ESC08: Tools, guarding, PPE, and walking-working surfaces

## Learning objectives
- Inspect tools and PPE for task suitability, condition, and limitations.
- Recognize guarding, energy-control, and entanglement boundaries.
- Control housekeeping and walking-surface hazards before exposure.
- Apply defect reporting and stop-work decisions in worked situations.

### Select the method before picking up the tool
An ordinary hand tool can become a high-energy hazard when it slips, breaks, or contacts energized equipment. A powered tool adds rotation, flying material, electricity, pressure, heat, and noise. Before work, confirm what the task requires, what energy or material is present, and whether you are authorized to perform it. Choose the right tool, accessory, capacity, and work position. If the proposed method requires a missing guard, an improvised extension, or placing a hand in a pinch point, the method needs to change before work begins.

Read and follow manufacturer instructions together with the employer's task procedure. Check the work area for flammable atmosphere, combustible material, nearby people, overhead work, and hidden services. A grinder can create an ignition source even when the job is called cold maintenance. A battery-powered device can still create sparks and stored-energy hazards. Tools used in classified areas must have the required suitability; an ordinary label or your own tape does not make a tool approved. Obtain the applicable authorization rather than assuming portability means unrestricted use.

### Inspect and control defects
Inspect hand tools for cracked handles, loose heads, mushroomed striking surfaces, damaged grips, worn jaws, and incorrect size. Use a wrench that fits; do not add a pipe to obtain more leverage unless the equipment and procedure specifically permit it. Do not strike a tool that is not designed for striking. Cut away from the body with a suitable controlled blade and secure the work. Keep fingers outside the path that a slipping blade or wrench would travel. Never use a screwdriver as a substitute chisel or pry bar just because it fits a gap.

For power tools, inspect housings, switches, cords, plugs, battery packs, guards, and accessories before use. Match accessories to the manufacturer's specifications, including size and speed rating. A damaged abrasive wheel or incompatible attachment can fail violently. Check pneumatic hoses, fittings, and retention devices; secure connections using the specified system. Do not carry a tool by its hose or cord. Protect leads and hoses from sharp edges, traffic, heat, and pinch points. Use the electrical protection required for the environment and applicable rules.

When a defect affects safety, stop using the equipment and prevent inadvertent reuse. Tag and segregate it through the site's process, tell the responsible person, and obtain a suitable replacement. A tag without physical control may leave the item available to the next shift. Tape over damaged insulation is not an authorized repair by itself. Do not bypass an interlock to test whether the machine still works. Repairs and return to service require authorized personnel and the applicable inspection. Record the observed defect accurately rather than declaring an untested cause.

### Guarding and energy control
Machine guards separate people from points of operation, rotating parts, nip points, belts, chains, and flying material. A guard must protect without introducing a new hazard. Know the safe operating position and keep hands outside hazardous areas. Gloves, loose sleeves, jewelry, and unsecured long hair can be drawn into rotation; follow the task's entanglement controls. A glove may protect against a sharp edge while being unsuitable near rotating machinery. PPE selection must consider both hazards rather than treating more equipment as automatically safer.

An emergency stop is not the same as energy isolation. A machine can restart, coast, fall, release pressure, or move from stored energy after its normal switch is turned off. Clearing jams, changing certain components, cleaning inside guards, or servicing may require the employer's hazardous-energy-control procedure. Only authorized workers perform the isolation and verification for their role. Do not reach through or remove a guard because a machine appears idle. If your role is affected employee, recognize the boundary and contact the authorized person rather than removing another worker's lock.

General-industry guarding provisions include 1910.212 and power-tool rules such as 1910.243. Construction has different provisions including 1926.300. The applicable rule depends on the activity and equipment; not every oilfield task is construction. This lesson provides recognition and decision skills, not authorization to service equipment, perform electrical work, or certify guards. Ask for the task-specific demonstration before using unfamiliar equipment and stop if instructions conflict with the physical arrangement.

### PPE as a fitted, maintained barrier
The hazard assessment determines the needed eye, face, head, hand, foot, body, hearing, and respiratory protection. Select the specified type and size and inspect it before use. Safety glasses and a face shield have different functions; a shield normally supplements the required primary eye protection for flying-particle tasks. Chemical splash, grinding fragments, arc hazards, and optical radiation need different protective selections. Ordinary sunglasses do not substitute for the required eye protection. Do not enter a task relying on an unidentified lens or damaged shell.

Inspect straps, soles, lenses, seams, fasteners, and contamination. A hard hat with damage or unauthorized alterations needs evaluation under the manufacturer's requirements. Chemical-resistant gloves must match the substance and anticipated contact, not simply be thick. Replace damaged PPE through the designated process and store it away from damaging sunlight, chemicals, and heat. Share reusable items only under the approved cleaning and fit arrangements. PPE that prevents a reliable grip or impairs communication requires reassessment, not silent adaptation.

Required respirator use ordinarily brings program requirements such as selection, medical evaluation, fit testing for tight-fitting models, training, and maintenance under 1910.134. A seal check at each use is not a substitute for fit testing. Facial hair or other interference can defeat the seal. Do not borrow a respirator and assume it is suitable. 

### Housekeeping and walking-working surfaces
Keep work areas orderly as the task progresses. Store materials so they cannot fall, roll, obstruct access, or block fire and emergency equipment. Control sharp waste and protrusions; arrange disposal rather than leaving scraps for the end of a shift. Route hoses and cords away from walking paths or use approved protection and crossings. A housekeeping problem often indicates poor work layout, not simply an untidy worker. Plan staging areas and removal intervals so cleanliness can be maintained without entering operating equipment zones.

Inspect the route for oil, mud, ice, loose gravel, holes, changes in elevation, damaged grating, unsupported covers, and inadequate lighting. Stop and protect an unsafe route until it is repaired or a safe alternative established. Do not place an unmarked scrap plate over an opening and assume it is a suitable cover. Report missing rails and damaged stairs. Use handrails, keep sight of the route, and carry items so you can maintain balance. Suitable footwear helps but does not make a contaminated surface safe.

Choose an appropriate ladder or access system instead of a bucket, pallet, or equipment guard. Inspect ladders and set them up under their instructions and the applicable procedure. Maintain secure contact and do not overreach sideways or carry a load that defeats safe climbing. Elevated work requires the relevant fall-protection controls; do not infer that a short task is exempt. A mobile access platform has operator and equipment requirements separate from ladder use. Wet boots, gloves, and poor lighting can combine with an otherwise acceptable access method to create a fall.

### Worked scenario: a grinder and a tight deadline
A technician finds the correct-size grinder has a cracked guard. Another grinder has an intact guard but an accessory of uncertain rating. The crew is waiting to fit a replacement bracket. The technician rejects both as currently presented, tags and separates the damaged tool, and asks for an approved combination. The supervisor confirms the work authorization, nearby combustibles, suitable accessory, secure workholding, and eye, face, hearing, and other specified protection.

The authorized technician completes the work using the intact guard and safe operating position. The damaged grinder remains out of service for repair. A replacement tool alone would not have corrected the trip hazard or ignition-source question, so the whole setup needed review.

### Worked scenario: a stopped conveyor and oily access
At a service yard, Dana sees a small jam on a conveyor and a puddle at its access stair. A coworker offers to hold the stop button while Dana removes the obstruction. Dana refuses to reach inside because stopping does not establish isolation. She prevents access to the affected area and contacts the authorized maintenance worker. The worker follows the specific energy-control procedure, including stored-energy checks and verification before exposure.

Separately, the supervisor arranges safe leak control and cleanup and checks whether the stair surface needs repair. Dana does not spread absorbent into a moving mechanism or step through the puddle to reach a better handhold. The crew records both hazards and verifies the guard, work area, and access are restored before authorized restart. Treating the jam and the walking route as separate controls prevents one correction from concealing another unresolved risk.

# ESC09: Emergency action, first aid, fire, and spill boundaries

## Learning objectives
- Use the site emergency plan to alarm, evacuate, account, and communicate.
- Distinguish trained first aid from unsafe rescue and unqualified medical treatment.
- Recognize bloodborne-exposure precautions and prompt reporting.
- Determine when fire or spill conditions require withdrawal and specialist response.

### Learn the plan before an alarm
An emergency is a poor time to discover the address, radio channel, or assembly location. At orientation, learn how the site reports emergencies, what alarms mean, where primary and alternate exits lead, and how accountability works. Identify who contacts public responders and who meets them at a safe access point. Know the location's name, entrance, coordinates or address, and any access restrictions. A remote pad may need a gate opened and a guide positioned far from the hazard; provide this information through the plan rather than assuming a dispatcher recognizes the lease name.

The emergency action plan identifies evacuation arrangements and any designated roles such as shutdown, rescue, medical response, or accounting for personnel. Do not assign yourself a special role during an incident. General-industry 1910.38 applies when another standard requires an emergency action plan; construction includes 1926.35. Not every provision applies identically to every site. Regardless of classification, understand the actual site instructions before work. A plan on a wall is not sufficient if contract crews cannot recognize the alarm or find a safe route.

### Alarm, move, and account
When you discover an emergency, protect yourself first, alert others using the established signal, and notify the emergency contact. Give location, what happened, hazards observed, number of people affected if known, and a safe approach direction. Avoid unsupported claims such as declaring an atmosphere safe because nobody smells gas. Follow the evacuation or shelter instructions for the event. A gas release, lightning, severe weather, or security threat may require different protective actions. Do not automatically use the nearest enclosed room for every hazard.

Move by a safe route away from the hazard and follow the designated assembly process. Consider wind indicators and site instructions for releases, but do not cross a visible plume to reach a preferred muster sign. If the primary route is blocked, use the identified alternate and report your location. Leave possessions behind when retrieving them delays escape. Do not drive a vehicle through a release or bring an ignition source into a suspect atmosphere. Keep access clear for responders and do not crowd the boundary to watch.

At assembly, report to the designated accountability person. Tell them if someone was last seen elsewhere, including the exact location and time, without claiming the person is definitely trapped. Do not leave without being accounted for and released under the plan. A missing person triggers the planned response; it does not authorize spontaneous search teams. Reentry requires the authorized all-clear. An alarm stopping, a supervisor arriving, or equipment becoming quiet does not independently prove the hazard has ended.

### Medical first aid and rescue limits
Assess scene safety before approaching an injured person. Traffic, electricity, pressure, unstable equipment, toxic gas, fire, and confined spaces can create additional casualties. Call for trained help and use only the assistance within your training and authorization. Know where the first-aid equipment, AED, emergency shower, and eyewash are located, and keep access open.

For a suspected life-threatening event, activate emergency services promptly. Follow dispatcher instructions while staying within a safe scene and your ability. Do not move an injured person unnecessarily where movement could worsen injury, unless immediate danger requires a trained emergency action. Do not give food, drink, medication, or a ride to an impaired person as an improvised substitute for the medical response. Tell responders what happened, known exposures, and care already provided. Do not publicly speculate about a person's diagnosis.

A collapsed worker inside a tank, pit, vessel, or suspected hazardous atmosphere needs a planned rescue response. Do not enter, lean into the opening, or follow a second person who attempts rescue. A harness and a rope alone do not create a rescue system. Alert the designated rescue service and isolate the area from a safe position. Only trained, equipped, authorized responders use the applicable rescue plan. Many fatal events involve would-be rescuers; urgency increases the need to preserve the entry boundary.

### Bloodborne pathogens
Treat blood and designated potentially infectious materials as requiring appropriate precautions. Exposure can occur through a needlestick, cut, splash to eyes or mouth, or contact with nonintact skin. Use the employer's response arrangements and trained personnel. Appropriate gloves and other protection help prevent contact, but gloves can be punctured or transfer contamination during removal. Do not handle broken contaminated glass or sharps by hand, compress waste bags, or put a used needle into ordinary trash.

If exposure occurs, perform the relevant immediate washing or flushing, notify the designated contact promptly, and obtain urgent confidential medical evaluation through the employer's procedure. Do not use bleach on skin, squeeze a wound aggressively, or delay reporting until symptoms appear. Record the route and circumstances accurately while protecting personal information. Under 1910.1030, occupational exposure determinations drive requirements such as an exposure-control plan, training, hepatitis B vaccination offers, and post-exposure evaluation. This is not a claim that every employee has the same occupational exposure classification.

### Fire prevention and extinguisher decisions
Keep ignition sources and combustibles controlled through the work plan, including smoking locations, hot-work authorization, electrical suitability, waste storage, and bonding or grounding where required. Do not defeat a fire door, obstruct an extinguisher, or use fire equipment as ordinary storage space. Learn whether the employer requires full evacuation or permits designated workers to use extinguishers. Having an extinguisher nearby does not create a duty for every worker to fight a fire.

Only attempt an incipient-stage fire response if the employer permits it, you have the required training, the correct extinguisher is available, the alarm and emergency notification are made, and a reliable escape path remains behind you. If conditions grow, smoke impairs visibility, the fuel is unknown, a pressurized system is involved, or you doubt control, withdraw. Ordinary extinguishers cannot make an uncontrolled gas release safe. Do not extinguish a gas flame and leave an unisolated escaping fuel source without the incident command's specialist direction.

Extinguisher classes correspond to the fuel and hazard; water can be dangerous on energized electrical equipment and incompatible materials. Check the label and site training rather than relying on cylinder color. Inspection, maintenance, selection, and training provisions under 1910.157 depend on the employer's fire-response arrangements. Construction fire-protection provisions differ. Never delay evacuation to search for an extinguisher.

### Spills and environmental interfaces
An incidental release is one that trained personnel can safely control using the available resources without a significant emergency hazard. A spreading unknown substance, dangerous vapor, fire potential, uncontrolled source, or threat beyond local capability demands withdrawal, isolation, and the emergency plan. Do not classify a spill solely by gallons. A small release can create a dangerous atmosphere; a familiar product may still be unsafe in a poorly ventilated area.

Awareness-level workers recognize, protect themselves, notify, and keep others clear. Do not plug a leak, enter a vapor area, place absorbent, or operate a valve unless trained, equipped, authorized, and safe under the actual response procedure. From a safe location, report product identity if known, migration direction, drains, soil or water threatened, and ignition sources. Authorized environmental personnel determine regulatory notifications and disposal. Do not wash a spill into a drain or conceal it under soil.

### Worked scenario: a collapsed tank worker
During a delivery, Elena sees a person motionless at a tank access opening. A coworker starts climbing toward them with a rope. Elena calls the established emergency contact, gives the tank identification and location, and warns the coworker to stop. She recognizes a possible atmospheric emergency, keeps others away, and moves to the safe reporting position identified by the site plan. She does not put her head through the opening to check breathing.

The trained rescue service is activated and receives the last known location and possible hazards. At muster, Elena reports both the casualty and the coworker's location so accountability is accurate. Nobody reenters because the alarm becomes quiet. Rescue and any medical assistance proceed under qualified direction. The correct outcome for an awareness worker is effective notification and prevention of additional victims, not an improvised entry.

### Worked scenario: a leak, injured hand, and approaching traffic
A forklift strikes a chemical container near a loading area. There is a sharp odor, liquid moving toward a drain, and a worker with a bleeding hand. The receiver stops nearby traffic from a safe position, sounds the alarm, and reports the container identification and drain location without walking through the liquid to read a label. Workers withdraw by the safe route, keeping clear of vapors and ignition sources.

A trained first aider attends the injured person only after reaching a safe area, using the appropriate blood-contact precautions. Any splash or blood exposure is reported for prompt evaluation. The receiver does not cover the drain personally while the atmosphere and chemical remain uncertain. Authorized responders identify the substance, control the source and migration, and arrange cleanup and environmental notifications. The event is closed only after medical follow-up, safe reentry authorization, and review of why vehicle separation failed.

### Waste prevention and fire classifications
Reduce waste at the planning stage by ordering suitable quantities, preventing contamination and choosing approved reusable equipment where compatible. Keep identified waste streams separate under the site plan; never mix materials merely to reduce container count. Mark containers, use the designated storage and disposal routes, and stop if identity or compatibility is uncertain. A drain is an environmental pathway, not a disposal instruction. Report a release promptly so the designated personnel can meet actual containment, cleanup and external-notification duties.

At recognition level, Class A concerns ordinary combustibles such as wood/paper; B concerns flammable liquids and related fuels; C indicates energized electrical involvement; D concerns combustible metals; and K concerns cooking oils/fats. Read the approved extinguisher markings and actual plan rather than choosing from color or a remembered label. The categories help explain why the wrong agent can worsen an event; they do not assign an untrained worker firefighting duties or establish compatibility with every chemical. Evacuate when the plan requires it, the conditions exceed your trained role, or escape could be lost.

# ESC10: Organizational safety, personal readiness, and integrated jobsite decisions

## Learning objectives
- Apply employer policies without confusing policy with universal law.
- Recognize impairment, violence, stress, and short-service-worker vulnerabilities.
- Report incidents and near misses with factual, useful information.
- Integrate planning, boundaries, emergency decisions, and corrective-action verification in complete jobsite scenarios.

### Culture becomes visible in work decisions
A safety program works when workers can identify a problem, obtain help, and pause unsafe work without being pressured to hide it. Written rules matter, but scheduling, supervision, equipment availability, and responses to concerns show whether the rules can be followed. Before starting at a new employer or location, learn reporting channels, stop-work expectations, prohibited conduct, fitness requirements, emergency arrangements, and contractor coordination. Ask how to report if the immediate supervisor is involved in the concern. Do not let a missing signature or an unfamiliar reporting application delay protection from an immediate hazard.

Company requirements, client rules, manufacturer instructions, and law are different sources of obligations. An employer may set a stricter phone rule, vehicle restriction, or drug policy than a general regulation. Explain it as a policy unless an applicable legal requirement has been established. Federal OSHA general-industry and construction rules depend on the work; state-plan requirements and transportation or environmental rules may also apply. 

### Drugs, alcohol, and work readiness
Arrive capable of performing the assigned task safely. Alcohol, illicit drugs, misuse of medication, and some properly used medications can impair coordination, attention, and judgment. Fatigue, illness, and emotional distress can create similar signs, so workers should report observed behavior rather than diagnose a coworker as intoxicated. If a person cannot safely operate equipment, stop the exposure and contact the designated supervisor or fitness resource. Do not hand them vehicle keys or tell them to drive home as a solution.

Learn the employer's possession, use, testing, disclosure, and return-to-duty policies and the procedures that apply to your role. Requirements can vary. For work-relevant medication concerns, use the confidential occupational-health process and provide required functional restrictions. Do not stop prescribed treatment without medical advice or share medication with a coworker. A lawful prescription does not guarantee safe operation for every task.

A concern about fitness should produce an immediate safe work arrangement and a fair, confidential assessment. Avoid public accusations, searches, threats, or amateur sobriety tests. Document what you directly observed, such as stumbling at a ladder, delayed responses, or an admission of inadequate sleep, and when it occurred. Follow the designated process for transportation and supervision. Privacy does not mean concealing a serious immediate hazard; share necessary information with the appropriate people rather than the entire crew.

### Respectful work and violence prevention
Harassment, bullying, discriminatory behavior, threats, and intimidation undermine communication and can escalate. Treat questions and stop-work concerns respectfully, including from contractors, new workers, and workers communicating in another language. Report threatening messages, stalking, physical aggression, or coercion through the established channel. If there is immediate danger, move to safety and activate emergency help. Do not attempt to settle an escalating confrontation by blocking exits, touching the person, or matching their aggression.

For an active assailant or active-shooter event, follow the site emergency plan and responder directions. If a safe escape is available, leave the danger area, warn others when safe, and contact emergency services from safety. If escape is unsafe, use a suitable secure hiding location under the plan, reduce visibility and sound, and avoid exposing yourself to investigate. Physical resistance is a last-resort survival action when facing imminent harm, not a workplace intervention assignment. Keep hands visible when encountering police and do not run toward responders carrying objects that may be misinterpreted.

Prevention includes controlled access, visitor management, clear reporting, supportive supervision, and a plan for credible threats. Do not dismiss threats as jokes because you know the speaker. Equally, avoid labeling a distressed person as violent merely because they seek mental-health support. Report specific conduct and immediate risk, not stereotypes. The responsible employer and appropriate professionals assess and respond. Save relevant messages through the approved process without circulating them as entertainment.

### Stress, mental wellbeing, and short-service workers
Stress can reduce concentration, sleep, patience, and willingness to speak up. Long shifts, uncertainty, isolation, family concerns, and repeated exposure to difficult events can accumulate. Check in respectfully when a coworker's behavior changes. Ask whether they need support, help them reach the employer's assistance resources, and adjust immediate safety-critical duties through the supervisor where needed. Do not promise secrecy when there is an immediate risk of harm. If someone expresses an immediate intent to harm themselves or others, activate emergency assistance and remain engaged only as safely appropriate; do not leave the situation to an untrained coworker.

Wellbeing support complements changes to work. Repeated overtime, poor staffing, unclear instructions, and harassment cannot be corrected solely by telling people to be resilient. Report patterns so the organization can address them. Use recovery time and available support without treating symptoms as a personal failure. Supervisors should make expectations clear and give workers realistic routes to raise concerns. 

A short-service worker may be new to the industry, employer, task, or location despite extensive experience elsewhere. Identify the employer's mentoring and supervision arrangements. A helmet sticker or roster designation is a communication aid, not proof of competence. The mentor should demonstrate the task, explain hazards, observe performance, and confirm understanding through teach-back. Provide language support where needed. Do not assign a new worker alone to recognize unfamiliar alarms, operate equipment, or manage permits simply because orientation is complete.

The learner also has an active role: identify limits, ask before changing methods, and report uncertainty. If the mentor is unavailable, pause tasks requiring that supervision and obtain an authorized replacement. A successful short-service period depends on demonstrated capability under the employer's process, not only elapsed days. No fixed universal mentoring duration is invented here. Every worker remains entitled and expected to raise a hazard after the introductory period ends.

### Reporting, investigation, and corrective action
Report injuries, illnesses, exposures, property damage, releases, and near misses promptly using the established channel. A near miss is useful evidence that a person reached danger without the worst outcome. First protect people and activate emergency arrangements, then preserve the scene where safe and as instructed. Do not move damaged equipment to recreate the event, delete messages, or alter a guard to make the setup look compliant. Obtain consent and follow privacy rules for photographs; never delay aid to take a picture.

Separate observation from interpretation. Record time, location, task, people present, equipment identification, conditions, what changed, existing barriers, immediate actions, and witnesses. Write that a truck moved while a worker was in the marked route rather than asserting the driver was careless without evidence. A worker's report is not the same as the employer's OSHA recordkeeping or external-reporting determination. Responsible personnel evaluate those obligations; workers should not suppress an event because they assume it is not recordable.

Effective investigations look at equipment, procedures, training, layout, communication, staffing, and organizational pressures alongside individual actions. Corrective actions need an owner, due date, interim protection, and verification of effectiveness. Telling everyone to be careful is weak if the path still crosses reversing vehicles. Verify in the actual work that the revised layout or control functions and has not created another hazard. Share applicable learning across shifts and contractors and update the task plan before repetition. Closing an electronic item without checking the field condition does not establish control.

The supervisor first stops unsafe departures and separates the verbal conflict without confrontation. A relief transport arrangement is requested. Reporting fatigue is handled as useful safety information, and the disrespect concern goes through the proper channel.

The liquid is unfamiliar and moving toward a drain, so the crew activates the site response and withdraws rather than assigning the newest worker to place absorbent. From a safe position, they report container location, visible markings, migration, and nearby ignition sources. Accountability confirms everyone has moved clear. Authorized responders control the release and determine cleanup and environmental notifications. Only designated personnel approve reentry.

After immediate protection, the incident report connects the leaking container, overtime, vehicle plans, and communication breakdown without claiming they share one proven cause. Review identifies damaged storage protection and an unrealistic end-of-shift transport assumption. Actions include a protected storage arrangement, an earlier journey-fitness decision, and supervisor follow-up on respectful reporting. Each action has an owner and verification date. The job is not declared safely complete merely because the spill disappears; the crew verifies the area, restores equipment status, and completes a safe journey under the revised plan.

### Prohibited items and searches: understand the actual policy
At induction, obtain the employer and host site's rules for weapons, alcohol, drugs, unauthorized ignition sources, cameras, and other restricted items. Restrictions vary by location and applicable law; do not assume an item allowed at one facility is allowed at another. Ask how restricted items must be declared, stored, or left outside the site before bringing them through access control. Learn the written search policy, who may conduct an authorized search, what process applies, and where questions or complaints go. This lesson does not assert a universal employer right to search every person or possession, require workers to surrender legal rights, or authorize peers to search each other. If an access issue arises, stay out of the operating area and obtain the designated security or management decision without confrontation. Where a weapon or threat creates immediate danger, use emergency protective actions rather than attempting a search or seizure.

# Part II — rig-specific instruction

## R01 — Arrive ready and understand the operation

**Objectives:** Identify the operating phase and responsible contact; obtain the actual site briefing; distinguish orientation from task qualification; manage short-service and human-performance risks.

A drilling location changes as equipment arrives, the rig is assembled, drilling proceeds, pipe is moved, casing is run, maintenance occurs, and the rig is dismantled. The same walkway can be open during one phase and inside a lifting or pressure boundary during another. Before entering, check in through the designated point, explain your purpose, and receive the current instructions. An invitation to the site is not permission to wander onto the rig floor. Ask who controls the area, where you may walk or wait, which operations are underway, and how you will be accounted for in an emergency.

During rig-up, equipment is unloaded, positioned, and assembled. Changing traffic, suspended components, temporary access, missing final guards, and incomplete systems make this different from an established operating rig. During drilling, rotating machinery, fluid systems, and pipe movement create interacting hazards. During tripping, pipe is withdrawn or returned and handling paths may change repeatedly. Casing activity introduces its own handling equipment and crews. Maintenance and rig-down introduce isolation, dismantling, and stored-energy concerns. A new worker needs to recognize these phases to ask the right questions, not to operate their equipment.

Before a task, identify the job steps, hazards, controls, responsible people, stop signal, and restart authority in the pre-job discussion. Explain the task back if communication is unclear. When operator and contractor instructions appear inconsistent, hold the affected work and obtain a coordinated instruction that satisfies applicable requirements; do not choose whichever permits faster work. A stricter limit can apply, but a worker cannot combine incompatible procedures into an improvised method.

A short-service worker may have a designated mentor and visible identification under the employer's program. The marker communicates a need for support, not inferiority or permission to take risks. Ask the mentor before a changed or unfamiliar activity. Experienced people can also make errors. Good leaders invite questions, verify critical steps, and fix conditions that make mistakes likely instead of relying on blame after an event.

Four common reasoning traps deserve attention. Confirmation bias means noticing evidence that supports the planned action while overlooking contradictions. Similarity bias means treating a new task as identical to a familiar one. Frequency bias means assuming a hazard that has not occurred recently will not occur now. Availability bias means over-weighting a vivid recent event while neglecting other credible hazards. Counter these by checking the current conditions and asking what evidence would show the plan is wrong.

**Worked case:** A new contractor recognizes the same rig model used at a previous site and heads toward the usual stairs. The stairs are inside a temporary rig-up boundary. The contractor stops, checks with the area controller, and uses the approved route. Familiar equipment did not establish a familiar operating condition.

**Practice and explained response:** A mentor says, “We have never had trouble with this shortcut.” Identify the assumption. It treats past absence of harm as proof of current safety. Verify the actual route and controls; request correction of the unsafe shortcut rather than copying it.

## R02 — Stay outside motion and stored-energy paths

**Objectives:** Recognize line-of-fire and pinch exposures around tubulars and machinery; use exclusion and communication arrangements; identify hand and pressure hazards without unauthorized intervention.

Tubulars can roll, slide, swing, or fall. A worker between a moving tubular and a fixed structure can be crushed even when the load moves slowly. The catwalk and V-door are handling paths, not convenient shortcuts. Stay outside the designated area while pipe or equipment moves, and do not walk beneath suspended loads. A signal from another pedestrian does not substitute for the operator's controlled movement and the designated signal arrangement.

Hands are particularly vulnerable at contact points: between pipe and rack, around slips and tongs, in rotating equipment, and while aligning components. Gloves may reduce some surface injuries but do not stop crushing or entanglement. Use the approved hands-free aids and methods only after training; do not grab a rolling tubular or a suspended load to rescue the placement. If a load or tool is unstable, warn and stop through the established signal from a safe position.

Rig equipment can contain electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, and gravitational energy. A stationary machine is not necessarily isolated. A suspended component may descend; pressure can remain after a pump stops; automatic equipment may restart. Maintenance requires the authorized energy-control procedure, isolation of all relevant sources, control of stored energy, and verification by qualified personnel before exposure. An affected worker respects locks and barriers and never removes another person's lock to help the schedule.

High-pressure connections can release fluid or move violently if they fail. Do not stand astride a pressurized line, look closely for a leak with your hand, or tighten a suspect connection under pressure. Keep outside the designated boundary and notify the responsible crew. Small-looking leaks can be serious; any suspected injection injury needs urgent medical assessment rather than dismissal as a minor puncture. The work team must follow its isolation and pressure-verification procedure; this orientation does not teach opening a pressure system.

Noise, poor visibility, and multiple crews can make communication unreliable. Know the stop signal before movement starts. If the signal is lost or instructions conflict, the operation must be brought to the safe hold defined by the responsible operator and plan. Never substitute frantic gestures or guess which person has control. A good briefing identifies the signaler, who may give an emergency stop, and how communication failure is handled.

**Worked case:** A pipe begins to move from a rack and a worker reaches to steady it. The safer response is to remain outside its path, warn others, and use the established stop communication. Catching the pipe converts an equipment issue into a crushing exposure.

**Practice and explained response:** A pump is silent and a line appears relaxed. Is that enough to disconnect it? No. Appearance is not verified isolation or absence of stored pressure. Only the authorized procedure and responsible personnel establish the safe condition.

## R03 — Access, work-at-height interfaces, and dropped objects

**Objectives:** Inspect the intended walking route; recognize ladder and edge hazards; distinguish prevention, restraint, and arrest; maintain dropped-object boundaries.

Use installed, approved access. Stairs and handrails must be secure, and the route must be free of obstacles, spills, and temporary holes. Keep a hand available for the rail; arrange materials so carrying them does not prevent safe access or visibility. Report missing steps, loose rails, damaged covers, or slippery surfaces and avoid the route until controlled. Do not step on pipes, braces, or machinery as substitutes for access equipment.

A ladder must be appropriate, inspected, stable, and used within its limits. For a non-self-supporting ladder, the familiar four-to-one setup means the foot is approximately one-quarter of the working length away from the top support. It is a setup relationship, not permission to climb any ladder or use it on unstable ground. Follow the current ladder instructions and applicable rules for securing, access above the landing, load, and electrical hazards. A self-supporting stepladder is not automatically safe when leaned closed against a wall. Damaged equipment must be removed from use through the site's process.

Fall prevention keeps a person from falling, for example with a suitable guardrail. Restraint limits movement so the person cannot reach the fall edge. Arrest stops a fall after it begins and requires compatible equipment, suitable anchorage, clearance, and rescue arrangements. A harness by itself is not a complete system. The applicable height threshold depends on the work, jurisdiction, and company requirements; a worker must not transfer a number from another activity and assume it applies. Work at height requires its own training and authorization.

Dropped objects include loose tools, bolts, components, bottles, and material left on a structure. Before authorized overhead work, the responsible team establishes the exclusion area and approved securing arrangements. Account for what goes up and comes down. Do not attach an improvised tether to a tool or body point without approved compatibility, and do not enter the drop zone because the overhead worker appears to be pausing. A small object can injure someone well below the work point.

If an access path intersects overhead work, hold the crossing and have the controlling personnel coordinate a safe route or pause. Do not remove a barricade yourself. Personnel lifting is a separate planned operation using approved equipment and qualified personnel; riding a material hook, load, or improvised basket is not an access method.

**Worked case:** A worker drops a wrench onto a protected lower deck. Nobody is hit. The crew reports the near miss, maintains the exclusion, accounts for other tools, and reviews the securing method before restart. Simply retrieving the wrench would leave the failure mechanism unchanged.

**Practice and explained response:** A harness is available but no rescue arrangement has been discussed. The missing rescue provision must be resolved before work relying on fall arrest. Wearing the harness does not make the task ready.

## R04 — Land operations: excavations, pits, and ponds

**Objectives:** Distinguish worker and competent-person duties; recognize cave-in, utility, atmosphere, and water hazards; refuse unsafe entry and improvised rescue.

An excavation can collapse without giving a worker useful time to escape. The competent person identifies hazards and has authority to correct them; that role requires actual capability and designation, not merely a senior job title. Workers follow the established protective arrangements and report changes. They do not select a protective system by looking at the soil from the edge or decide that a quick entry is too short to matter.

Before digging, the responsible organization must identify underground installations through the applicable locating and notification process and control them as the work approaches. A remembered route or an old drawing is not sufficient verification. Notification periods and ticket validity depend on the jurisdiction and system; obtain the actual current requirement rather than inventing one universal waiting time. Overhead utilities, nearby structures, vehicle loads, water, and adjacent operations also affect the plan.

Protective systems may slope or bench soil, support it through shoring, or shield workers through a suitable trench box. These approaches have different design and use conditions. A box does not make every part of an excavation safe, and a worker outside its protected space may still be exposed. Required inspection, access, atmosphere evaluation, water control, and separation from loads remain necessary. Rain, vibration, water seepage, cracks, or other changed conditions require the competent person's reassessment. Do not enter while the needed protection or access is missing.

Pits and ponds on energy sites may contain drilling fluids, produced water, wastewater, or other liquids. Hazards can include drowning, steep or slippery banks, unstable edges, vehicle entry, hazardous atmospheres, chemicals, and an unseen bottom. A lined pond can be difficult to climb out of. Barriers and designated access keep people and vehicles away; do not cross them to retrieve a dropped item, take a shortcut, or get a closer photograph. A personal flotation device, where required by the assessment, must be appropriate and fitted, but does not eliminate atmospheric or chemical hazards.

If someone falls in or a trench collapses, activate the emergency plan from safety. Do not enter or send another untrained person into the same hazard. Specialized rescue requires equipment and competence suited to the conditions. A ladder or rope nearby is not a complete rescue system. Provide the last known location and observed conditions to responders without approaching an unstable edge.

**Worked case:** Rain ends and a crew wants to finish a trench task before dark. The earlier inspection does not establish current conditions. The work remains on hold for the competent person's reassessment and required controls.

**Practice and explained response:** A worker says a lined pond is shallow and offers to climb down for a tool. Refuse the entry and report the retrieval need. Depth is unverified and does not remove slipping, atmosphere, or escape hazards.

## R05 — Protect health and the environment during rig work

**Objectives:** Recognize task-specific exposure mechanisms; distinguish containment and waste controls from cleanup authorization; use current labels and reporting arrangements.

Drilling work can expose people to dust during handling of dry additives, mist from fluid systems, noise, diesel exhaust, chemicals, and contaminated surfaces. Exposure is not always visible, and a familiar product may present a different hazard when sprayed, heated, disturbed, or used in an enclosed area. Read the current label and safety data sheet and use the actual task assessment. Respiratory protection, if needed, comes from the employer's program, not an improvised selection of a mask.

Dust control should address the release source and handling method. Avoid defeating enclosed handling or ventilation to work faster. Do not dry sweep or blow down a contaminated area without an assessed method; that can move settled material into breathing air. Report damaged seals, dust escape, ineffective ventilation, and unusual exposure conditions. Noise control and hearing protection likewise require actual assessment and fitting; the ability to hear someone shout is not a reliable exposure measurement.

Chemical information must travel with the material. An unlabeled container cannot be identified by its color or storage location. Stop its use and have the responsible person establish identity and correct labeling. Hazard communication labels and transport placards serve different purposes; neither substitutes for the task's safety data and handling instruction. Do not reclassify a waste or select shipping labels merely from this orientation course.

Prevent waste by using only the needed quantity and keeping incompatible or separately managed streams apart. Place waste in the designated identified container, maintain required closure and containment, and report leaks or full containers before overflow. Do not mix unknown liquid into another waste stream to simplify disposal. Hazardous-waste handling and transport require the assigned training and controls; ordinary housekeeping authority is not hazardous-release response authority.

For a spill, recognize the material if known without approaching danger, warn and restrict access from safety, notify the responsible site contact, and follow the response plan. Only perform a containment or cleanup action when trained, authorized, equipped, and within the defined conditions. A small volume can still create a serious toxic, fire, slip, or environmental hazard. Report releases even if someone believes they have already cleaned them up; observations and corrective actions must be preserved.

**Worked case:** A bag-handling enclosure is removed because it slows the operation. A worker sees dust leaving the task and stops the affected work for correction. The existence of disposable masks in a cabinet does not authorize bypassing the engineering control.

**Practice and explained response:** A drum contains an unknown liquid and its label is damaged. Keep it out of use, preserve the area according to the plan, and request identification and proper handling. Do not smell, mix, or pour it away to determine what it is.

## R06 — Coordinate emergencies, handovers, and restart

**Objectives:** Recognize rig emergency signals and role boundaries; communicate reliable handovers; maintain accountability and obtain authorized restart.

Learn the actual site's fire, gas, well-control, evacuation, and all-clear signals before duty. On an offshore facility also learn abandon-facility and person-overboard arrangements. A sound heard at a previous rig is not a universal code. Know the safe areas, primary and alternate routes, emergency communication method, and who accounts for your crew. Check how a change of shift or a visiting service crew is included.

Well-control equipment and procedures belong to specifically trained personnel. An orientation learner's role is to recognize the warning, notify the responsible person, and follow the assigned protective action, not operate controls or diagnose the well. Do not use a course example to decide how to shut in a well. Keep access available for the designated response team and remain outside restricted areas.

Fire planning begins with prevention: control fuel, heat and ignition sources, oxygen-related conditions, and the sustaining reaction through approved arrangements. The four-part fire model helps explain why extinguishing methods differ; it does not make every extinguisher suitable for every fire. The included core chapter teaches fire classes, extinguisher labels, and the boundary between evacuation and trained incipient-fire response. Never allow an attempted response to block your escape route or exceed your authorization.

Security emergencies require a different response from a gas release. Report credible threats through the site's emergency arrangements, avoid confrontation, and move away from immediate danger when a safe escape is available. If escape is not possible, use the emergency plan's secure shelter arrangements, contact emergency services when safe, and follow responders' instructions. Do not move toward a threat to investigate or film it. This orientation does not provide tactical intervention training.

A handover communicates the actual state, not just tasks accomplished. Include open permits, isolations, inhibited or faulty equipment, temporary boundaries, changed routes, ongoing simultaneous work, weather constraints, and pending corrective actions. Identify the responsible person and the limits of any temporary measure. The incoming worker should repeat back critical information and inspect the authorized work area as required. Signing a log without understanding a changed condition is not a reliable transfer.

After an incident or interruption, restart follows the designated authority and verification. An all-clear for occupying an area does not necessarily authorize a suspended lift, maintenance task, or drilling activity. Resolve the specific task's controls and communicate them to every affected crew. Record facts and uncertainties accurately so the next shift does not inherit a false impression of readiness.

**Worked case:** A repair team leaves at shift change with one guard removed and the machine isolated. The outgoing lead identifies the unfinished condition and isolation in the handover; the incoming team does not start the machine because the repair team has gone home. Restoration follows the authorized procedure.

**Practice and explained response:** A gas all-clear is issued, but your lifting permit was suspended. Return only within the all-clear instructions; do not restart the lift until its separate authorization and controls are restored.

# Part III — offshore instruction

## O01 — Preparing for an offshore assignment

### Objectives

Prepare a travel and personal-readiness plan; distinguish identification, access permission and task qualification; resolve fatigue, medication and extended-stay concerns before boarding.

An offshore assignment starts before departure. A worker who arrives at the shorebase without the correct documents, too fatigued to follow a briefing, or without an adequate supply of necessary medication has a work-planning problem, not merely a personal inconvenience. Distance, transport schedules and weather can delay access to ordinary support. Preparation makes those limitations visible while they can still be addressed.

Confirm the destination, reporting time, dispatcher contact, transport mode and baggage arrangements with your employer. Ask which identification, operator-accepted orientation, medical clearance and task qualifications are required for your particular assignment. These are different records. A general safety course does not establish medical fitness or authorization to operate equipment. A maritime access credential does not prove completion of a site induction. Check names, dates and the method used to verify records before travel rather than asking the dispatcher to resolve everything at boarding.

Plan the journey so you can arrive rested. Driving through the night and then starting a demanding shift makes the journey part of the exposure. Tell the responsible supervisor when delays or an extended previous shift undermine the plan. Stop safely before consulting navigation or communicating a route change. Do not try to recover lost time by speeding. The useful question is whether the revised journey and work schedule remain safe, not whether the original arrival time can still be reached.

Use the employer's confidential process for medication and fitness questions. Prescription ownership, correct use and fitness for a particular duty are separate issues. An over-the-counter product can affect alertness; a prescribed product can also require review. Keep medication in its identified container and follow the relevant travel and company requirements. Do not take another person's medication or stop a prescribed treatment merely to pass a work check. Consult the appropriate healthcare professional and employer fitness contact about safe arrangements. Share necessary fitness restrictions through the designated channel rather than broadcasting medical details to coworkers.

Prepare for a delayed return. Arrange essential home responsibilities, sufficient permitted personal supplies and a way to contact the appropriate family or support person. Confirm how the employer handles weather extensions and medication shortages. A backup plan is not a promise that a worker will remain offshore indefinitely. If an essential need cannot be met, report it early so the responsible people can change the travel or assignment plan.

Check prohibited-item rules before packing. Weapons, ammunition, alcohol, illegal drugs, ignition sources and other restricted articles may be controlled by the facility or carrier. Never assume an item allowed in a private vehicle is permitted in baggage or on a helicopter. Declare uncertainty before entering a controlled area. Security staff, not a coworker's informal assurance, resolve an access issue.

Her orientation record does not remove either limitation. The error would be concealing the problem to avoid missing transport; that removes the opportunity to arrange a safe alternative.

### Practice and explained response

The learner should name the responsible contacts and state that a travel booking alone resolves none of these decisions. If the response is only “get more sleep,” revisit how travel, duty and delayed return interact.

## O02 — Shorebase arrival, security and accountability

### Objectives

Use the authorized access process; explain maritime security levels; correct manifest discrepancies; maintain accountability from the shorebase to the offshore destination.

A shorebase combines passenger movements, freight handling, traffic and controlled access. Arriving at the right address does not mean every route through the property is open to you. Park where directed, secure the vehicle, report to the named dispatcher or security contact, and stay within the permitted waiting area. Read the local instructions rather than following a group that may have a different destination or permission.

For covered maritime secure areas, a Transportation Worker Identification Credential concerns access screening. The facility or vessel controls actual entry. Where an authorized escort arrangement is available, follow that arrangement; do not assume an escort must be offered. A person with a valid card can still be denied a particular area or directed to remain with an escort. Never lend an access card, follow closely through a controlled door, or treat a familiar uniform as proof of authorization. Resolve missing or expired documentation through security before entry.

MARSEC is a maritime-security alert framework. Level 1 maintains baseline protective measures. Level 2 adds protection for heightened risk. Level 3 applies further specific measures when an incident is probable, imminent or has occurred. Workers do not select the level themselves. Follow the current facility instructions, which can change screening, waiting locations or movement permissions. Do not photograph or publicize sensitive access arrangements to explain a delay. Report a suspicious item or behavior to the designated security contact without investigating it yourself.

The passenger manifest is an accountability record. Confirm your identity, destination and any required passenger or baggage information through the carrier's process. Do not understate weights, move baggage between people to conceal a limit, or change seats when seating is assigned for loading or emergency arrangements. A spelling discrepancy is worth resolving before departure because an emergency team may depend on that record. Notify dispatch if you miss a movement or transfer to another authorized transport; an unchanged manifest can leave rescuers looking in the wrong place.

Attend the transport briefing even if you have travelled before. The vessel, aircraft, exits, flotation equipment, route and conditions may differ. Tell the crew before departure if you cannot hear, understand or physically perform the required passenger action. Ask for a demonstration or other understandable instruction. Nodding through an unintelligible briefing does not make a passenger prepared.

Stay clear of freight operations while waiting. Suspended loads, reversing equipment and mooring activity can cross what looks like an empty walkway. Use designated pedestrian routes, respect barriers and obtain positive direction when the route changes. Do not move a barrier or duck beneath a load to reach a boarding group. Report the conflict so the movements can be separated.

## O03 — Helicopter travel and helideck awareness

### Objectives

Identify passenger hazards around a helicopter; use the crew's briefing and loading instructions; distinguish ordinary passenger preparation from specialist underwater-escape training.

Rotor movement, noise, wind and constrained deck space make a helicopter different from a car with overhead blades. The safest passenger movement is the one directed by the flight or helideck crew for that aircraft and location. Remain in the designated waiting place until directed. Do not infer permission from an open door, another passenger's movement, or your previous trip.

Before entering the movement area, secure loose belongings and follow instructions for baggage, headwear, eye and hearing protection, and clothing. A loose article can become a distraction or an airborne hazard. Do not chase a dropped item toward an aircraft or over the deck edge. Stop in a safe location, alert the crew, and let them control recovery. Keep long objects and baggage in the manner the crew specifies; do not lift them overhead. Never walk toward the tail or choose an unbriefed route around the aircraft.

Provide accurate information for the passenger and baggage loading process. Aircraft loading concerns both total load and its distribution. An apparently small unreported item or unauthorized seat change can defeat that plan. Let the crew handle loading limits and seating; passengers do not calculate an exception or trade places informally. Report a seatbelt or seat problem before movement rather than improvising a restraint.

In the briefing, locate your restraint, normal exit and the emergency arrangements relevant to your seat. Understand who directs door operation, how instructions will be communicated, and what passenger safety equipment is provided. Ask the crew to resolve a point you do not understand. Do not touch aircraft controls or open doors except as instructed. During a critical phase, avoid distracting the crew; communicate an urgent safety problem using the briefed method.

Overwater equipment is aircraft- and operation-specific. The briefing and required specialist training establish how to use the provided flotation or breathing equipment and when to act. This written orientation cannot replace a supervised helicopter underwater-escape or water-survival course. It deliberately does not teach a universal ditching sequence: seat orientation, exits, restraints and equipment differ. Before accepting an assignment, confirm that any required transport survival training is current and accepted by the operator.

Arrival still requires direction. Stay secured until the crew authorizes the next movement. Leave by the designated route, maintain the instructed clearance and go to the identified safe area. The helideck can have simultaneous freight or fuel activity, weather exposure and limited escape paths. Do not stop for photographs or stand in a route while searching your bag. If the deck is wet or moving, follow the crew's movement instructions and report any inability to proceed safely.

### Worked situation

The safe response is to stop the unsafe movement from a safe position and alert the crew. Another passenger should not run after the person through the same hazard. Recovering property is secondary to keeping everyone in the controlled passenger route.

## O04 — Vessel travel and personnel transfers

### Objectives

Recognize vessel-motion and transfer hazards; follow the master and transfer crew; identify conditions requiring a hold; explain why this course does not qualify transfer operators.

A vessel and an offshore structure may move differently. A gap that looks manageable can change while a passenger is committed to crossing it. Slippery surfaces, wind, wake, hand injuries and loose baggage add to the exposure. Do not choose your own transfer method or timing. The authorized transfer team determines whether conditions, equipment and personnel are suitable.

Before boarding, receive the vessel briefing and identify the passenger area, alarms, muster location and relevant emergency responsibilities. Locate the instructions or station bill provided by the crew. Make sure the manifest reflects your presence. Wear the specified personal flotation and other protective equipment as instructed; ask the crew to correct fit or a defect before transfer. A carried flotation device is not the same as one correctly worn when required. Do not substitute an unapproved item because it is more comfortable.

Use only the designated boarding arrangement when released by the crew. Keep hands away from surfaces that can close together, moving lines and unguarded edges. Do not use mooring lines as handholds or stand inside loops of line. Keep baggage under the crew's arrangement so it does not block your view or occupy hands needed for safe movement. If the route, handhold or footing is unclear, ask before committing to the transfer. Do not jump a gap or attempt a transfer because the person ahead succeeded.

During transit, remain in permitted areas, secure belongings and use the handholds or seating arrangements provided. Report illness or impairment early. Seasickness can reduce the ability to follow instructions; hiding it until transfer leaves fewer options. Never lean beyond a rail to retrieve property, dispose of waste or obtain a better view. A person overboard requires an immediate alarm and the vessel's response, not an untrained passenger entering the water.

Personnel transfer devices require equipment-specific instruction and a controlled operation. Designs vary: a method suitable for one device may be unsafe on another. Before use, the transfer team must brief the passenger's position, restraints or handholds, baggage rules, signals and response to an aborted transfer. The designated crew handles equipment inspection, crane operation and transfer authorization. Passengers must report a defect or inability to follow the method, rather than treating a current orientation certificate as competence to rig or operate the system.

If communication is lost, weather changes, a passenger becomes unwell, or the landing area is obstructed, report it immediately and follow the crew's hold or abort instruction. Do not improvise signals. Schedule pressure is not a control for vessel motion. An incomplete transfer can mean remaining offshore or returning ashore under a revised plan; it does not justify self-directed climbing or jumping.

## O05 — Arrival, living offshore and exposure at the worksite

### Objectives

Complete a site-specific orientation; establish routes, contacts and restrictions; recognize dropped-object, hand, clothing and living-area hazards; respond to changing weather and extended duty.

Transport arrival and permission to begin work are separate events. Report to the designated person, confirm accountability and receive the facility-specific orientation before starting an assignment. Find out who is in charge, how the shift is organized, which areas are restricted, and what operations are occurring. Walk the permitted route with the appropriate guide. Locate the actual muster area and understand an alternative if the normal path becomes unsafe. Identify where required protective equipment is worn and where equipment must be kept out of accommodation or food areas. Look for changes in floor level, open gratings, wet stairs, hoses and overhead work. Use handrails and maintain stable footing; do not carry a load that prevents seeing the route or using required support. Report damaged stairs, guards or lighting rather than normalizing them because other people pass that way.

Report unsecured objects from a safe position. Do not reach over an edge to retrieve one or remove a barrier because the task appears paused.

Living quarters are part of the work environment. Observe quiet periods, hygiene and smoking restrictions, keep escape routes clear, and store personal items where they cannot fall or obstruct movement. Report illness through the designated medical or supervisor process. Respect confidential personal information and maintain professional conduct in shared accommodation. Do not tolerate threats or harassment as an unavoidable feature of close quarters; use the reporting routes explained by the employer and seek immediate help for an urgent threat.

Weather can affect access, visibility, footing, vessel movement and evacuation options. Follow the facility's weather restrictions and announcements. Do not invent a personal wind or wave limit. If conditions change during a task, pause the affected work and notify the responsible supervisor. The same applies to fatigue from extended duty or interrupted rest. A revised schedule requires a revised readiness decision; a worker's presence offshore does not mean that person remains fit for every task.

## O06 — Applying SEMS to an ordinary work decision

### Objectives

Explain how the safety and environmental management system affects a worker's job; distinguish stop-work and restart decisions; identify changes requiring review; communicate hazards and preserve accurate records.

An offshore safety and environmental management system connects management decisions to the conditions in which work occurs. For a worker, the practical question is not whether a binder exists. It is whether the correct information, people, procedure and protective arrangements are available for the actual task. On covered U.S. offshore operations, the operator's SEMS program includes documented arrangements for hazards, changes, procedures, training, equipment integrity, emergency response, learning from incidents and worker participation. Follow the program applicable to your facility and role.

Think of the system as a chain of decisions. Facility information describes what is present. Hazard analysis identifies how people or the environment could be harmed. A task-level job safety analysis makes those concerns concrete for the job. Operating procedures and safe work practices explain the authorized method. Training establishes who has the skills and knowledge to perform it. Equipment-integrity and pre-startup arrangements address whether the physical system is ready. A break in any link should not be covered up with another signature.

Before a task, participate in the briefing. State the step you will perform, the exposure you could encounter, the control you depend on and the person controlling the interface. Ask what happens if conditions change. If the work pack identifies one pump but the physical label identifies another, stop before intervention. A permit authorizes specified work under specified conditions; it does not make contradictory information harmless. Do not operate a valve to discover which equipment is connected.

Changes can be physical or organizational. A replacement component, revised procedure, different crew, temporary bypass or altered simultaneous operation can affect risk. Workers identify the change and refer it through the facility process; they do not decide that a change is “like for like” merely because a replacement fits. Management-of-change review and a task briefing serve related but different functions. Updating a JSA cannot by itself approve an engineering change, and an approved engineering change still needs communication to the people doing the work.

Stop-work authority means a person can raise a hazardous condition and initiate the site's safe stop process. Use a clear message about the location, exposure and requested hold. Do not create another emergency with an unplanned abrupt equipment action; follow the procedure for stopping safely. The facility also identifies ultimate work authority for operational safety decisions. Knowing that responsible role matters when a disagreement or unresolved hazard persists. The person who reports a concern is not automatically the person permitted to restart the equipment.

Employee participation includes contributing observations, asking questions and reporting unsafe conditions. Use the internal routes provided, and understand the available route for reporting concerns to BSEE for covered operations. A supervisor's reluctance to hear a concern does not erase that route. Use the current official reporting information provided in the source register or facility posting. Give factual information: what was observed, where, when and what exposure may remain. Do not alter evidence, speculate about motives or enter danger to collect proof.

Records should support the next decision. A truthful JSA, inspection report, handover or incident account identifies the actual condition and any remaining restriction. Do not sign for an inspection you did not perform, mark someone present when unaccounted for, or erase an unsuccessful observation to make a record look clean. Incident investigation and audits should lead to corrected conditions and verified learning. A completed form is not evidence that the physical control works.

### Objectives

Marine debris begins as an ordinary work-planning problem: loose packaging, an unsecured lid, a damaged waste container or material staged where wind and movement can carry it away. Once in the water, an object may be difficult to recover and can harm wildlife, vessels or other users. Prevention is more reliable than trying to retrieve a lost item from an exposed edge.

Before unpacking or moving supplies, identify where wrapping, offcuts and small parts will go. Use the designated containers and secure them under the facility's arrangements. Keep lids or other closures effective; do not place light material on an open bin and assume it will remain there. Inspect the route and staging location for wind, drainage openings, edges and nearby work. A tidy indoor packing method may fail on an exposed deck. Ask the responsible person to correct a damaged container or unsuitable staging arrangement before material is released.

Keep waste streams identified and separated according to the site plan. A food container, contaminated absorbent, oily rag, chemical container and damaged battery do not become the same waste because they fit in one bag. Consult the label, applicable product information and designated environmental contact when classification is uncertain. Do not pour unwanted liquid into a drain or overboard. Do not use an unlabeled container to make a material easier to transport. The person responsible for waste handling determines the authorized storage and disposal route.

Report a dropped object or release promptly. From a safe position, identify the material if known, approximate amount, location, time, movement and any immediate exposure. State uncertainty instead of inventing a quantity. Keep others out of the affected area and follow the site's notification instructions. Do not climb outside rails, lean overboard or enter the water to recover property. Recovery decisions belong to authorized personnel using a planned method.

A spill may involve more than pollution. Vapors, fire, slippery footing, toxicity or pressure can make an apparently small release dangerous. Do not judge an unknown material only by the size of the puddle. Follow the alarm, isolation and withdrawal arrangements for your role. ## O08 — Offshore alarms, muster, evacuation and recovery

### Objectives

Act on the facility's alarm and muster instructions; preserve accountability; identify the limits of orientation during abandonment or rescue; distinguish emergency reentry from authorization to resume work.

An offshore emergency can affect escape routes and transport options at the same time. Preparation therefore depends on the actual facility plan: alarm meanings, reporting method, primary and alternate muster locations, assigned emergency roles and available evacuation arrangements. Learn these on arrival, not while an alarm is sounding. Confirm how you will receive instructions in noisy work areas or accommodation, and tell the site contact if you cannot perceive the alarm or understand the briefing.

When an alarm or instruction requires withdrawal, follow the site procedure for making your immediate activity safe only within your assigned role and without delaying escape. Do not remain to diagnose the alarm, finish a short task or retrieve personal property. Move by the instructed safe route. If that route is blocked or exposed, use the site's alternative arrangement and communicate your location from safety. Do not enter smoke, an unknown atmosphere or a restricted area because it appears to provide a shortcut.

At muster, report to the accountability person and remain available for instructions. Identify yourself and provide factual information about anyone you last saw, including location and time if known. Do not mark another worker present based on an assumption, leave without being recorded, or circulate rumors that complicate the count. If you are assigned a muster role, follow the specific training for that role. Being helpful does not authorize an untrained person to conduct a search.

Evacuation and abandonment are controlled operations. Lifeboats, rafts, transfer equipment and passenger positions have equipment-specific procedures. Follow the designated crew and your required survival training. This course does not provide a universal water-entry method or authorize a practice jump. Water entry can introduce impact, cold, entanglement, burning-surface or separation hazards; never use it as an improvised shortcut. Raise a concern if you cannot use the planned arrangement and obtain the responsible instruction before an emergency where practicable.

If someone goes overboard, raise the alarm immediately using the vessel or facility's method, report the location and maintain observation from safety if you can. Do not enter the water or improvise a rescue from an unprotected edge. Trained responders and the responsible marine crew coordinate recovery. If assisting from a protected location, perform only the actions you are trained and directed to perform. A second casualty makes the original recovery harder.

Medical response offshore also depends on communication. Give the precise facility location, access route, observed condition and known hazards. Follow the trained responder's instructions within your competence. Do not enter an unsafe scene, move an injured person casually, or provide treatment for which you are not trained. Emergency transport decisions involve medical and operational authorities, weather and suitable transport. Your factual report helps those people plan; a general safety credential does not transfer their authority to you.

After an all-clear, confirm what it permits. Return to accommodation, reentry to a deck and restart of a maintenance task may be separate decisions. Permits, isolations, damaged equipment and conflicting work may need reassessment. Report any control disturbed during the emergency. Participate in the debrief with observations about routes, communications and accountability; record deficiencies so they can be corrected before the next event.

### Worked situation

During an alarm, a worker reaches the primary route and sees smoke beyond the next door. The worker withdraws from that exposure, uses the briefed alternate route and reports to the designated accountability contact. At muster, the worker reports where a colleague was last seen but does not mark that colleague safe. After the all-clear, the worker obtains the responsible task restart instructions instead of assuming the earlier permit automatically remains valid.

### Practice and explained response

Why is “I will go back and check” an unsafe default when someone is missing? The missing person creates a response need, but not safe entry conditions or rescue competence. Give the last-known location to the response team, remain accounted for and perform only an assigned, trained action from safety.

# Drilling Site Safety Orientation — five application stations

## Administration

Use a classroom, paper diagrams, and simulated communication only. Each learner must demonstrate every listed criterion without prompts after instruction. Mark Pass, Needs practice, or Not assessed, with observed evidence. A critical error stops the scenario. Explain the reason, reteach, and administer the changed retry. Every criterion must pass; do not average away an unsafe intervention. These stations assess orientation decisions, not equipment operating skills.

## 1. Arrival during a changing operation

**Materials:** fictional arrival card: “You are delivering a sealed parts box. Rig-up is underway. The usual stairs are inside a lifting boundary. Your escort has not arrived. The gate contact can reach the area controller.” Draw a safe waiting point at the gate and mark the ordinary stairs closed.

**Task:** Describe and role-play your check-in and movement decision. **Criteria:** identifies delivery purpose; remains at safe waiting point; confirms area controller/escort; does not assume experience grants access; requests the current route and emergency/accountability briefing; recognizes the rig-up phase changes access.

**Critical error:** crossing the lifting boundary or climbing an improvised route. **Worked resolution:** check in, wait for coordinated access and escort as required, learn the current emergency instructions, then use only the approved route. **Retry:** rig is operating but a maintenance crew has temporarily removed a walkway guard. The learner must recognize the changed route rather than treating the operating phase alone as clearance.

## 2. Pipe movement and pressure boundary

**Materials:** draw rack A, handling path B, fixed wall C, and safe pedestrian path D. A tubular is suspended over B. A hose at C is labelled “pressure status not verified.” A radio card states “communication with operator lost.”

**Task:** Select a safe position, explain the hold, and communicate concerns. **Criteria:** avoids B and the pinch position between load and C; does not grab the tubular; uses the agreed stop/hold process; identifies lost communications; does not touch or tighten the hose; seeks responsible verification rather than assuming a silent pump means safe.

**Critical errors:** entering under the load, hands-on intervention with moving pipe, or manipulating the unverified line. **Worked resolution:** remain on D outside boundaries, notify the controlling personnel, hold affected operations, and let authorized people restore controls. **Retry:** movement has stopped but the load remains suspended. The learner still respects the exposure and does not enter merely because it is stationary.

## 3. Land hazards after weather changes

**Materials:** trench card: “Inspected before dawn. Heavy rain since then. Water visible. The crew wants a short entry.” Pond card: “Lined produced-water pond; barrier moved; tool at the bottom; depth unknown.”

**Task:** Explain the decisions and notifications for both locations. **Criteria:** holds trench entry for competent-person reassessment and required controls; recognizes water and changed soil conditions; does not select shoring independently; stays outside pond barrier; recognizes slipping/drowning/chemical/atmosphere concerns; activates trained response rather than entering if a person falls.

**Critical errors:** approving a quick unprotected entry or entering the pond for retrieval/rescue. **Worked resolution:** restore control through the responsible site personnel and preserve safe exclusion; duration and apparent depth do not remove the hazards. **Retry:** no rain, but heavy equipment has moved next to the trench and cracks are observed. The learner recognizes a new reassessment trigger.

## 4. Offshore transfer and accountability

**Materials:** manifest card with the learner's name missing; transfer card: “Crew has not given boarding permission; vessel motion is increasing”; site card listing two muster points and a controller-selected safe point B. These are classroom cards, not a real transport instruction.

**Task:** Resolve travel readiness, state boarding limits, and make a simulated arrival check-in. **Criteria:** corrects manifest through the responsible dispatcher; waits for authorized crew instruction; reports unclear briefing or unready equipment; does not jump or improvise transfer; checks in on arrival; identifies current emergency instructions and safe point B.

**Critical errors:** boarding without clearance, inventing a transfer technique, or bypassing the manifest. **Worked resolution:** hold until records, equipment, and crew-controlled conditions are ready; follow site-specific induction after arrival. **Retry:** manifest correct but the assigned flotation equipment is damaged. The learner holds for approved replacement and instruction rather than accepting the corrected paperwork as complete readiness.

## 5. Integrated handover, exposure, and restart

**Materials:** handover card: “A dust-control enclosure was removed; one machine is isolated with a guard off; gas all-clear issued for the area; lifting permit remains suspended; visitor not yet checked out.”

**Task:** Give a structured incoming-shift handover and identify what may resume. **Criteria:** reports each unresolved condition; preserves isolation; does not substitute PPE for the removed enclosure; distinguishes area all-clear from lift authorization; resolves visitor accountability; identifies responsible corrective action owners; records unknowns rather than inventing safe status.

**Critical errors:** authorizing machine startup, lift restart, or uncontrolled exposure solely because of the all-clear. **Worked resolution:** transfer the actual limits and require each task's controls and authority to be restored independently. **Retry:** all equipment is restored but the incoming crew has not been briefed on a relocated emergency route. The learner completes communication before dependent work.

## SEMS teaching reference — what the elements mean at the worker interface

Use this reference with O06. It explains connections, not a substitute operator SEMS manual.

| Element | What the worker should recognize and do |
|---|---|

| Safety and environmental information | Use current equipment identity and hazard information. If the field label and work pack disagree, hold the affected activity and have the responsible people resolve the discrepancy. |
| Hazard analysis | Identify the exposure path and affected people. Contribute what you see at the worksite; a facility assessment does not excuse skipping the actual task briefing. |
| Management of change | Report a departure from the approved arrangement before using it. A temporary workaround can alter risk just as a permanent modification can. |
| Operating procedures | Obtain the current authorized method and understand its limits. Stop for an unexplained step rather than combining fragments of different revisions. |
| Safe work practices | Apply the relevant permit and protection arrangements. A signed permit must correspond to the actual task and conditions; it is not blanket authorization. |
| Training | Perform only the assigned duties for which you have the necessary instruction and competence. Report an unfamiliar operation before someone mistakes your presence for qualification. |
| Mechanical integrity | Report leaks, damage and degraded controls. Do not bypass a protective device to maintain production or sign a check you did not perform. |
| Pre-startup review | Recognize that completion of installation or maintenance is not the same as authorization to start. The responsible review must resolve readiness and communicate restrictions. |
| Emergency response | Know your alarm, route, muster and reporting actions. Leave specialist response to people trained and assigned to perform it. |
| Incident investigation | Supply accurate observations and preserve evidence from safety. Distinguish what you saw from what you heard, and do not hide near misses. |
| Audits | Cooperate with truthful records and descriptions of actual practice. An audit is an opportunity to identify gaps, not to stage controls that disappear afterward. |
| Records and documentation | Identify the task, version, actual response and remaining action. Keep records through the employer's prescribed process; do not invent retention periods or destroy unsuccessful attempts. |
| Stop-work authority | Raise a specific hazardous condition and initiate the safe hold procedure. Seek resolution without creating an uncontrolled equipment action. |
| Ultimate work authority | Know which designated role makes final operational safety decisions on the facility. Escalate unresolved conflicts through that route; seniority alone is not a substitute for designation. |
| Employee participation | Contribute to briefings, improvement discussions and hazard reporting. Ask whether the proposed control works in the real work arrangement. |
| Reporting unsafe conditions | Use the site's reporting process and understand the available BSEE reporting route for covered operations. Report factual concerns without exposing yourself to gather proof. |
