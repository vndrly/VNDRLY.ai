# VNDRLY/U — Drilling Well Control: Role-Based Decisions

VUC007 · Original instructional material · Authoring version 1

## How to use this course

This program teaches the physical reasoning, observation, calculations, communication, and supervised decision making used in drilling well control. The examples are invented training wells. They are not operating instructions for a real well. Actual responses depend on the approved well program, installed equipment, formation limits, fluid system, rig procedures, and the authority of the person performing the operation.

The embedded foundation describes awareness, introductory, driller and supervisor responsibilities. This package uses the field-team, operator and supervisor pathways defined in its own instructor guide. Awareness learners recognize loss of control and report it. Introductory learners also interpret basic pressure and volume relationships. Drillers apply detection, approved response, monitoring, and team communication in assessed simulations. Supervisors additionally evaluate pressure windows, circulation plans, changing conditions, and recovery decisions. Surface and subsea equipment are distinguished throughout. A knowledge examination cannot demonstrate console operation or qualify a person to supervise a live well.

Read the worked examples before attempting the exercises. Write units at every calculation step. Mark the depth datum and whether pressure is gauge or absolute. If information is missing, identify it; an invented value is not a reasonable substitute for a safety-critical measurement.

## 1. The well as a pressure system

### Objectives

Explain why influx occurs; distinguish pore pressure, hydrostatic pressure, friction pressure, surface pressure, and fracture limits; choose the correct depth for a calculation.

Formation fluid occupies connected pore spaces. At an exposed permeable interval it can enter the well when the pressure inside the well is sufficiently below the formation pressure. The size of the imbalance, permeability, fluid properties, and time affect the influx. A small imbalance sustained long enough can matter. Detecting an influx early reduces the volume that must subsequently be controlled.

A static liquid column produces pressure from its density and vertical height. In customary drilling units:

**Hydrostatic pressure, psi = 0.052 × density, lb/US gal (ppg) × true vertical depth, ft.**

The coefficient combines units; it is not dimensionless permission to mix metres with feet. In SI units, pressure in pascals is density in kg/m³ × gravitational acceleration in m/s² × vertical height in metres. Divide pascals by one million for MPa. Measured depth follows the borehole path. True vertical depth measures vertical separation from the selected datum. A deviated hole can have much more measured depth without gaining equivalent hydrostatic head.

When circulating, annular friction contributes to bottomhole pressure. Surface backpressure can contribute too. A useful simplified bookkeeping expression is bottomhole pressure = hydrostatic contribution + annular friction contribution + applied surface pressure. It omits acceleration and other transient effects; use it to understand a case, not to replace an engineered hydraulic model.

The upper side of the operating window matters as much as the lower side. Excess wellbore pressure can fracture a weak exposed formation, lose fluid, reduce column height, and create another route to loss of control. A heavier fluid is therefore not automatically a safer fluid. The operating objective is a controlled pressure within the applicable window, allowing the margins and uncertainty specified in the well program.

### Worked example: depth and pressure

A training well has 12,400 ft measured depth, 10,000 ft TVD, and uniform 10.0 ppg fluid. Static hydrostatic pressure is 0.052 × 10.0 × 10,000 = **5,200 psi**. Using measured depth would give 6,448 psi, overstating support by 1,248 psi. If the formation pressure is 5,350 psi, the static column alone is 150 psi underbalanced. A 200 psi annular friction contribution while circulating can conceal that deficiency: calculated circulating bottomhole pressure is 5,400 psi, but stopping circulation removes that contribution.

### Field application and common errors

At a pump shutdown, the driller must understand that removal of friction is a change in the pressure system, not merely a change in machinery noise. Supervisors should ask which measurements support the assumed pressure window. Common errors include treating surface pump pressure as bottomhole pressure, using measured depth for hydrostatic pressure, and comparing pressures referenced to different datums.

### Practice

Calculate hydrostatic pressure for 1,200 kg/m³ fluid over 2,500 m using 9.81 m/s². Explain why that result does not establish that a well is safe to drill.

**Worked answer:** 1,200 × 9.81 × 2,500 = 29,430,000 Pa = **29.43 MPa**. Formation pressure, weakest exposed formation, dynamic effects, uncertainties, and the approved margins are still needed. If you used the borehole length or omitted units, repeat the calculation with a labelled vertical sketch.

## 2. Fluid columns, pressure windows, and useful calculations

### Objectives

Calculate layered hydrostatic pressure, a conditional balancing density, and equivalent circulating density; explain the assumptions that make each calculation valid.

A well may contain several fluids rather than a uniform column. Calculate each vertical segment separately and add the pressure contributions. Density samples should represent the relevant fluid, temperature, pressure, and location. A surface sample can differ from the fluid downhole. Gas entrainment and compressibility require more than the incompressible liquid model used in introductory examples.

For a uniform conventional system, an additional pressure deficit can be expressed as an additional fluid density:

**Density increase, ppg = pressure deficit, psi ÷ (0.052 × TVD, ft).**

When valid stabilized shut-in drillpipe pressure represents the formation-to-column imbalance, a simplified balancing density is current density plus that increase. This is often called kill mud weight in conventional calculations. It is not valid merely because a gauge has a number. A float, trapped pressure, off-bottom condition, plugged string, differing fluids, gas migration, or unreliable readings may defeat the assumed pressure connection. The supervisor must establish the data's meaning before approving a plan.

Equivalent circulating density converts the annular friction contribution into a density equivalent at a specified depth:

**ECD, ppg = static density + annular friction loss, psi ÷ (0.052 × TVD, ft).**

ECD is specific to flow, fluid properties, geometry, cuttings loading, and depth. Drillpipe friction is not substituted for annular friction. A planned density must be checked for both static control and circulating formation loading.

### Worked example: a balancing calculation with a limit check

Use a uniform 10.0 ppg column at 10,000 ft TVD. For this exercise only, the verified stabilized drillpipe pressure is 520 psi with an unobstructed pressure connection and the string at bottom. Density increase = 520 ÷ 520 = **1.0 ppg**, giving **11.0 ppg** balancing density. A model predicts 260 psi annular friction at the selected circulation condition, equivalent to 0.5 ppg at that depth. The simplified ECD becomes **11.5 ppg**. If the applicable formation constraint at the evaluation depth were 11.3 ppg equivalent, the proposed condition would not pass that check. Increasing density alone would not solve it; the engineer must reassess the complete approved plan.

### Worked example: layered fluids

An imaginary vertical column contains 2,000 ft of 9.0 ppg fluid above 6,000 ft of 10.5 ppg fluid. Hydrostatic pressure = (0.052 × 9 × 2,000) + (0.052 × 10.5 × 6,000) = 936 + 3,276 = **4,212 psi**. Its average density over 8,000 vertical feet is 4,212 ÷ (0.052 × 8,000) = **10.125 ppg**. A dense pill at a different position changes local pressure relationships even if the total volume stays constant. Model its actual position rather than assuming the whole well has the pill's density.

### Practice

A verified training deficit is 390 psi at 7,500 ft TVD with 9.5 ppg original fluid. Calculate the balancing density and identify two reasons not to apply the result to an actual well without review.

**Worked answer:** 390 ÷ (0.052 × 7,500) = 1.0 ppg; balancing density **10.5 ppg**. Examples of missing checks include formation tolerance under circulation, validity of the pressure connection, fluid distribution, equipment limits, and the approved method. A correct number with an unverified assumption is not a correct operating decision.

## 3. Volume accounting and prevention while moving pipe

### Objectives

Reconcile active volume, distinguish pipe capacity from displacement, interpret a trip record, and explain swab and surge effects.

The fluid inventory is a measurement system. A gain may indicate influx, but it can also result from a documented transfer. A loss may indicate formation loss, a surface leak, or a transfer out. The correct response is disciplined reconciliation with operations and independent observations, not dismissal of an inconvenient reading. Maintain a clear designation of active pits, reserve pits, transfer routes, and trip tank. Record intentional movements before or as they occur under the rig procedure. Unannounced transfers can conceal a kick or produce a false alarm.

Capacity is the volume inside a pipe or space. Displacement is the volume excluded by the pipe material or, for a specified condition, by its closed external envelope. Whether the pipe is open-ended, draining, filled, or closed changes the expected fill relationship. Use the approved tally and tool geometry. Do not apply a single displacement value to every bottomhole assembly component.

For circular dimensions in inches and length in feet, ideal cylindrical volume in US barrels is **diameter squared × length ÷ 1,029.4**. Annular capacity uses the difference of squared diameters. This geometric model excludes tool joints, washouts, eccentric effects on flow, and compressibility; real capacity calculations must account for the actual geometry and inventory.

Swabbing is a pressure reduction associated with withdrawing pipe through fluid. Surging is a pressure increase associated with running pipe. Speed, clearances, fluid rheology, restrictions, and assembly geometry matter. A pipe movement can create an influx even when the static fluid density was adequate, or create losses that undermine the primary barrier. The planned movement limits and monitoring requirements must address those effects.

### Worked example: trip fill discrepancy

For a fictional open-ended, drained string segment, the approved displacement is 0.007 bbl/ft. Pulling 900 ft should require **6.3 bbl** of replacement fluid under the stated condition. The calibrated trip tank records only 3.8 bbl. The **2.5 bbl shortfall** is a warning that the well did not take the expected volume. A possible influx must be considered promptly under the approved procedure; do not pull another interval merely to see whether the discrepancy disappears. First communicate the discrepancy and suspend the operation as required, then use the prescribed verification and response.

The opposite discrepancy also matters. If the well takes much more than calculated, investigate possible losses, geometry assumptions, tank error, and transfer errors. Neither direction should be normalized by changing the tally to match the observation.

### Practice

An ideal 8.5 in bore surrounds 5.0 in pipe over 1,000 ft. Calculate annular volume. Then explain why that number is not a measured pit gain.

**Worked answer:** (8.5² − 5²) × 1,000 ÷ 1,029.4 = **45.90 bbl** approximately. This is a geometric estimate of space, whereas pit gain is an observed change in surface inventory that must be reconciled with pumping, transfers, and well behaviour. If you added the squared diameters, draw the pipe occupying part of the hole and repeat.

## 4. Reading the well before an influx becomes large

### Objectives

Distinguish warning signs from direct flow evidence; reconcile trends; communicate an actionable alarm without waiting for certainty.

A change in drilling rate, cuttings characteristics, gas readings, torque, or other drilling indicators may suggest a change in formation conditions. These observations are not individually a diagnosis. Record the operating context: weight on bit, rotary speed, pump rate, lithology, mud properties, and recent transfers. Comparing unlike operating conditions can produce a false trend.

More direct evidence includes unexplained increased return flow, unexplained active volume gain, flow when the prescribed stopped-flow condition should not produce it, and a trip-fill discrepancy. A plausible alternative explanation is a reason to investigate through the approved response, not permission to continue an unsafe operation. A flow check itself has procedural limits; do not improvise its duration or allow it to become a delay in a required response.

Sensors have range, calibration, latency, resolution, and failure modes. A plugged line, incorrect pit selection, failed paddle, or uncertain zero can produce believable values. Cross-check independent sources: pit trend, measured flow, pump strokes, trip tank, direct observation from a safe location, and the current activity log. Multiple screens displaying the same sensor are not independent corroboration.

### Worked scenario: the convenient transfer explanation

During steady circulation, returns rise while pump strokes remain constant. Active volume increases by 4 bbl. A crewmember says, “Probably the reserve transfer.” The transfer log shows no authorized transfer and the responsible operator cannot confirm one. The driller states the observed discrepancy, initiates the approved response, and assigns verification tasks without postponing required control actions. The supervisor receives the time, activity, pump state, gain, flow observation, and uncertainties. The team does not describe the gain as proven gas; it describes the evidence accurately.

### Communication model

Use: **identity and location → observation → operation affected → action already taken under procedure → assistance or decision needed**. The receiver repeats the critical values and intended responsibility. For example: “Driller at console. Unexplained 4-barrel active gain and increased returns at unchanged pump strokes. Executing the approved kick response. Confirm transfer status and notify the well supervisor.” Do not bury the concern beneath a long history.

### Practice

Two displays show identical flat pit volume, but the trip tank indicates inadequate fill and the pit transmitter has a fault alarm. Is the flat display reassurance?

**Worked answer:** No. Both displays may share the failed input. Preserve the alarm, use independent approved monitoring, and follow the response to the trip discrepancy. Remediation: trace each displayed value to its sensor before calling it independent evidence.

## 5. Barriers, equipment, and response preparation

### Objectives

Describe barrier functions and verification; identify equipment limitations; separate a tested component from a verified pressure-containing system.

A barrier is a system of elements that prevents unintended flow along a potential path. Fluid can provide a primary barrier only while its pressure and condition are adequate. Mechanical barriers depend on their design, placement, operating state, integrity, and the flow path being considered. A barrier diagram should identify the formation, internal string path, annulus, casing, cement, wellhead, preventers, and connected lines. A component's presence on the diagram is not proof that it is effective.

An annular preventer seals around compatible objects within its operating envelope; it is not interchangeable with every ram function. Pipe rams must match the applicable tubular condition. Blind and shear functions have specific prerequisites and limitations. Tool joints and nonshearable components complicate positioning. A supervisor must know the documented equipment capability and available operating energy rather than assuming that any object can be sealed or sheared.

The choke controls a restricted flow path; it is not a cure for an unsuitable downstream system. Lines, valves, pressure ratings, erosion, gas handling, separation capacity, vent routing, and ignition hazards all matter. The mud-gas separator has operating limits. A degasser used to remove entrained gas from fluid is not a substitute for appropriately designed well-control gas handling. The accumulator/control system must be available and verified under the applicable maintenance and test program.

Test results are meaningful only with the test boundary, pressure, duration, acceptance criteria, instrument condition, and observed leakage recorded. A successful function test shows movement; it does not alone establish pressure integrity. A pressure test of one route does not verify an untested route. Failed or uncertain barriers require the specified response and authorization before work resumes.

### Worked scenario: the reassuring checklist

The handover says “BOP tested.” The current job requires a different tubular size and a different pressure path from the documented test. The correct decision is to reconcile the installed configuration, verified capabilities, and required tests before the operation. Copying yesterday's green check would obscure the exact barrier now relied upon.

### Practice

Draw a hypothetical surface well with fluid, casing, cement, wellhead, pipe rams, internal string valve, and choke route. Trace an annular flow path and an internal string flow path. Identify which evidence you would need for each barrier element.

**Worked answer:** The drawing must show both routes rather than assuming annular closure closes the pipe interior. Required evidence includes the approved configuration and procedure, compatible tubular/equipment dimensions, valid test records, correct lineup, control availability, and known equipment limits. An assessor should challenge any element labelled effective without supporting evidence.

## 6. Initial response, verification, and stabilized information

### Objectives

Execute the approved response in a configured training simulator; verify the resulting state; distinguish observed readings from assumptions; maintain a usable incident record.

The initial response must be learned against a specific rig procedure and equipment configuration. A generic book cannot supply a universally correct valve sequence. Before operating, identify the applicable response for drilling, tripping, pipe out of the hole, and other relevant conditions; establish responsibilities and communications; know the approved equipment positioning and operating limits. Practice those responses until a crew can act without inventing a sequence under stress.

In a simulation, recognition should lead promptly to the specified response, communication, and verification. Verification means checking what the well and equipment actually did: relevant flow observations, commanded versus indicated equipment state, pressure behaviour, leaks, and other procedure-defined evidence. A control command alone is not proof of closure or containment. If a response fails, use the documented contingency and escalate; repeatedly issuing the same command without observing the system wastes time.

Record shut-in drillpipe pressure, casing pressure, gain, time, pump state, string position, fluid condition, and the behaviour of readings over time where applicable. Identify whether readings are stable, rising, fluctuating, or unreliable. Do not overwrite the first readings with later numbers. A sequence of measurements preserves information about migration, leaks, trapped pressure, and measurement faults.

Drillpipe and casing pressures need not match. Different columns, influx distribution, and connection conditions can produce different pressures. Selecting whichever number produces a convenient density is not acceptable. The supervisor checks the physical meaning of the data, pressure connectivity, fluid distribution, and the approved interpretation method. Pressure trapped by an operation is not automatically formation pressure. Conversely, assuming all unexpected pressure is trapped can conceal a real imbalance.

### Worked scenario: unstable readings

A training well shows 420 psi drillpipe pressure immediately after response, then 490 and 520 psi at successive logged observations. The casing reading rises too. The recorder retains all three times and values and reports the trend. The supervisor does not average them into a fictional “stabilized 477 psi” and calculate a final plan. The team maintains the prescribed monitoring and evaluates the cause and approved options. If instrument range or integrity is in question, that uncertainty is explicitly carried forward.

### Practice

Write a handover using: 10:14 initial gain 3 bbl; 10:15 response initiated; 10:16 closure indicated; pressure connection uncertain because of a string float; no confirmed stabilized drillpipe pressure.

**Worked answer:** “At 10:14 an unexplained 3-bbl gain was observed. Approved response began at 10:15; closure indication at 10:16 still requires the procedure's containment verification. Drillpipe pressure is not verified as a formation connection because of the float. No approved balancing-density calculation is available yet.” Remediation: remove any unsupported statement that the well is killed or the pressure is stabilized.

## 7. Circulation methods and pressure schedules

### Objectives

Explain the reasoning behind conventional circulation methods; calculate a simplified training pressure schedule; identify conditions that invalidate it.

Conventional circulation methods seek to remove influx and replace inadequate fluid while controlling pressure within the well's limits. In the Driller's Method concept, an initial circulation removes the influx using the existing fluid, followed by circulation of the selected heavier fluid. In the Wait and Weight concept, suitable heavier fluid is prepared before the planned circulation. These names describe concepts, not permission to choose a method without an approved plan. Fluid mixing capacity, time, migration, pressure limits, equipment availability, and uncertainty influence the decision.

In a simplified surface training case with a verified conventional pressure connection and specified constant reduced circulation rate, initial circulating drillpipe pressure can be represented as verified shut-in drillpipe pressure plus the recorded circulating pressure for the original fluid at that rate. A simplified final circulating pressure scales the original reduced-rate pressure by the new-to-original density ratio. These relationships assume the training model's geometry and friction behaviour; non-Newtonian rheology, changing rate, restrictions, subsea line losses, and changing fluid columns require more complete treatment.

The drillpipe pressure schedule represents changing hydrostatic support as the selected fluid travels down the string. It is not a timetable to follow regardless of observed conditions. Pump strokes, output calibration, string capacity, rate, and fluid arrival determine the expected progression. A linear decline is an educational simplification for a uniform vertical string; complex geometry and density placement can require a different schedule.

### Worked example: simplified surface schedule

Assume the training case has 10.0 ppg original fluid, 11.0 ppg selected fluid, verified 520 psi shut-in drillpipe pressure, and recorded 400 psi reduced-rate circulating pressure. Initial circulating pressure is **920 psi**. Simplified final circulating pressure is 400 × 11/10 = **440 psi**. String capacity to the bit is 120 bbl and calibrated output is 0.10 bbl/stroke, so nominal travel is **1,200 strokes**. In this deliberately linear model, the change is 480 psi over 1,200 strokes, or 40 psi per 100 strokes. At 600 strokes the schedule value is **680 psi**.

This exercise does not specify real valve operations, pump startup transitions, choke adjustments, or safe pressure limits. Those must come from the approved simulator operating procedure and case data. The learner must distinguish calculating a schedule from safely executing it.

### Deviations during circulation

A sudden pump-pressure change can reflect rate change, restriction, washout, density arrival, gauge fault, or another condition. A change in casing pressure may reflect influx expansion or changes in the annular column. Identify the observations and follow the approved contingency rather than forcing the system to a memorized number. Do not continue simply to finish the calculated strokes if the well response contradicts the model.

### Practice

Using the same model, the pump is found to deliver 0.095 rather than 0.10 bbl/stroke. How many strokes move 120 bbl, and why does the discovery require plan review?

**Worked answer:** 120/0.095 = **1,263.16 strokes**, approximately 1,263 before operational rounding under the plan. The original schedule would predict arrival too early. Output, efficiency, actual volumes, and procedure-controlled rounding must be reconciled. Recalculate associated milestones; do not silently change only the final number.

## 8. Gas behaviour, weak formations, and constrained decisions

### Objectives

Explain why gas expansion and migration matter; distinguish pressure limits at different locations; reject unsupported solutions to losses and shallow influx.

Gas changes volume substantially as pressure changes. In an idealized constant-temperature, fixed-mass example, absolute pressure multiplied by volume remains constant. Real gases, temperature variation, solubility, phase behaviour, and friction complicate a well. The simple relationship teaches direction and scale, not a complete gas-handling prediction.

### Worked example: absolute pressure

A fictional 2-bbl gas volume at 3,000 psia would occupy **6 bbl** at 1,000 psia under the idealized constant-temperature assumption. Gauge pressure must not be used as though it were absolute. Near surface, a modest change in absolute pressure can cause a large proportional expansion. This helps explain why surface handling limits and controlled pressure management are central to a circulation plan.

Gas migration in a closed well can increase pressure. Containment does not mean that all pressures remain constant. Monitoring and the approved migration-management method remain necessary. Arbitrarily releasing pressure can reduce bottomhole support and admit more influx; arbitrarily allowing pressure to rise can exceed limits. Volumetric and other specialist methods require a separately specified procedure, validated data, and assessed personnel. Their purpose is pressure management when normal circulation is unavailable, not an invitation to invent a bleed schedule.

Formation integrity data constrain plans. A formation integrity test checks a specified pressure condition; a leak-off test explores the onset of leak-off under a defined test procedure. They are not interchangeable labels. Interpret results at the correct depth and with the test fluid, datum, uncertainty, and later changes considered. Maximum allowable surface pressure is not the equipment nameplate rating alone. Weak formation, casing, wellhead, preventer, lines, and operating configuration can each impose a lower limit.

### Worked example: simplified shoe constraint

For a classroom calculation only, assume a 6,000 ft TVD shoe, a verified 13.0 ppg equivalent formation limit at the shoe, and a uniform 10.0 ppg static column above it. The simple pressure difference is (13−10) × 0.052 × 6,000 = **936 psi**. This is a theoretical static difference for the stated model, not an approved operating limit. The actual plan must include applicable margins, dynamic losses, influx location, equipment limits, and the source and validity of the formation test. If a lower equipment operating limit is 800 psi, the higher theoretical number does not override it.

Shallow formations may not support conventional containment. A diverter routes flow according to a designed and approved arrangement; it does not kill the well and must not be mistaken for a universal shut-in substitute. Shallow gas or water flow needs a phase-specific plan, personnel protection, environmental considerations, and established emergency actions. The response selected for a deep cased section may be unsafe in a shallow section.

### Practice

A colleague proposes adding heavier fluid immediately after unexplained losses. Explain the decision problem.

**Worked answer:** Heavier fluid can increase formation loading and worsen losses. Lost column height may also reduce bottomhole support and permit influx elsewhere. Determine the actual barrier condition, observations, pressure window, and approved loss/influx contingency. “More weight” is not a complete diagnosis or safe universal response.

## 9. Subsea differences and managed-pressure interfaces

### Objectives

Explain subsea control and pressure-reference differences; identify riser and choke-line effects; distinguish managed-pressure equipment from the conventional well-control barrier system.

On a subsea installation, the preventer is remote from the surface crew. Control communication, hydraulic response, position indication, redundant systems, and emergency functions introduce additional verification needs. Response times and available functions must come from the installed system's documentation and approved procedures. A surface-style assumption about immediate response or accessible equipment can be wrong.

Long choke and kill lines have friction losses that can materially affect pressure relationships during circulation. The effect changes with rate and fluid. Surface readings must be interpreted with the actual route and datum. A pressure schedule that ignores choke-line friction may impose an unintended bottomhole pressure. At transitions, removing or adding a friction contribution is itself a pressure event requiring the approved method.

The marine riser is part of the fluid system but must not be treated as an unlimited well-control pressure vessel. Gas above the subsea barrier can affect the riser and surface handling even after the well below is contained. Changing a riser fluid column or disconnecting changes hydrostatic support. Riser margin, gas-in-riser response, disconnect decisions, and emergency sequences require the specific engineering basis and roles. They cannot be derived from a surface-only worksheet.

Managed pressure drilling uses a planned pressure-control system to manage the operating envelope. A rotating control device, dedicated choke system, and measurement system have defined limits and functions. Their presence does not establish that a kick can be handled indefinitely by that system or that the conventional BOP response is unnecessary. The interface plan defines normal operation, abnormal indicators, transition thresholds, responsibilities, and handover to the well-control response.

### Worked scenario: pressure disappears with a system change

A simulated MPD operation relies on 250 psi surface backpressure as part of its planned pressure balance. A proposed change removes that contribution. The correct review asks how the lost contribution will be addressed within the approved pressure envelope and transition procedure. The crew does not label the fluid weight alone “adequate” while ignoring the planned backpressure. If the MPD system fails, the predefined contingency and responsible authority govern, not an improvised attempt to recreate the previous gauge number.

### Practice

Identify three reasons a surface-stack circulation worksheet cannot simply be reused on a subsea case.

**Worked answer:** Examples include choke-line friction, remote equipment/control response and verification, different pressure references, riser fluid effects, gas above the stack, and emergency disconnect constraints. Remediation: draw the surface-to-stack flow path and label every pressure contribution that the surface-only worksheet omitted.

## 10. Supervising the operation and proving readiness

### Objectives

Build a coherent pre-operation record; evaluate a proposed response against uncertainties and limits; lead a handover; establish restart criteria after an event.

The supervisor's task is not merely to perform the driller's arithmetic faster. It is to ensure that the data, equipment, roles, method, limits, contingencies, and decision authority form a coherent plan. Before a phase change, verify the well schematic, casing and cement status, fluid properties and inventory, pressure window, relevant formation test data, equipment configuration and tests, calibrated pump output, current reduced-rate information where applicable, communication arrangements, and emergency response readiness.

Casing and cementing change the pressure system. Different fluids and spacers create changing hydrostatic columns. Displacement rates introduce friction; losses can alter the intended column; cement placement and verification determine whether a planned barrier actually exists. A successful volume displacement alone does not prove cement isolation. Require the applicable verification and barrier acceptance before relying on it in the next operation.

A useful pre-job review makes uncertainties visible. Record what is measured, calculated, assumed, or pending. Identify the operational decision each uncertainty affects. Establish stop or escalation triggers in the approved plan before the event. Do not create arbitrary thresholds merely to make a blank field look complete.

### Worked integrated case

The crew plans to resume after a simulated influx has been circulated. The volume record is reconciled, but a pressure indication remains unexplained and a barrier test failed during the exercise. “No more gas at surface” is insufficient. The supervisor keeps the operation in its prescribed safe state, assigns resolution of the pressure uncertainty and failed barrier, reviews whether the plan remains valid, and requires documented verification and authorized release before restart. The event record includes the observations, response, changed conditions, outstanding defects, responsible persons, and next decision point.

### Handover that supports the next decision

Use a structured record: well and phase; current physical state; barriers relied upon and their evidence; pressure/volume trends with time and units; equipment defects and restrictions; fluid condition; tasks underway; unresolved uncertainties; approved next action; authority for changes. The receiving person reads back the important restrictions. A handover is incomplete if the next shift must guess whether a number is a target, a limit, or an observation.

Human factors affect well control. Production pressure can make an uncertain signal seem inconvenient. Authority gradients can silence the person watching a trip tank. Assign specific monitoring roles, invite challenges, and require closed-loop communication for critical actions. During simulation, assess whether the leader responds constructively to a challenge, not only whether the calculated density is correct.

### Practice

Create a two-minute handover for an operation with a reconciled gain, unresolved transmitter fault, verified alternative monitoring, and a prohibited next phase pending engineering review.

**Worked answer:** The receiver must learn the current state, the fault, which independent monitoring is valid, who owns the engineering review, and that the next phase remains prohibited. A statement such as “all good, monitor it” fails because it deletes the restriction and responsibility. Remediation: have a second learner identify the next permitted action solely from the handover; revise if they cannot.

## 11. Calculation workshop: margins, placement, and uncertainty

### Objectives

Check a multi-step worksheet independently, distinguish arithmetic precision from measurement accuracy, evaluate fluid placement, and explain how a changed assumption changes the decision.

### A. Pressure gradient and formation-test interpretation

A pressure gradient expresses pressure per unit vertical distance. For uniform fluid in the customary units used here, multiply ppg by 0.052 to obtain psi/ft. For example, 11.2 ppg gives 0.5824 psi/ft. Multiplying this gradient by TVD reproduces the hydrostatic pressure. The numerical value is meaningless without units: 0.5824 psi/ft is not 0.5824 MPa/m.

Suppose a fictional formation test at a 5,000 ft TVD shoe used 10.0 ppg fluid and recorded 650 psi applied surface pressure at its specified evaluation point. The equivalent density of that test condition is 10 + 650/(0.052×5,000) = **12.5 ppg**. This calculation describes a test condition; whether it is an integrity target, leak-off point, valid test, or permitted design basis depends on the actual test record. The word “test” does not establish a fracture limit by itself.

With 10.5 ppg current fluid above the shoe, the corresponding simplified static pressure difference would be (12.5−10.5)×0.052×5,000 = **520 psi**. Changing the column to 11.5 ppg reduces that difference to **260 psi**. The denser column has consumed part of the pressure allowance. A supervisor who carries forward the old surface allowance unchanged can overload the formation. Applicable operating margins and dynamic effects must be applied through the approved plan; these classroom differences are not released operating limits.

### B. Fluid mixing and inventory

For an ideal additive-volume mixture of two compatible liquids, mass balance gives mixture density as the sum of each density times its volume divided by total volume. Real drilling-fluid preparation also involves compatibility, weighting material, rheology, temperature, solids, and mixing efficiency. The simple example is not a recipe for adding weighting solids.

Combine 40 bbl at 10 ppg and 60 bbl at 12 ppg in this ideal model. The density is (40×10 + 60×12)/100 = **11.2 ppg**. Adding 10 bbl of 8.5 ppg compatible liquid to that mixture gives (100×11.2 + 10×8.5)/110 = **10.9545 ppg** before reporting precision. A volume addition intended for another purpose has changed hydrostatic support. The fluid system needs verification after mixing; a mass-balance prediction is not a substitute for representative testing.

### C. Pill placement and U-tube reasoning

Consider two connected vertical legs containing fluids of different density. At equilibrium, pressure at the common connection must balance when other contributions are accounted for. A denser segment can drive a level change in the connected system. A return observed after placing a slug may have an explainable volume relationship, but it still must be predicted and reconciled; the word “slug” must not become an excuse to dismiss any gain.

In a deliberately simplified open, uniform geometry, place 20 bbl of 12 ppg fluid into a string initially containing 10 ppg fluid. The ideal total displaced return associated with the heavier slug's equilibrium is 20×12/10 = **24 bbl**; the additional return beyond the introduced 20 bbl is **4 bbl**. If string capacity is 0.02 bbl/ft, the ideal resulting level change corresponding to that additional volume is 4/0.02 = **200 ft**. These relationships depend on the stated geometry, connections, fluid behaviour, and final equilibrium. They are not a procedure for slugging a live well. Unexpected differences must be investigated rather than explained away.

The location of a dense segment also matters at intermediate depths. Two fluid arrangements can produce the same bottom pressure but different pressure at a weak formation above. A supervisor evaluates the full pressure profile during displacement, not only the final bottomhole number.

### D. Pump output, rate, and time

Calibrated volume per stroke multiplied by strokes per minute gives volume per minute. At 0.08 bbl/stroke and 30 strokes/minute, flow is **2.4 bbl/minute**. Moving 96 bbl nominally requires 1,200 strokes and 40 minutes at that unchanged rate and output. Efficiency changes, compressibility, leakage, and actual flow conditions can alter the relationship. Use the calibrated value and approved monitoring; do not substitute theoretical pump output when the case calls for measured output.

Some training models approximate friction pressure as proportional to the square of pump rate. If 400 psi corresponds to 40 strokes/minute, that model predicts 400×(30/40)² = **225 psi** at 30 strokes/minute. This is an approximation, not a universal rheological law. A measured reduced-rate pressure under relevant conditions is stronger evidence than an unqualified extrapolation. The planned startup and shutdown transitions must control the pressure effects as friction changes; a constant-rate schedule cannot simply be extended through a transition.

### E. Pressure force and restraint

Pressure acting over area produces force. A pressure difference of 1,000 psi across an effective circular area of 3 inches diameter corresponds to 1,000×π×3²/4 = approximately **7,069 lbf**. The effective area depends on the actual geometry and pressure path. This explains why apparently small pressurized areas can produce substantial forces. It does not establish the capacity of a restraint, a tubular, or a handling system. Engineering limits and the approved procedure govern.

### F. Rounding and decision quality

Arithmetic may produce many digits even when the measurements are coarse. Keep enough precision to avoid compounding errors, then use the rounding rules specified for the approved calculation system. Do not round a minimum required value downward or a maximum allowable value upward merely for convenience. Different training and operating systems specify particular conventions; record which applies. If the operating window is smaller than the uncertainty, more decimal places do not solve the problem.

### Integrated practice

A hypothetical 7,000 ft TVD well has original density 10.0 ppg, a verified 364 psi deficit, 320 psi original reduced-rate pressure, 84 bbl string capacity, and calibrated output 0.07 bbl/stroke. A separate shoe evaluation is at 4,000 ft TVD with a stipulated 12.0 ppg equivalent limit for this exercise. The current column above the shoe is uniform. Calculate the balancing density, simplified initial/final circulating pressures, strokes to bit, and the simplified static surface-pressure differences at the shoe before and after replacing the uniform column. State why none is an operating approval.

**Worked answer:** Density increase 364/(0.052×7,000)=1.0 ppg, giving **11.0 ppg**. Initial circulating pressure **684 psi**; simplified final **352 psi**. Travel **1,200 strokes**. Original shoe difference (12−10)×0.052×4,000 = **416 psi**; after replacement (12−11)×0.052×4,000 = **208 psi**. Different reference depths must remain distinct. Dynamic conditions, influx distribution, actual test basis, margins, equipment limits, and approved method are still required. Remediation: draw a depth-labelled pressure profile if you used bottom TVD in the shoe calculation.

## 12. Troubleshooting without inventing an operating procedure

### Objectives

Distinguish plausible causes of abnormal indications, identify discriminating evidence, and use the approved contingency while maintaining clear authority and records.

Troubleshooting during well control is not a trial-and-error search using live valves. Begin with the current safe-state requirements and the approved response to the observed deviation. Identify what changed: pump rate, output, fluid, valve lineup, equipment indication, volume, pressure, or sensor status. Assign observations without creating conflicting control commands. The supervisor considers candidate explanations and the evidence that would distinguish them; the driller continues only within the permitted response.

### Pump-pressure decrease

A decrease can accompany a lower rate, different fluid, a changed flow path, a washout, or a faulty reading. The event does not prove that the well is killed. Check the recorded rate and strokes, fluid arrival model, independent pressure information where available, and the approved equipment observations. Do not compensate blindly by changing choke pressure to recover the old number if the pressure loss is elsewhere. That could change bottomhole pressure in an unintended way.

### Pump-pressure increase

An increase can reflect a higher rate, changed rheology, plugging or restriction, different fluid, or a faulty indication. Compare the actual rate and relevant route to the planned model. Respect the operating limits and the prescribed response. “Pressure must rise because the fluid is heavier” is not enough when the change is abrupt or inconsistent with predicted arrival.

### Continuing flow or gain after an indicated response

Possible explanations include incomplete containment, another open path, flow from a volume above the barrier, compressibility/transient effects, or measurement error. None should be presumed harmless without the procedure-defined verification. A subsea case especially requires distinguishing flow below the stack from material already in the riser. Report the location and source of observations rather than simply saying “still flowing.”

### Pressure rise during a stationary period

Migration, temperature effects, trapped pressure behaviour, continued influx, or other conditions may be relevant. The correct response depends on the actual system. Preserve time-stamped measurements and follow the approved pressure-management contingency. Do not label a rising trace stabilized, and do not improvise a release sequence from a classroom gas-law calculation.

### Conflicting volume information

The active-pit display shows a gain while a separate calibrated tank shows no change. Check whether both observe the same inventory boundary. One may include a transfer that the other excludes. Check instrument health and the operation log. Inconsistency reduces confidence; it is not permission to select whichever reading supports continuation. Maintain the required response while the responsible team resolves the discrepancy.

### Worked case: competing explanations

During a training circulation, pump pressure drops 150 psi while indicated strokes remain constant. The predicted heavier-fluid arrival is still 300 strokes away. An operator says it must be normal density arrival. The driller reports the contradiction and follows the configured contingency. The supervisor asks for calibrated output/rate confirmation, pressure-source validation, and the modelled arrival record, while preserving the applicable pressure and volume limits. The proposed explanation is not accepted solely because it is familiar. The exercise succeeds when the team recognizes uncertainty and follows the correct response; it does not require guessing a hidden fault without evidence.

### Practice

Prepare a three-column troubleshooting record for an unexplained pressure rise: observation, possible explanation, and permitted evidence-gathering action. Include an explicit statement of who controls any change.

**Worked answer:** A suitable record might state “pressure rose from X to Y at these times, rate unchanged per logged source”; candidate explanations “restriction, fluid/rheology change, instrument error”; permitted checks “compare planned arrival and fluid record, independent approved reading, equipment indications under procedure.” The response must retain the configured limit and contingency, and name the driller/control authority. An answer proposing uncontrolled valve trials fails. Revisit the distinction between diagnosis and authorized action.

## 13. When the normal circulation model does not apply

### Objectives

Recognize off-bottom and noncirculating conditions; explain the purposes and limitations of specialist methods; identify the additional information required before choosing a method.

The conventional worksheet assumes a particular pressure connection, fluid distribution, and circulation path. A string above bottom, a plugged string, a failed circulation path, or a complex influx distribution can invalidate that model. A supervisor must name the invalid assumption rather than merely choose a different density from the same worksheet. A method appropriate to one condition can worsen another.

### Off-bottom conditions

If the string is above the influx or above the deepest exposed interval, the fluid column inside the string does not necessarily provide a direct description of the pressure at the bottom of the well. The lower open interval, influx position, fluid gradients, and connectivity matter. A familiar drillpipe formula can be numerically tidy and physically inapplicable. Plot the string end and suspected influx on the schematic. Establish what each gauge can actually communicate with and which volumes will circulate through the proposed route.

The decision to move pipe under pressure introduces additional barriers, displacement, pressure forces, and equipment requirements. It is not a routine extension of the trip-volume calculation. A stripping or snubbing operation needs its own engineered plan, compatible barriers and handling equipment, trained personnel, pressure/volume control, and contingency. This course's calculation of pressure force explains one hazard; it does not authorize the operation.

### Volumetric pressure management

When normal circulation is unavailable, a planned volumetric method can manage the pressure consequences of migrating gas by controlling the relationship between pressure change and liquid volume. The essential reasoning is that removing liquid changes hydrostatic support while migrating gas changes pressure distribution. Both effects must be managed; releasing pressure until a gauge looks comfortable can reduce bottomhole support and admit more influx.

For a purely geometric classroom relationship in a uniform vertical annulus, liquid height is volume divided by capacity. Suppose capacity is 0.05 bbl/ft and liquid density is 10 ppg. A 1-bbl liquid-height equivalent is 20 ft and its hydrostatic equivalent is 0.052×10×20 = **10.4 psi**. This relationship helps interpret why measured liquid volume matters. It does **not** specify a bleed increment, operating margin, allowable pressure, or field sequence. In a real annulus, changing geometry, gas position, inclination, fluid compressibility, and pressure limits require the validated method and calculations. The instructor must not convert this example into an unsupervised operating drill.

### Lubrication concepts and gas replacement

A lubrication-and-bleed concept introduces liquid and allows it to replace part of a gas-filled space under a controlled pressure-management plan. Liquid must actually reach the intended position before its hydrostatic contribution is credited. Time, segregation, gas behaviour, and geometry matter. Treating pumped volume as immediate verified hydrostatic replacement can produce an unsafe pressure decision. This is a specialist operation requiring an approved plan and competent personnel, not a generic fallback when circulation is inconvenient.

### Bullheading concepts

Bullheading attempts to force fluid into a formation rather than circulate the influx to surface through the usual route. It can expose weak formations and equipment to significant pressure and can move fluid into an unintended interval. Feasibility depends on formation behaviour, injectivity, barriers, fluid compatibility, environmental consequences, and pressure limits. A blocked circulation path alone is not proof that bullheading is appropriate. The supervisor must involve the designated engineering and well-control authority.

### Kick tolerance and casing decisions

Kick tolerance concerns the influx condition that can be handled within the planned system's constraints. It depends on where the influx is, its properties and expansion, pressure at weak exposed formations, fluid properties, well geometry, and the selected method. A single allowable surface-pressure number is not a complete kick-tolerance model. The same influx volume at different locations or with different fluid properties can impose different loads.

Casing-seat design and verified cement barriers influence which formations are exposed and which paths can communicate. A pressure window assessed before drilling another interval may not remain adequate after exposing a weaker or higher-pressure formation. Supervisors should know the plan's assumptions and escalation triggers; the model is reassessed by the responsible engineering team when those assumptions change.

### Worked decision case

A training string is 1,500 ft above bottom. A suspected influx is below the string end, and the normal circulation path is unavailable. A colleague proposes using the standard at-bottom kill sheet and then moving pipe down “to make the sheet true.” The supervisor rejects both unsupported steps. The current barrier and pressure state are maintained under the approved contingency. The schematic records the off-bottom interval and uncertain influx location. Specialist options are evaluated with the responsible authority, using actual pressure limits, equipment capability, and method-specific requirements. The correct lesson is to recognize the model boundary before a calculation becomes an instruction.

### Practice

For the uniform classroom annulus above, what hydrostatic equivalent corresponds to 2.5 bbl of liquid height? Why must it not be used as a field pressure-release instruction?

**Worked answer:** 2.5/0.05 = 50 ft; 0.052×10×50 = **26 psi**. The calculation contains no validated gas position, geometry changes, operational margins, pressure limits, or authorized sequence. It describes a liquid-height relationship only. Remediation: label every missing operating input instead of supplying guessed values.

## 14. Human response, drills, and learning from an event

### Objectives

Prepare role-specific drills, recognize communication failure, preserve personnel safety during well-control events, and turn a debrief into measurable improvement.

Well control is a team activity conducted around equipment, pressure, noise, and potentially hazardous releases. The person recording volume, the driller controlling the console, the supervisor evaluating the plan, and personnel managing fluid or gas handling need a common picture. A rapid response with conflicting instructions is not coordinated control.

Before a drill, define its objective, configuration, participant roles, start condition, stop condition, and who may inject faults. Tell participants how to distinguish the drill from a real emergency. Do not use a live well as a surprise training device. Simulator exercises permit repetition without exposing people or formations to an intentional influx.

Good drills vary the activity: drilling, connections, tripping, equipment out of service, and shift handover. The learner must recognize the applicable response, not memorize one script regardless of context. Measure recognition, communication, correct use of the supplied procedure, verification, record quality, and handling of uncertainty. Speed matters only together with correct action; a fast wrong sequence is not competence.

### Personnel protection

A well-control event may involve gas, fire, pressure release, or hazardous fluid. Personnel must follow the site alarm, exclusion, muster, and emergency plan. Gas may be toxic as well as flammable. An unqualified person must not enter a suspect atmosphere to inspect a leak or retrieve a casualty. The designated emergency organization manages rescue and medical response. The driller's control task does not cancel the need to protect the rest of the crew.

### Communication under stress

Use names or roles, a single clear instruction, and read-back of critical values or actions. When a message is not understood, say so. Avoid vague phrases such as “open it a little” without a named control and authorized procedure context. The receiving operator must know which equipment and which approved step is intended. If conflicting instructions arrive, resolve control authority while maintaining the prescribed safe state.

### Debrief with evidence

Separate what happened from why participants think it happened. Start with the time-stamped log and simulator record. Identify the first cue, who saw it, what they communicated, the response, verification, and any deviation. Ask what information was missing and whether the procedure, interface, training, or coordination contributed. Assign a specific improvement, owner, and reassessment condition. “Be more careful” is not an actionable corrective measure.

### Worked debrief

In a drill, the trip-tank observer reported a discrepancy, but the driller heard only “tank issue.” The supervisor then saw a volume gain without knowing the earlier observation. The improvement is not to tell the observer to shout louder. Rehearse a standard report including expected volume, actual volume, current activity, and requested acknowledgment. The driller repeats the discrepancy and confirms the response. The next drill tests whether the information reaches the supervisor accurately. The team retains the first failed observation record and the later successful reassessment instead of rewriting history.

### Practice

Write one measurable corrective action for a learner who repeatedly states that a closure command proves containment.

**Worked answer:** “Before reassessment, learner will explain and demonstrate the configured procedure's command, position, and containment-verification steps in two changed simulator cases without prompting, with the assessor recording each observation.” This targets the actual misunderstanding. “Review safety” does not.

## 15. Formation and fluid behaviour that can mislead the crew

### Objectives

Distinguish porosity from permeability; explain why fluid and temperature changes complicate detection; recognize breathing and hydrate hypotheses without using them to dismiss danger.

Porosity describes the fraction of rock volume occupied by pores. Permeability describes how readily fluid can move through connected pathways. A porous rock need not transmit fluid rapidly if its pores are poorly connected. Conversely, fractures can provide a flow path through rock whose matrix has low permeability. Sandstone commonly has pore networks between grains; carbonate flow may be strongly affected by fractures or dissolution features; fine-grained shale may have low matrix permeability yet still contain pressure and fracture pathways. The actual formation behaviour must be established from the well's evidence rather than a rock-name shortcut.

An influx may contain water, oil, gas, or a mixture. Fluid properties affect density, expansion, solubility, and surface hazards. A water influx can still cause serious loss of control; absence of a gas alarm does not prove absence of influx. A low-permeability interval or small underbalance may produce a slow gain, so an inconspicuous initial rate is not proof that continuation is safe.

Drilling fluids are engineered systems. Density supports pressure control, while rheology influences transport, suspension, and friction. Cuttings, formation fluids, dilution, solids additions, and temperature changes can alter their behaviour. Compare samples from meaningful locations and times. A suction-pit reading describes fluid entering the system; return fluid provides different information after exposure downhole. Record where the sample came from, how it was measured, and the operating condition. A single convenient sample should not stand in for the entire circulating system.

Gas can dissolve in a nonaqueous fluid under conditions where an obvious free-gas volume is not yet seen. As conditions change, gas can come out of solution and affect observed volume and handling requirements. The detection and response plan must account for the actual fluid system. A water-based intuition applied unchanged to an oil-based system can be misleading. Temperature and pressure also affect density and rheology; a cold subsea line and a warm active system may not behave identically.

### Breathing is a hypothesis, not a waiver

Some formations take fluid under a higher circulating pressure and return some of it when pressure falls. This behaviour is often called wellbore breathing or ballooning. It can resemble influx evidence. A previous breathing event does not establish that the next flow is harmless. Follow the approved response to unexplained flow and evaluate the evidence against the specific breathing criteria. Do not invent a universal amount or duration that may be ignored.

### Hydrates and trapped pressure

Under suitable pressure, temperature, water and gas conditions, hydrates can obstruct a flow path. A blockage can isolate pressure from a gauge. A zero reading on one side does not prove that the other side is depressurized. A suspected plug may move if pressure is changed, creating a projectile or sudden-release hazard. Stop unauthorized dismantling, protect personnel from the line of fire, and use the approved engineered isolation and remediation plan. Do not heat, strike, open, or bleed a suspected blockage based on an improvised classroom rule.

### Worked case

After circulation stops, a small return flow appears. One person calls it breathing; another notes that the fluid system recently changed and the volume measurement is uncertain. The correct response preserves the unexplained-flow concern and follows the approved procedure while the responsible team reviews the evidence. The supervisor records the earlier formation behaviour as context, not a diagnosis. If a line later shows inconsistent pressures and possible blockage, personnel protection and the approved isolation plan take priority over opening a fitting to “see whether pressure remains.”

### Practice

Explain why the statement “no gas detected, so no kick” fails in two different ways.

**Worked answer:** An influx may be liquid, and gas detection can also be affected by fluid behaviour, transport, sensor limitations, and where measurement occurs. Monitor pressure, flow, volume and context under the plan. Remediation: identify at least two independent observations that would remain relevant when a gas reading is absent.

## 16. Readiness at the monitoring and control panels

### Objectives

Verify monitoring boundaries and alarms; interpret control-system evidence; distinguish a familiar panel from a ready system.

Before the operation, match the active fluid system to the monitoring system. The pit volume totalizer must include the intended active pits and exclude unrelated inventory in the way the approved plan specifies. The pump suction and return lineup must correspond to the physical and displayed configuration. A correct instrument on the wrong inventory boundary can provide a misleading total.

An alarm requires a justified threshold, a functioning measurement, an enabled alert path, and a defined response. The actual high/low values come from the approved operating plan and the system's capabilities; a course should not invent one universal barrel threshold. In a configured simulator, the learner enters the supplied values, reads them back, and demonstrates that the test signal reaches the intended observer. Silencing an alarm is not correction of its cause. Record any temporary suppression under the applicable authorization and restore it as required; never leave a safety function silently disabled at handover.

Floating-rig movement, changes in vessel condition, inventory transfers, and measurement geometry can affect apparent levels. The appropriate compensation or monitoring method must be understood. A noisy trace should trigger investigation of measurement reliability and approved alternatives, not wholesale disabling of alarms.

### Hydraulic control readiness

The closing unit supplies operating fluid and energy through its configured system. Accumulators store hydraulic energy with a gas charge; charging equipment, regulators, valves and lines must support the intended functions. Correct gas type, precharge, maintenance and test methods are manufacturer-specific and must be handled by qualified personnel. The learner is not authorized to service a pressurized accumulator by reading this course.

Different indications answer different questions. A control light may show a selected command. A pressure gauge may indicate available supply or a function circuit. A flow indication may show fluid movement. Position feedback may indicate a component's state. Understand the installed panel legend rather than treating every green light as proof of a sealed well. Loss of power, supply pressure, communication or feedback requires the configured response and escalation.

### Drillstring safety equipment

Equipment intended to control flow through the string must be compatible with the connection, bore, pressure, handling arrangement and operating condition. Its accessibility and readiness matter before an event. A valve that cannot be safely installed or operated in the intended condition is not a usable contingency simply because it is somewhere on the rig. Check readiness under the approved procedure; do not attempt improvised installation on a flowing string based on a generic illustration.

### Worked panel exercise

The simulator's approved active inventory is Pits A and B. The displayed total includes A and C. Its high alarm is disabled, and a recent transfer was not logged. The learner identifies the mismatch before accepting a stable total as proof of normal conditions. Under the training procedure, the learner reconciles the physical lineup, selects the correct monitored inventory, enters and verifies the supplied alarm settings, tests the alert path, and records the transfer discrepancy. The assessment includes checking that the corrected configuration persists through handover.

### Practice

A BOP panel shows a close-command light, available supply pressure, and no reliable position feedback. Write a truthful state report.

**Worked answer:** “Close command selected; indicated supply pressure available; position feedback unreliable; containment not yet verified by the required observations.” Follow the installed procedure's contingency. Remediation: identify which indication supports each clause, and remove any clause supported only by assumption.

## 17. Surface handling and subsea emergency functions

### Objectives

Explain separator seal failure, distinguish emergency control functions, and identify the evidence needed to use an equipment contingency.

### Gas separation is a constrained process

A mud-gas separator separates gas from returning liquid within its designed flow and pressure envelope. In a liquid-seal arrangement, a column of liquid in the outlet path resists gas passage into the liquid-return route. If the pressure relationship overwhelms that seal, gas can enter an area intended for liquid handling. This is commonly described as blow-through. A functioning upstream choke does not eliminate this downstream hazard.

Seal condition, fluid density, outlet geometry, gas flow, vent resistance, and maintenance affect the system. A partially obstructed vent can increase backpressure. Gas or light fluid in the liquid leg can alter its effective hydrostatic resistance. The operating envelope must come from the actual engineered design and verified condition, not the apparent vessel size. Monitor the specified pressure and seal indications, maintain the required flow path, and follow the response to approaching limits. Do not bypass protective restrictions to keep a circulation schedule on time.

For a classroom illustration only, a uniform 10 ppg liquid head of 10 vertical feet corresponds to **5.2 psi**. This shows that relatively modest gas-side pressure can matter to a liquid seal. It is not the operating limit of any actual separator. Manufacturer limits, design backpressure, instrumentation, fluid changes and margins remain required.

### Rotating control devices

An RCD provides a seal around the rotating/moving drillstring within its design envelope, with a bearing/sealing arrangement and housing appropriate to the product. It helps direct returns into a controlled path. Seal wear, compatible pipe dimensions, rotational/movement limits, pressure and temperature ratings, lubrication/cooling requirements, and maintenance affect performance. A pressure-rated component is not automatically qualified for every dynamic condition. Use the installed product's documentation; a photograph or generic name cannot establish its limits.

### Distinct subsea emergency functions

Emergency disconnect, deadman, and autoshear refer to different functions or initiating conditions in a subsea control arrangement. Broadly, an emergency disconnect sequence is a configured emergency response involving well security and separation; deadman functionality responds to specified losses of control capability; autoshear functionality responds to a specified separation condition. Their exact logic, arming, sequence, available energy and final state depend on the installed design. They are not three interchangeable buttons or a guarantee that every failure produces the desired result.

The supervisor needs the approved cause-and-effect description, current armed/available state, test and maintenance evidence, responsible authority, and the consequences for well and riser pressure. Do not copy a trigger threshold or sequence from another installation. Emergency operation must account for compatible tubular position and system capability. After a command or automatic event, the team verifies the resulting state through the available evidence and follows the emergency plan.

An ROV interface can provide an intervention route where designed. It requires the correct interface, tooling, accessible location, compatible operating parameters, trained team, and coordinated procedure. Its existence on a drawing does not prove readiness or immediate availability. The rig team must communicate the actual required function and current well state to the intervention authority.

### Worked case

A circulation plan is within the pump's capability, but the separator's seal indication is unreliable and the vent route has a known restriction. The supervisor does not infer safe gas-handling capacity from pump capacity. The plan remains subject to resolution of the separator defects and verification of its permitted envelope. In a separate subsea exercise, a control-system failure occurs while a tubular component with uncertain shear compatibility is at the stack. The supervisor reports both facts and uses the installed emergency procedure; the uncertainty must not be erased by saying “deadman will handle it.”

### Practice

Explain why an RCD, a BOP, and a mud-gas separator must not be treated as substitutes for one another.

**Worked answer:** They perform different functions in different parts of the system: directing/controlling returns around a moving string within an RCD envelope, providing configured well barriers through BOP functions, and separating/handling returning gas and liquid within separator limits. Each has its own verification and failure consequences. Remediation: trace the flow and barrier path and label each component's function.

## 18. Planning transitions, margins, and the end of a circulation

### Objectives

Explain pressure effects during transitions, avoid double-counting margins, evaluate delayed response, and require appropriate post-circulation verification.

### Startup and shutdown are not constant-rate conditions

During a pump transition, friction changes with flow. A plan that controls bottomhole pressure at a steady rate must also specify how the transition is controlled. The driller, choke operator and supervisor need agreed roles, communication and expected observations. The applicable surface or subsea procedure accounts for the route's pressure contributions. It cannot be replaced by immediately setting the steady-state calculated drillpipe pressure before the intended rate and flow condition exist.

Startup readings may differ from a simple predicted value because fluid properties, gel behaviour, rate, temperature, instrumentation or restrictions differ from the recorded condition. The response is to use the approved acceptance and deviation process, not silently redefine every discrepancy as normal. Likewise, shutdown readings must be interpreted with the actual state; a number different from the initial shut-in reading is not automatically proof of a new influx or a successful kill.

### Response lag and overcorrection

A change at a control point may take time to appear at a remote measurement because the system has volume, compressibility, propagation and instrument response characteristics. Repeated large adjustments before observing the previous adjustment's effect can cause oscillation or overshoot. In a configured simulator, learn the documented control method and expected response, make only authorized changes, and observe the system as required. Do not invent a universal waiting time; the correct response depends on the installation and condition.

### Margins are part of one pressure budget

A margin is intended to address defined uncertainty or operational variation. It is not free additional pressure. If extra fluid density and extra surface pressure are both added without recognizing their combined effect, the formation can be loaded more than intended.

For an original classroom example, a 0.2 ppg density increment over 10,000 ft TVD contributes **104 psi**. Adding another 100 psi surface contribution creates a combined **204 psi** addition in the simple static model. If the plan's allowable additional pressure budget at the relevant location is 150 psi, the combined proposal fails that classroom check even though each person thought their individual addition was small. Actual margins require the complete depth-dependent pressure profile and responsible engineering approval.

### Change management during the event

Wrong fluid, unavailable weighting material, power failure, deteriorating weather, equipment leakage, sensor failure or a change in personnel capability can invalidate a plan. A pre-agreed stop or escalation point prevents the crew from improvising under pressure. Record the changed assumption, current state, authority, revised plan, and the verification needed before continuation. The pressure of lost time is not new technical evidence.

### Completion must be verified

Reaching the planned stroke count does not alone prove that the intended fluid reached every relevant location or that the well is ready to reopen. Account for actual output, volumes, fluid properties, pressure behaviour and the applicable verification procedure. In a subsea system, fluid above the stack and trapped gas in stack spaces require explicit attention before a change in barrier state. Opening a barrier can expose a different pressure and gas-handling condition even when the deeper well appears balanced.

The final decision belongs to the designated authority using the approved procedure and recorded evidence. Document the resulting barrier state, restrictions, defects and monitoring plan. Preserve the event data for review rather than replacing it with a single “killed” label.

### Worked case

The team reaches its predicted strokes-to-bit and final circulating pressure, but actual pump output was uncertain for part of the operation. A separate compartment still contains older fluid, and an unexplained pressure remains. The supervisor treats the calculated milestone as one observation, not a completed verification. The team follows the configured verification and resolves uncertainties before any authorized barrier change. A changed pressure state after stopping circulation must be interpreted, not forced to match the old worksheet.

### Practice

Two people independently propose margins: 0.1 ppg over 8,000 ft TVD and 80 psi surface pressure. Calculate their combined contribution in the simple model and explain the planning error if the approved additional budget is 100 psi.

**Worked answer:** 0.052×0.1×8,000 = 41.6 psi; combined with 80 psi gives **121.6 psi**, exceeding the stipulated 100 psi budget by 21.6 psi. The error is failing to combine pressure contributions within one reviewed plan. The budget in this exercise is fictional, not a universal field value.

# Drilling well control — role and equipment lessons

VUC007. These four lessons extend the embedded eighteen-chapter foundation. All cases are original and fictional. Use paper or configured simulators, never live well controls. The course awards only the specified VNDRLY learning record after actual assessment; it is not an IWCF certificate.

## R19 — Read the equipment drawing before trusting a control label

The first task is to identify what each component can do in the present geometry. An annular sealing element and a ram assembly are different mechanisms. A pipe ram must suit its specified tubular geometry; a blind closure cannot be assumed usable across an occupied bore. Shearing capability and subsequent sealing depend on the installed design and the object at the cutting position. The manufacturer's documentation supplies these capabilities. A generic BOP name does not.

SLB's [BOP explanation](https://www.slb.com/-/media/files/oilfield-review/defining-bops.ashx) describes annular and ram mechanisms. Use it to understand function, then use the actual equipment specification for limits. Do not infer that an annular element seals every possible shape, that every shear cuts every tool, or that a component's successful movement proves pressure sealing.

### Original functional drawing

```text
                      tubular through bore
                               |
                     sealing function A
                               |
                     sealing function B ---- controlled outlet C
                               |                     |
                     isolation function D     flow restriction E
                               |                     |
                          well source          processing route F

                  hydraulic supply H --> controls A/B/D
                  position feedback J <-- controlled functions
```

This is a functional model, not a recommended stack order. Learners trace the pressure path from the source to every exposed outlet. H supplies actuation energy; J supplies an indication. Neither is a pressure barrier merely because it appears on the drawing. F must be available and suitable for the expected stream before a controlled-flow plan can rely on it.

**Worked case:** B is verified for a 5-inch pipe body. The position record places a 6.5-inch tool joint at B, with no qualified tool-joint sealing capability supplied. A command to close B cannot create that missing capability. The learner identifies the conflict and uses the configured contingency, preserving the correct containment state. Moving the string without assessing the movement's pressure and load consequences is not an acceptable improvised cure.

### Gauges, valves and flow direction

A pressure gauge measures at its communicating point. An isolated or blocked gauge can display a value unrelated to the volume about to be opened. Trace its connection on the drawing. For any controlled flow, distinguish the upstream pressure-containing path, restriction and downstream handling. A hose downstream of a restriction may see a different pressure during normal flow than during a blockage or lineup change. The approved envelope must consider credible states, not just normal operation.

**Practice:** C's position indicator shows CLOSED, but the test evidence does not identify the current assembly and a bypass around C is open. Identify two independent defects. **Answer:** closure indication is not applicable sealing evidence, and the bypass defeats the assumed isolated route. Correcting one does not resolve the other. Draw the bypass and record both issues.

### Drillstring internal controls

The bore inside the drillstring is a separate path from the annulus. An outside seal does not close the inside bore. Appropriate internal control tools must match the connection, pressure/flow envelope and approved installation/use method. A tool stored nearby is not proof it can be deployed under the present condition. Preparation includes access, compatibility, role assignment and the approved contingency. Do not attempt an improvised installation against uncontrolled flow.

**Practice:** the crew has the correct outside sealing element but an incompatible internal safety tool. **Answer:** both flow paths must be addressed; a correct outside barrier cannot compensate for an unmanageable internal path. Resolve compatibility before crediting readiness.

## R20 — Casing and cementing change the well-control problem

Running casing changes displacement, flow area and the geometry that pressure-control equipment must accommodate. The crew must know the actual casing size, connections, running tools and available sealing arrangements. A control configured for drillpipe is not automatically suitable for casing or a running assembly. The fluid system must account for displacement, filling and returns throughout movement.

### Worked volume and pressure case

The exercise supplies an approved closed-end displacement of 0.012 bbl/ft for a fictional assembly. Running 300 ft displaces 3.6 bbl. The measured return is 5.1 bbl and no transfer is logged. The 1.5 bbl discrepancy requires the prescribed response and investigation. It does not establish the cause by itself. Do not label it “normal casing displacement” without reconciling the supplied expectation and actual observations.

A different exercise uses 4,000 vertical feet of 10 ppg liquid above 1,000 vertical feet of 14 ppg slurry. Under the explicit static liquid-column assumption, head is 0.052 × (10 × 4,000 + 14 × 1,000) = **2,808 psi**. A single 10 ppg assumption would give 2,600 psi and miss 208 psi. During actual placement, moving interfaces, friction, losses and changing material properties make the pressure profile time-dependent. The worksheet is not a cementing design.

### Float equipment and confirmation

Float equipment provides a specified check function intended to limit reverse flow after displacement. The [SLB float-collar definition](https://glossary.slb.com/en/terms/f/float_collar) explains that function. Presence of a float collar is not proof of its condition or successful operation. If observations suggest reverse flow or an unexpected pressure change, preserve the evidence and follow the approved contingency rather than assuming the valve must be working.

**Original case:** the planned displacement finishes, but the measured returned volume differs from the worksheet. Someone proposes changing the recorded strokes to match the intended total. That would conceal the actual evidence. Record actual delivered and returned volumes, the discrepancy, pressure history and response. Establish what the evidence supports about placement and containment before accepting the job.

### Material transition and the barrier claim

Cement slurry changes as it sets. Its contribution cannot always be treated as a static liquid head throughout that transition. BSEE's [bottom-hole pressure research](https://www.bsee.gov/sites/bsee.gov/files/tap-technical-assessment-program/008ca.pdf) discusses pressure behavior during cement setting. A completed pumping action is not proof of the intended permanent barrier. The approved program supplies waiting, monitoring and verification criteria applicable to the actual material and well.

**Practice:** a coworker says, “We pumped the correct amount, so the barrier is proven.” **Answer:** amount is one piece of evidence. Placement, condition, isolation and required verification remain distinct. The correct closeout records those results rather than substituting elapsed time or a signature.

## R21 — The observer, operator and supervisor have different jobs

A field-team learner must recognize meaningful evidence, report it promptly and remain within the assigned role. An operator must additionally use the configured response, monitor the actual result and transfer accurate information. A supervisor must evaluate the operating envelope, direct the team, interpret deviations and decide whether the next phase is justified. Titles do not supply competence; the assessment must observe the relevant behavior.

### One event, three role responses

The fictional log shows a sudden drilling-rate change, a return-flow increase and an unexplained active-volume gain. The field-team observer reports the well, time, readings and activity without waiting to prove the mechanism. The operator applies the supplied detection/response procedure and confirms the indicated equipment state. The supervisor checks the reliable shut-in data and the available pressure/volume model before authorizing the next operation. All three preserve uncertainty; none should invent a stabilized pressure simply because the form has an empty box.

**Weak observer response:** “The well is kicking because the tank looks high.” It blends an observation with a diagnosis. **Improved response:** “At 10:14 the active volume increased by the measured amount shown in the log while no transfer was authorized; return flow also increased. I reported the discrepancy and the operator is executing the response procedure.” The message gives usable evidence and action without overstating the cause.

### Operator handover

The operator hands over the current configuration, actual indications, readings with times/units, string position, volume account, actions taken, pending commands and unresolved issues. A requested closure must not be reported as verified isolation until its required verification exists. The supervisor repeats the controlling restrictions so both parties share the same state.

**Practice:** a close command was sent, its position indicator is pending and a pressure trend is still changing. Write the handover. **Model:** “Close commanded at the recorded time; position not yet confirmed. Readings are changing and not accepted as stabilized. Current restrictions remain. Verification/contingency is in progress under the supplied procedure.”

### Supervisor decisions and instructions

Before directing a modeled circulation, state its objective, limits, rate reference, pressure reference, volume checkpoints, communication responsibilities and deviation response. A good instruction is specific enough to be checked. “Keep it safe” is not a control instruction. The operator repeats the instruction; the supervisor tracks the achieved state and any delay rather than assuming instant response.

**Original simulator case:** the approved classroom schedule gives target 520 psi at the current step. The observed value is 590 psi and the model's tolerance is ±10 psi. The supervisor must identify the unexplained mismatch and call the model's hold/verification response. They must not invent a diagnosis or instruct repeated corrections without understanding the resulting pressure distribution. The assessor changes roles on a fresh attempt so memorizing a sentence cannot conceal misunderstanding.

The real equipment's procedure defines the actual control movements. This material teaches the required decision, evidence and communication and supplies independent simulation tasks; it does not invent a universal valve sequence.

## R22 — Surface and subsea cases must preserve their different reference points

A surface pressure reading is not automatically the pressure at a subsea wellhead or at a formation. Hydrostatic contributions, line friction and the locations of restrictions matter. Mark the reference datum on the schematic and calculation sheet. Distinguish the water column outside equipment from the drilling fluid inside the connected flow path; they do not have the same density or necessarily serve the same function.

### Original depth-reference exercise

The model supplies a formation 8,000 vertical feet below the surface reference, a subsea wellhead 2,000 vertical feet below that reference, and a continuous 10 ppg liquid column in the modeled well path. Its total head to the formation is 4,160 psi; the modeled contribution from wellhead to formation is 3,120 psi. Confusing the 6,000-ft below-wellhead interval with the full column loses 1,040 psi in the arithmetic. The exercise does not prescribe the actual riser/return configuration or seawater contribution.

### Changing a return route

At the same model rate, return route A contributes 180 psi pressure loss above a chosen reference point; route B contributes 80 psi. With other terms fixed, the modeled pressure at that reference differs by 100 psi. Copying a pressure schedule across the route change would miss that difference. The approved procedure must account for the transition, measurements, line condition and limits. Do not simply add or subtract a generic “subsea correction” without its model.

**Practice:** route B becomes restricted and its verified model loss rises to 140 psi. **Answer:** compared with its previous state, the pressure contribution increases by 60 psi at that rate under the stated model. This is a changed condition requiring interpretation; it is not permission to turn a control by an inferred amount.

### Emergency functions and actual scope

Emergency shutdown, disconnect and backup actuation functions have different purposes and configuration-dependent effects. A command may involve tool-position restrictions, energy availability, barriers and subsequent well status. The operator must know what the actual assigned system does, who commands it and what confirms the result. A classroom explanation of these functions cannot qualify an unassessed subsea console.

**Original case:** the planned response assumes a qualified cut-and-seal capability, but the tracked position places a nonshearable component at the relevant plane. The response assumption is invalid for that state. Record the limitation and follow the approved contingency. Neither a green panel light nor the existence of a backup button changes the geometry.

For a combined surface/subsea endorsement, the learner must complete the specified configured subsea simulation as well as the common instruction. A surface-only learner still studies the differences so they recognize the boundary, but their record must state surface-only. Do not award combined scope by attendance at the same lecture.

# Drilling Well Control — practical evaluation packet

These original cases are training instruments. Do not connect them to live controls. Assessment outcomes are not prefilled. This packet specifies tasks and observable criteria; equipment-specific console sequences must come from the supplied simulator procedure.

## Common assessor instructions

Identify learner, pathway, assessor, date, case version, and equipment configuration. Brief the learner on simulator controls, emergency stop, allowed references, and role boundaries. Demonstrate interface use before assessment; assessment is not a test of guessing an unfamiliar interface. Provide the case data and procedure without the answer key. Record time-stamped actions and words, not impressions such as “seemed confident.”

Score each criterion 0 = not demonstrated/incorrect, 1 = demonstrated after prompting, 2 = demonstrated independently. Every safety-critical criterion must score 2 on the final attempt. All other criteria must score at least 1, with an overall score of at least 80%. A prompt during practice is teaching; during assessment it must be recorded. A critical error stops the assessed attempt. Reassessment uses changed numerical inputs or a different fault, not immediate repetition of a memorized answer.

Required tools: calculator, blank calculation and event forms, well schematic, fluid/volume table, equipment and test records, approved simulator procedure, and configured training simulator for operational endorsements. Tabletop-only performance cannot earn a console endorsement.

## Station A — pressure and volume worksheet

Applicable: introductory, driller, supervisor. Awareness learners discuss the decision meaning without a calculation endorsement.

### Learner data and tasks

Case A is a synthetic vertical conventional surface well: TVD 8,000 ft; measured depth 9,600 ft; uniform original fluid 10.0 ppg; verified stabilized drillpipe pressure 416 psi; pressure connection unobstructed; string at bottom. Recorded reduced-rate pressure with original fluid is 360 psi. The exercise model assumes friction scales with density. String internal volume is 100 bbl; calibrated pump output is 0.10 bbl/stroke. The supplied linear schedule is a training simplification. No operational equipment limits are provided in this calculation-only case.

1. Draw the pressure contributions and state the datum and units.
2. Calculate original hydrostatic pressure, inferred formation pressure under the stated assumptions, balancing density, simplified initial/final circulating pressures, and strokes to bit.
3. Calculate the midpoint pressure in the supplied linear model.
4. State why the worksheet cannot authorize circulation on a real well.
5. Independently check another learner's worksheet and identify an intentional measured-depth substitution planted by the assessor.

### Assessor key and criteria

Hydrostatic 4,160 psi; inferred formation pressure 4,576 psi; balancing density 11.0 ppg; initial circulating pressure 776 psi; simplified final 396 psi; 1,000 strokes; midpoint 586 psi. Accept arithmetic rounding within 1 psi and 0.01 ppg where appropriate, with correct units. The learner must flag the missing pressure window, equipment limits, actual hydraulics, approved method, and procedure. **Critical:** does not authorize real operation from this worksheet; does not use MD for hydrostatic support. Other criteria: accurate equations, units, independent check, transparent assumptions, clear explanation.

## Station B — trip discrepancy and recognition

Applicable: all pathways; awareness role reports and withdraws from operational control decisions.

### Case and injects

The approved exercise displacement for the current pipe condition is 0.006 bbl/ft. The crew has pulled 600 ft. Expected fill is 3.6 bbl, but the calibrated trip tank records 1.4 bbl. There is no authorized transfer. At the next inject, a colleague says the tank “always reads low.” At the final inject, the normal pit display reports a sensor fault.

The learner states the discrepancy, identifies the potential influx concern, communicates to the responsible role, and follows the supplied exercise response. Operational candidates execute the appropriate configured simulator response; awareness candidates must not operate controls. The assessor supplies the actual procedure before the exercise and records the version used.

### Criteria

Identifies 2.2-bbl shortfall; does not dismiss it because of informal reassurance; checks independent observations; preserves sensor fault; uses closed-loop communication; follows role and procedure. **Critical:** does not continue pulling simply to average out the discrepancy; does not invent a transfer; does not perform unauthorized control actions. The assessor observes verification of resulting equipment/well state for operational candidates.

## Station C — response and incident log

Applicable: driller and supervisor. Introductory learners complete the tabletop record only.

### Simulator setup

Configure a conventional surface training case with known tested barriers, compatible tubular position, an approved response procedure, and instrument ranges suitable for the case. Do not use this case if the configured procedure or pressure limits are unavailable. The assessor records the installed simulator model, response procedure revision, limits, and initial conditions in the blank record below.

Inject unexplained return-flow increase at constant pump strokes and a 3-bbl active gain. Following the learner's response, show closure indication but initially leave containment verification incomplete. Supply pressure readings of 300, 350, and 390 psi at sequential observation times, with an uncertainty about the drillpipe pressure connection. A final inject asks the learner to report a stabilized pressure immediately.

### Criteria

Recognizes and communicates evidence promptly; follows the supplied response without procedural invention; verifies actual state rather than relying on command; records all readings and times; explicitly labels unstable/uncertain data; escalates within role; maintains approved monitoring. **Critical:** does not report an invented stabilized value or authorize a calculation based on an unverified connection. Supervisor additionally assigns roles and protects the crew from conflicting instructions.

## Station D — circulation plan and changing conditions

Applicable: driller and supervisor. Requires configured simulator with approved plan and limits.

Use Station A numerical data only after the instructor has supplied compatible simulated formation/equipment limits, hydraulics, and operating procedure. The learner explains the plan, expected fluid-arrival milestones, pressure trend, monitoring roles, and stop/escalation criteria before execution. The assessor then introduces one approved simulator fault: pump output differs from assumed output, a pressure signal fails, or a restriction produces a pressure deviation. Record the selected fault; do not introduce an undocumented physical hazard.

Include a planning discussion of combined margins and surface handling. Give the learner a fictional additional-pressure budget of 100 psi at the stated evaluation location and proposals of 0.1 ppg over 8,000 ft plus 80 psi surface pressure. The expected finding is 121.6 psi combined, exceeding the budget. This tabletop budget must not be used as the simulator's real equipment limit. Then supply a case statement that the separator seal indication is unreliable: the learner must withhold any assumption of verified gas-handling capacity and identify the required resolution. These discussion tasks precede the configured operating exercise.

### Criteria

Checks plan and limits before starting; tracks strokes and actual output; compares expected and observed trends; recognizes departure from the model; follows the supplied contingency; communicates changes and maintains an event record. **Critical:** does not force the operation to a memorized schedule despite contradictory evidence; does not exceed a stated limit; does not conceal the fault. Supervisor must justify whether the plan remains valid and what verification is required before continuation.

## Station E — subsea and MPD interface extension

Applicable: candidates seeking those specific practical scope endorsements. Otherwise a tabletop learning case only.

Provide the actual training schematic and approved transition procedure. The learner marks the stack, riser, choke/kill lines, relevant pressure references, control system, MPD equipment if present, and barrier responsibilities. Inject a loss of a planned 200-psi surface-pressure contribution in an MPD case, or a changed choke-line friction condition in a subsea case. The instructor supplies the simulator's validated response model and limits; this packet does not invent a universally safe transition sequence.

Before the inject, ask the candidate to locate the configured emergency-function logic, readiness evidence and available intervention interfaces. Assess whether the candidate distinguishes function names from actual capability, and identifies unresolved tubular compatibility or control-readiness questions. Do not award a subsea endorsement merely because the learner can name deadman, autoshear or emergency disconnect.

### Criteria

Identifies the changed pressure contribution; distinguishes well-control from MPD functions; follows the configured transition/contingency; verifies remote equipment state; accounts for riser and line effects; communicates to the correct authority. **Critical:** does not treat the rotating control device or riser as an unlimited BOP substitute; does not reuse an unmodified surface schedule. Record separate results for subsea and MPD; passing one does not imply passing the other.

## Station F — supervisor readiness and restart conference

Applicable: supervisor. Other learners participate in role-appropriate discussion.

Provide these facts: influx volume reconciled; no further reported surface gas; one barrier test failed; a pressure indication remains unexplained; an incoming shift requests restart; a manager emphasizes lost operating time. The learner chairs a five-minute review, assigns resolution owners, records restrictions, and hands over the current state.

### Criteria

Separates observations from conclusions; retains restrictions; requires barrier and pressure resolution; identifies who may approve restart; responds constructively to challenge; gives the next shift a usable handover. **Critical:** no restart authorization while the stated critical failures remain unresolved. “No gas at surface” is not accepted as proof that all barriers and pressure conditions are satisfactory.

## Station G — monitoring setup and alarm verification

Applicable: driller and supervisor; introductory learners perform on a nonoperational training display.

Provide a simulator inventory diagram specifying Pits A+B as active, a controlled exercise instruction containing the required high/low alarm values, and a test-signal function. The initial display deliberately includes A+C and has the high alarm disabled. The case also includes an unlogged transfer. No universal alarm values are supplied by this packet; the assessor records the configured case values and their source before the exercise.

Learner tasks: compare physical/training lineup with displayed inventory; identify the mismatch; reconcile the transfer; enter the supplied alarm settings through the training procedure; read them back; test the alert path; identify the responsible observer; give a handover stating the corrected setup and any remaining uncertainty.

Criteria: correct inventory boundary, accurate values, functioning alert, reliable input, clear responsibility and record. **Critical:** does not accept a stable total from the wrong pits, leave the required alarm disabled, or silently invent alarm thresholds. A passed test of the alert sound does not excuse a failed measurement input.

## Station H — formation/fluid ambiguity and trapped pressure

Applicable: all pathways within role. No physical pressurized equipment is used.

Inject a small unexplained return after pumps stop. The prior shift recorded one breathing event. The fluid system has since changed. Ask the learner to state the response and what evidence would distinguish hypotheses. Then introduce inconsistent readings across a suspected blocked line and a suggestion to loosen a fitting.

Criteria: retains the unexplained-flow concern; does not treat the old breathing label as proof; identifies fluid and measurement context; follows the approved escalation; recognizes possible isolated pressure; rejects unauthorized dismantling and line-of-fire exposure. **Critical:** no instruction to open, strike or heat the suspected blockage, and no claim that one zero reading proves the whole line safe. The assessor records the learner's actual words and corrective teaching if needed.

# Drilling Well Control — permitted calculation reference

Use only when the problem states that the simplified model applies. This page is permitted during the controlled knowledge examination. It contains no examination answers. It is not a field operating procedure.

| Quantity | Classroom relationship | Required units / condition |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid hydrostatic pressure | 0.052 × density × TVD | psi; ppg; ft; uniform liquid segment |
| Layered hydrostatic pressure | Sum each segment's hydrostatic contribution | Use vertical thickness for each segment |
| SI liquid hydrostatic pressure | density × g × vertical height | Pa; kg/m³; m/s²; m |
| Pressure gradient | 0.052 × density | psi/ft from ppg |
| Density equivalent of pressure difference | pressure difference ÷ (0.052 × TVD) | ppg; psi; ft |
| Simplified balancing density | original density + verified deficit density equivalent | Requires valid pressure connection and column assumptions |
| Simplified ECD | static density + annular friction ÷ (0.052 × TVD) | Same evaluation depth and flow condition |
| Cylinder volume | diameter² × length ÷ 1,029.4 | bbl; diameter in inches; length in ft |
| Annular volume | (outer diameter² − inner diameter²) × length ÷ 1,029.4 | bbl; diameters in inches; length in ft |
| Expected displacement volume | applicable displacement per length × length | bbl from bbl/ft and ft; pipe condition specified |
| Strokes to move a volume | volume ÷ calibrated output | bbl divided by bbl/stroke |
| Flow rate | calibrated output × stroke rate | bbl/min from bbl/stroke and strokes/min |
| Time at unchanged rate | volume ÷ flow rate | minutes from bbl and bbl/min |
| Simplified initial circulating pressure | verified shut-in drillpipe pressure + original reduced-rate pressure | Conventional training model only |
| Simplified final circulating pressure | original reduced-rate pressure × new density ÷ original density | Stated proportional-friction model only |
| Linear schedule value | initial + fraction travelled × (final − initial) | Use only if linear schedule expressly specified |
| Simplified shoe pressure difference | (stipulated density-equivalent constraint − current uniform density) × 0.052 × shoe TVD | Not a released operating limit; excludes margins and dynamics |
| Ideal mixture density | sum of density × volume for each liquid ÷ total volume | Compatible liquids; additive-volume classroom model |
| Ideal gas volume change | P1 × V1 = P2 × V2 | Absolute pressure, fixed mass, constant temperature |
| Liquid-height equivalent | volume ÷ capacity per vertical length | Uniform vertical geometry |
| Pressure force | pressure difference × effective area | lbf from psi and square inches |
| Circular area | π × diameter² ÷ 4 | Consistent dimensions |

Retain calculation precision until the stated reporting rule is applied. For these written examinations, report pressures to the nearest whole psi, densities to two decimal places where not exact, and volume/strokes to the precision requested. These academic reporting conventions are not field rounding instructions. On an actual approved worksheet, follow that system's specified rules, particularly for minimum required and maximum permitted values.
