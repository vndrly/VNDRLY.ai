# Part I — Routine rigging

## R01 — Know the task and your responsibility

Rigging connects a load to the lifting system so that it can be raised, controlled and landed without losing stability or damaging equipment. A rigger does more than attach a hook. The job includes checking the load information, inspecting equipment, confirming the connection, keeping people clear and stopping when conditions no longer match the plan.

Before work, identify who directs the lift, who operates the lifting machine, who signals and who determines the rigging arrangement. One person can hold several roles only when actually qualified and able to perform them safely. An operator card is not proof of rigging skill. Likewise, completing this course does not make the learner the qualified person for every lift. Under OSHA construction crane rules, qualified riggers are required for specified assembly/disassembly hoisting and when workers perform the listed hooking, guiding or connection activities within the fall zone.

For a Level I assignment, the load's weight, center of gravity and appropriate arrangement are known or provided through established experience and planning. Verify that today's load is the expected one. Stop if equipment is changed, the load contains unknown contents, the planned hitch cannot be installed, or the path creates an unplanned hazard. Escalation is a successful safety decision, not a failed attempt to be helpful.

Worked example: yesterday's dry pump weighed 1,800 pounds; today's similar pump contains liquid and has a new motor. Do not reuse yesterday's rigging decision by appearance. Obtain the current weight and balance information, then have the arrangement confirmed. Common mistake: calling a repeated job routine even after its important conditions change.


## R02 — Read the load and prepare the path

Confirm load identity, reliable weight, dimensions, center of gravity, intended lifting points and loose or moving parts. Inspect the load itself: a rated sling attached to an unverified lifting eye is not a safe system. Shipping brackets, pipe nozzles and handrails are not lifting points unless identified for that purpose. Remove or secure loose items according to the plan. Account for trapped fluids or material, attachments and packaging.

The center of gravity is the point at which the load's weight acts. Place the hook and planned suspension so the load hangs in the intended orientation. When the center of gravity lies away from the apparent middle, equal-length legs may carry unequal forces. A high center of gravity can make a load overturn even if the slings are strong enough. Do not solve an unfamiliar balance problem by trial-and-error hoisting around people.

Prepare the route and landing before lifting. Look for power lines, live equipment, traffic, overhead obstructions, occupied areas, excavations and trip hazards. Establish swing/fall exclusion controls with the lift team. Set stable dunnage so the load will not roll, tip or crush slings at landing. Keep an escape route; do not position yourself between the suspended load and a fixed object.

Worked decision: a crate's label states a gross shipping weight, but one side is broken and the supplied lifting points are inaccessible. Knowing the weight does not solve the connection or containment problem. Hold the lift and have an appropriate method established. Common mistake: treating the load as a single solid object when its contents can shift.


## R03 — Understand ratings, angles and the weakest component

Working load limit or rated capacity is the permitted load for stated conditions. Minimum breaking strength is not the usable working limit. Design factors are already part of the rated equipment's design; they are not extra capacity available to a user. Read the identification and manufacturer instructions for the actual sling, hitch, angle, temperature and attachments.

Different configurations change the allowable load. Vertical, basket and choker values are not interchangeable. A basket's favorable rating depends on the stated leg angle, balanced support and other conditions; do not automatically double a vertical number. Choking bends and loads the sling differently, so use the applicable choker rating and angle restrictions. A four-leg bridle does not prove equal quarter shares.

Low sling angles increase leg tension. For a symmetric two-leg instructional arrangement, tension per leg equals total weight divided by twice the sine of the leg angle above horizontal. For 4,000 pounds, each leg carries 2,000 pounds at 90 degrees, about 2,309 at 60 degrees,2,828 at 45 degrees and 4,000 at 30 degrees. These are ideal static values, not permission to design an unfamiliar lift. Use the approved arrangement and manufacturer's rated chart. Clarify whether an angle is measured from horizontal or vertical; confusing the convention can reverse the conclusion.

The system is limited by its weakest applicable component and configuration. A5,000-pound sling does not make a 3,000-pound connector adequate for 4,000 pounds. Include actual angle, load sharing and attachment effects rather than comparing every label to total load blindly. Stop for missing/illegible identification; guessed capacity based on color, diameter or resemblance is not a substitute.

Application: a plan specifies two rated legs at 60 degrees but limited headroom makes them 30 degrees. The force per leg rises substantially. Do not proceed with the old arrangement or shorten a sling by a knot; have the plan revised. Common mistake: believing a flatter bridle reduces force because the hook is lower.


## R04 — Inspect and control defective equipment

Inspect the sling and every fastening/attachment before use each shift/day as required and again when conditions warrant. The employer's designated competent person and periodic-inspection arrangements remain necessary. Identify the item and its rating, then inspect the full accessible length and all connection points in good light. Look at both sides of webbing, chain links, rope contact areas, sleeves, eyes and hardware—not only the part nearest the tag.

Remove defective equipment from service and isolate it so another worker cannot unknowingly use it. Tag the item with identity, defect, date and reporter; follow the approved repair/disposition process. Do not repair a cut sling with tape, weld a chain link in the field, straighten a hook or replace a shackle pin with a convenient bolt. A proof test is not something a Level I rigger improvises to decide whether damaged equipment is usable.

Wire-rope slings need checks for broken wires, kinks, crushing, birdcaging, corrosion, damaged fittings and heat effects. Chain needs checks for cracks, stretch, bent/twisted links, wear and damaged attachments. Synthetic slings need checks for cuts, punctures, burns, melted areas, broken stitching and distorted hardware. Missing identification is itself a reason not to use the item. Apply the correct material/manufacturer removal criteria rather than a universal percentage to every product.

Periodic inspections do not replace today's check. Alloy-chain sling periodic inspection intervals must reflect service and must not exceed twelve months under the cited OSHA provisions. Keep the required record of the most recent inspection month. Severe use may require much more frequent inspection. A recent certificate cannot excuse a fresh defect.

Worked example: a sling was inspected last week but now has a cut at the bearing point. Isolate it immediately and replace it with an identified suitable item. Record the condition and inspect the load edge/protection so the replacement is not damaged the same way. Common mistake: replacing equipment without correcting the cause of damage.


## R05 — Select and handle sling materials

Alloy chain tolerates some environments that damage synthetics, but it still has material, temperature and configuration limits. Use rated lifting chain, not general-purpose chain of unknown grade. Shortening must use an approved device and method; do not tie, twist or bolt links together. A link should articulate as designed without being bent over an unsuitable sharp corner.

Wire-rope slings combine strength with flexibility, but bending over a small diameter reduces usable capacity and can damage the rope. Follow the manufacturer's bearing-diameter requirements. Do not knot wire rope. Under the construction rule, sling/bridle eyes are not formed with wire-rope clips or knots. A clip diagram for another permitted connection does not authorize making a sling eye. Protect hands from broken wires, but gloves do not make handling a moving or tensioned rope safe.

Synthetic web and roundslings are light and protect some finished surfaces, but edges, heat and chemicals can cut or degrade them. Use purpose-suitable edge protection that stays positioned and handles the actual contact, not an unverified rag. Keep the sling flat and unbunched in the fitting. A roundsling's cover protects internal load-bearing fibers; damage or uncertain core condition requires the manufacturer's rejection procedure. Color alone never establishes capacity across manufacturers.

Chemical compatibility matters. The construction web-sling rule prohibits nylon in specified acid/phenolic exposure and polyester/polypropylene in specified caustic exposure; manufacturer restrictions may be tighter. Polyester/nylon web slings are not used above 180°F under that rule, while polypropylene has a 200°F ceiling. These figures do not rate every synthetic product or permit use when heat damage is visible. Identify the actual material and conditions before selection.

Fiber-rope and metal-mesh slings also require identified ratings and material-specific inspection. Do not substitute an ordinary tag line for a lifting sling. Keep equipment clean and stored off damaging surfaces, away from incompatible chemicals, heat and sunlight exposure as directed. Application: an unknown cleaning chemical has soaked a synthetic sling. Isolate it and obtain the manufacturer's disposition; drying it does not prove restored strength.


## R06 — Install vertical, basket and choker hitches

A vertical hitch connects one end of the sling to the lifting device and the other to an approved load point. Ensure both ends seat correctly and the load cannot rotate or tip unexpectedly. A single vertical connection does not stabilize every shape. Follow the established arrangement and use required control lines without putting people beneath the load.

A basket supports the load in the sling body with both ends connected above. Place it so the load is contained and cannot slide out. Verify the actual leg angle, bend radius, balanced bearing and equipment rating. A basket around a smooth pipe may allow rolling or slipping unless the approved arrangement controls it. Two baskets are not automatically equal-sharing supports; the center of gravity and location matter.

A choker passes around the load and through its own approved eye or fitting. Position the choke so it closes as intended and does not bear on an unsuitable splice, fitting or damaged section. Use the actual choker capacity and permitted choke-angle conditions. A choker does not inherently contain loose pieces or prevent a long load from tipping. Do not hammer a choke tight or jerk it into position.

Before tension, remove twists, seat eyes fully, position edge protection and keep hands away from where slack will close. Ask for slow slack take-up, then stop and inspect the arrangement from a safe position. If a sling walks, pinches or pulls out of its intended position, land/relax safely and correct it. Never reach into a tightening hitch.

Worked comparison: an identified instructional sling is rated 4,000 vertical,3,200 choker and 8,000 basket only with vertical legs under the stated conditions. A3,500-pound choker lift fails despite passing the vertical rating. A7,000-pound basket case is not approved until its angles, balance and contact conditions match the stated basket rating. Common mistake: using the largest number on the tag regardless of the hitch installed.


## R07 — Connect hooks, shackles and lifting points correctly

Identify each hardware component, its manufacturer, rating and intended direction of loading. Inspect for cracks, deformation, wear, damaged threads and missing retainers. Confirm compatibility between sling eyes, hooks, pins and lifting points. A connection can have sufficient nominal strength but still pinch a sling, load a hook tip or bend a lifting eye in an unauthorized direction.

Seat a hook in its intended bowl/load-bearing area. Do not load the latch, tip or side unless the specific device is designed and rated for that condition. A latch helps retain the connection; it does not carry the load. Check the actual latch and any required retention. Eyebolts and swivel hoist rings have different installation and angular-loading rules. Use the correct thread, engagement, seating, torque and alignment from the actual manufacturer; do not force an eyebolt to point in a desired direction by backing it out.

For shackles, use the correct original-compatible pin and body. Engage screw pins fully according to the manufacturer's method; bolt-type assemblies require their specified nut and retention. Do not substitute bolts or mix unidentified parts. Center the intended loading and follow the actual product's side-loading restrictions. Round-pin, screw-pin and bolt-type shackles do not share every application rule. Multiple sling legs require a suitable collector arrangement, not arbitrary crowding on the pin.

A moving sling can rotate a screw pin if installed in a way that bears and moves against it. Select and orient the connection under the approved instructions so it cannot loosen. For long-duration, vibration or special use, follow the manufacturer's specific retention/type requirements. Do not invent a universal side-load reduction factor or assume that adding wire makes every arrangement acceptable.

Application: a synthetic eye is wider than the shackle's usable bearing surface and folds sharply. Stop and select a compatible connector; forcing it through can reduce capacity and damage fibers. Common mistake: selecting hardware only by its stamped tonnage while ignoring fit and load direction.


## R08 — Control the lift, communication and personal position

Brief the operator and signal person before lifting. Establish who gives normal motion signals, how communication transfers and how to stop. A rigger may signal only within the required qualification/authorization; this program's basic signal practice does not automatically satisfy a separate signal-person qualification. Anyone may give a stop warning and the operator must obey it.

Take up slack slowly while riggers move to safe positions. Verify seating and balance at the lowest practical trial clearance specified by the plan. If the load tilts, hardware shifts or a sling becomes unevenly loaded, stop and safely land it for correction. Do not stabilize it by pushing from a trapped position or placing hands under it. Maintain the exclusion zone throughout movement.

Use a tag line when the plan requires control of rotation. Keep it free of loops around people, sharp contacts and snag points. Do not wrap it around your hand, arm or body. Stand outside the load's fall/crush path, with a clear escape route. Avoid electrical exposure; a line is not safe to touch merely because it appears nonmetallic. If maintaining control requires entering a dangerous position, stop and change the plan.

Use clear signals: stop is a horizontally extended palm-down arm moving side to side; emergency stop uses both arms. Hoist uses an upward-pointing index finger/forearm making small circles; lower uses a downward-pointing hand/index finger circling. Swing indicates direction with an extended arm/index finger. Post the official chart and practice all signals needed by the task. Ambiguous or lost required signals mean stop, not guess.

Worked decision: the load is moving toward a column and the signal person is distracted. Give stop immediately and clear the pinch area. Do not wait because you were not the designated normal signaler. Common mistake: treating a tag line as permission to stand between the load and its destination.


## R09 — Knots for nonlifting control lines

Knot practice develops recognition and handling for approved nonlifting control-line tasks. It never authorizes knotted wire-rope/web/fiber slings, personnel support or improvised lifting eyes. Knots reduce rope strength and can slip, jam or capsize depending on rope, loading and construction. The rope's working end is the free end being manipulated; the standing part is the longer loaded or fixed portion. Dress a knot by arranging its strands neatly, then set it by controlled tension. Leave the manufacturer/task-required tails and use any specified backup. If the method is uncertain, do not put it into service.

**Square/reef knot, bench recognition:** cross the right working end over the left and tuck under, then cross the end now on the left over the other and tuck under. Dress the two interlocking bights flat; each tail lies alongside its own standing part. Repeating the same handed crossing produces a different, unreliable granny configuration. Use the square knot only for appropriate light binding, not joining loaded lifting lines or life safety.

**Sheet bend:** form a bight in one line. Pass the other working end through the bight, take it around both legs of the bight and tuck it beneath its own standing part. Dress it with the ends on the same side; use the thicker/stiffer line as the bight when the approved application permits unlike lines. Check the configuration rather than trusting resemblance. It is not a substitute for a rated sling splice.

**Clove hitch around a post:** take one turn around the post, cross the working end over the standing part, take a second turn and tuck the working end beneath that second turn. The finished hitch has two turns with a crossing between them. It can slip or roll under changing pull; use only in an approved control-line application with the required backup. Do not use it as an unqualified lifting or rescue connection.

**Two half hitches:** pass the line around the support, then carry the working end around the standing part and through the resulting loop to make a half hitch. Repeat in the same direction around the standing part. Dress the hitches together and set them. Both hitches encircle the standing part, not two unrelated points. Confirm the support and anticipated pull are suitable for the approved nonlifting task.

**Inside bowline:** make a small loop in the standing part, pass the working end up through it, around the standing part and back down through the small loop. Dress the fixed eye and collar so the tail finishes inside the eye. The trainer verifies the correct structure rather than only the familiar mnemonic. A bowline can loosen under cyclic/slack loading; use task-required backup and inspection. This bench exercise never grants fall-protection or hoisting approval.

Application: a worker proposes tying a bowline in an untagged rope to replace a missing lifting sling. Refuse. Correct knot shape does not establish a rated lifting assembly. Common mistake: assuming proficiency at a knot makes every use of that knot safe.


## R10 — Land, release, store and report

Check the landing surface and dunnage before the load arrives. Confirm capacity, spacing, stability and access for rigging removal. Keep people out of pinch points and use the planned tools/positions. Only necessary workers enter permitted receiving positions; nobody stands directly beneath an exposed suspended load. A load is not safely landed merely because one corner touches the ground.

Lower in a controlled way until the load is fully supported and stable. Obtain the agreed confirmation before slackening or disconnecting. Watch for rolling cylinders, spring-loaded components, high centers of gravity and stacked materials. Do not release rigging that is still carrying load. If a sling is trapped, do not jerk it out with the crane; re-support/reposition under an approved safe plan.

Remove hooks and hardware without pulling them across people or damaging the sling. Secure loose parts and store rigging on suitable racks or containers, protected from vehicles, weather and incompatible chemicals as required. Keep quarantined equipment separate. Update the equipment/defect record and tell the next user about unresolved restrictions.

Worked example: a pipe bundle lands on uneven blocks and starts to roll as tension is released. Stop the release, clear people and safely reestablish support according to the plan; do not try to wedge a foot beneath it. Common mistake: considering rigging finished at the instant the crane stops moving rather than after stable release and safe removal.


## R11 — Recognize changing conditions and special devices

Wind, ice, heat, chemicals, sharp edges and poor visibility can invalidate an otherwise familiar arrangement. Shock loading occurs when slack is suddenly taken up or a moving load stops abruptly; it can exceed static calculated forces. Do not use a jerk to free a snagged load or pull rigging from beneath weight. Stop if the load hangs on an obstruction or its apparent weight changes unexpectedly.

Below-the-hook devices such as lifting beams, spreaders, plate clamps, magnets and vacuum lifters require their own identification, inspection and instructions. A lifting beam resists bending while a spreader typically carries compression through its geometry; neither can be replaced with an unverified piece of steel. Plate clamps have material/thickness, orientation and engagement limits. Magnets and vacuum lifters depend on surface and operating conditions; visible contact alone does not prove secure holding.

Custom accessories require the applicable design, marking and test process; this course does not authorize shop-built substitutes. A manufacturer-specified load limit must be respected even if a device looks strong. Turning a load, working with an unknown center of gravity, lifting with multiple cranes or combining several hoists adds load-transfer questions that go beyond this Level I routine-task scope. Get appropriately qualified planning and personnel.

Application: a clamp selected for a flat plate is proposed for a curved, painted piece outside its listed conditions. Refuse the substitution and obtain suitable equipment and a revised method. Common mistake: treating every accessory with a hook as generic rigging.


## R12 — Integrated routine-rigging laboratory

The following data are fictional training data, never ratings for real equipment. A known uniform 4,000-pound practice load has approved symmetric lifting points. The established plan uses two equal legs at 60 degrees above horizontal. Each instructional leg is rated 3,000 pounds in this exact arrangement; each connector is rated 3,500 pounds in the specified in-line condition. Ideal calculated leg tension is 4,000/(2×0.866)=approximately 2,310 pounds, below both limits. This numerical comparison is one step: inspection, fit, actual center of gravity, support, machine rating, path and protective measures must also pass.

Change 1: actual angle becomes 30 degrees. Each leg would carry 4,000 pounds, exceeding both listed limits. Stop and get the arrangement revised. Change 2: the sling tag is unreadable but it looks identical to the other leg. Do not use it. Change 3: one lifting eye is visibly cracked. The entire lift is held even though the rigging is adequate. Change 4: a sharp edge contacts the sling during travel. Stop and provide appropriate protection or another approved arrangement before proceeding.

Second exercise: an instructional sling label states 3,000 vertical,2,400 choker,6,000 basket with vertical legs under the listed conditions. A2,600-pound load in a choker fails by 200 pounds. A2,200-pound load passes that numerical hitch limit, but not if the load can slip out, the sling is damaged or the connection is unsuitable. Explain the difference between a rating comparison and a complete safe-lift decision.

Third exercise: a supplied plan calls for a known 2,000-pound component, rated vertical sling and approved lifting point. Before take-up, the learner checks item identification, hardware seating/retention, exclusion controls, signal agreement and stable landing support. After a low controlled initial lift, the load hangs as planned. The learner guides only from the safe designated position, signals stop for an injected communication-loss card, then resumes only after clarification and lands/releases safely. The instructor evaluates actual handling with a suitable low training load, not just a spoken checklist.

Independent questions: a 4,800-pound symmetric load at 30 degrees imposes what ideal force per leg? Answer 4,800 pounds. A2,500-pound choker job with 2,400 capacity? Hold,100 pounds over. A rated shackle with an unmatched bolt? Reject the connection. A basket arrangement whose load can slide? Hold and obtain a stable approved method. In each answer, identify the evidence required to resolve the hold rather than merely saying 'be careful.'


## R13 — Hoists, jacks and controlled horizontal movement

Identify the complete support system for a chain hoist, lever hoist or winch: equipment rating, anchor/support capacity, hook connections, load path and actual line direction. A hoist's rating does not establish the capacity of the beam or anchor supporting it. Inspect chains/ropes, hooks, brakes, controls, casing, attachment and identification. Follow the actual instructions for setup and use; do not wrap the hoist's load chain around the load as a sling unless the device is expressly designed for that arrangement. Do not extend a lever or exceed permitted hand effort to force motion. Unexpected resistance means stop and investigate the load, alignment and restraint.

A trolley must fit its beam flange/profile and be adjusted as specified, with required end stops and retention. A beam clamp must be rated for its actual application and direction, not merely fit around steel. Do not treat an A-frame or portable gantry as stable at every height/span or permit side pull without its approved configuration. Check the floor/support, brakes and travel restrictions. Movement of a loaded frame or trolley follows its specific approved procedure; no universal permission comes from wheels being present.

Jacks need an identified capacity, firm support, suitable contact and protection against overtravel. Plan the lifting sequence and stable cribbing before raising anything. Once raised, secure the load promptly with suitable blocking/cribbing or another approved support; never rely on hydraulic pressure alone to protect someone beneath or between equipment. Keep hands out of the closing gap and do not crawl under to place a block. Follow the approved low-increment method and use tools/positions that keep people clear. Remove/tag defective jacks.

Dollies, skates and rollers change a load from suspended to rolling, but do not eliminate overturning or crush risks. Confirm floor capacity/condition, route slope, thresholds, turning geometry, load distribution and means of controlled propulsion/restraint. Keep the load secured to its intended supports and keep people out of the downhill or trapped side. Do not use loose pipes or improvised rollers as substitutes for a planned rated arrangement. Complex multi-jack or load-transfer work requires additional planning and competence beyond this routine-task course.

Application: a 4,000-pound hoist is hung from an unverified pipe rack for a 2,000-pound lift. Hold: the hoist label does not rate the supporting structure. Common mistake: checking the visible lifting device while ignoring where its reaction goes.


## R14 — Adjustable hardware, material-specific inspection and connections

Turnbuckles and other adjustable connectors must be intended and rated for the actual lifting/tension application. Verify thread condition, permitted engagement, straight load path and required locking/retention. Do not use a hardware-store tensioner for lifting or bend a turnbuckle to align it. A swivel that positions a load is not automatically rated to rotate continuously under load; verify the exact device's instructions. Master links and collector rings need adequate bearing space and compatible orientation so connected legs do not crowd, pry or cross-load them.

Rigging blocks redirect a line and transfer force to their anchorage. The support reaction can exceed the line tension: an ideal 180-degree return can impose approximately twice the line tension on the block support. This is a hazard-recognition example, not authority for a Level I worker to design a winch system. Confirm the specified block/sheave/rope compatibility, side-plate closure, pin retention and rated attachment before use. Do not let a rope run over an unsuitable sheave or place hands near the nip point.

Compression/swaged fittings and wire-rope terminations are manufactured/installed to specified procedures. A rigger inspects for cracks, distortion, slippage, corrosion and improper assembly, and rejects doubtful items; the rigger does not field-swage or repair them without the separate competence/equipment/procedure. Never copy a clip connection onto a construction sling eye where that method is prohibited.

For wire-rope slings in general industry, OSHA1910.184 identifies removal criteria including ten randomly distributed broken wires in one rope lay or five in one strand in a lay, specified outside-wire wear, structural distortion, heat damage and defective end attachments. Do not confuse these sling criteria with a crane running-rope rule. Construction and manufacturer requirements also apply according to the task. For hooks, use the actual product's criteria and applicable regulation, not an arbitrary universal wear allowance. Visible cracks or deformation demand isolation and assessment.

Metal-mesh slings need inspection of wire fabric and handles for broken welds/brazed joints, wire wear/corrosion, distortion and loss of flexibility. Coatings can hide damage; follow the manufacturer's inspection method. Fiber-rope slings need checks for cut/broken fibers, powdered fibers, abnormal wear, changed strand shape, discoloration/rot and distorted fittings. These products are not accepted merely because they lack a visible tear like a web sling. Use the correct identification, hitch rating, temperature and chemical limits for each material.

Application: a worker applies the crane-rope broken-wire threshold to a wire-rope sling and signs it off without checking the sling rule or manufacturer. Stop that acceptance and use the applicable criteria. Common mistake: memorizing one inspection number and applying it to every rope product.


# Part II — Advanced rigging

## A01 — Establish the task and decision authority

A routine known-load arrangement can become an advanced task when its center of gravity is unknown, the load must rotate, different lifting devices share the load, or the attachment geometry changes during movement. Advanced rigging starts by identifying those changes, not by choosing larger slings. The rigger must establish weight, dimensions, lifting points, structural integrity, travel path, landing arrangement and each stage of support. A machine can have enough rated capacity while its load is inadequately supported or its rigging is overloaded.

Before work, obtain the current load drawing, equipment data and plan. Compare the drawing with the object: attached machinery, residual liquid, insulation and temporary steel can invalidate the stated weight and center of gravity. Identify the lift director, operator, signal person, riggers and person authorized to approve changes. Explain the hold points and the stop signal to everybody. If a required fact is absent, record the uncertainty and obtain a qualified resolution. A guess written on a form remains a guess.

The employer's determination of a qualified rigger concerns the particular work. A certificate alone does not demonstrate capability for every complex rigging operation. Similarly, this course does not qualify a person to design lifting lugs, weld repairs, conduct personnel lifting or direct a multiple-crane lift. Those tasks require their own applicable expertise and controls. In construction, a multiple-crane lift requires a qualified plan, appropriate lift direction and a meeting with the involved workers; engineering expertise must be supplied when the qualified planner determines it is needed.

Worked decision: an approved plan covers lifting an empty pump skid level. The crew now wants to stand the skid on end with the pump still attached. Stop the change. The rotation alters force directions, load shares, stability, lifting-point loading and possible leakage. The original plan is evidence for neither the new geometry nor the pump's retention. Obtain an approved staged plan and verify the actual object before proceeding.

Application: mark every assumption on a sample work order as verified, unresolved or outside your authority. Name the person who can resolve each unresolved item. Do not begin tensioning merely because the crane is waiting.


## A02 — Determine weight with defensible inputs

Prefer traceable manufacturer or engineering information for the actual configuration, or a suitable verified measurement. Calculation from dimensions and density is useful only when the material, shape and contents are known. Break the object into simple solids, calculate their volumes, subtract verified voids and add attachments. Record dimensions, units, density source and uncertainty. Use external dimensions for a solid only if it really is solid; use wall dimensions for a hollow section. Never assume a vessel is drained because its valve is open.

For a rectangular solid, volume is length times width times thickness. Convert all dimensions before multiplying. For a cylindrical solid, volume is pi times radius squared times length. For a hollow cylinder, subtract the inner cylinder volume from the outer. Weight equals volume times weight density. These formulas do not establish lifting-point strength or the object's ability to remain intact when supported at proposed points.

Worked case: an exercise supplies a solid plate 36 by 24 by 1 inch and a hypothetical weight density of 490 pounds per cubic foot. Volume is 864 cubic inches divided by 1,728, or 0.5 cubic foot. Plate weight is 245 pounds. A documented 80-pound attachment produces a 325-pound assembly before rigging. The supplied density is a problem input, not a substitute for identifying a real material. If an 80-pound attachment is merely estimated, the total is not verified.

Include the weight carried by every part of the system. The crane's load accounting may include hook block and below-hook devices; the sling below a lifting beam does not carry the same components as the suspension above it. Draw boundaries and list the weight acting below each boundary. Do not deduct rigging twice or omit a spreader because it is reusable equipment.

Common mistakes are mixing inches with feet, confusing pounds with kilograms, forgetting trapped contents, and applying one density to a composite object. A successful trial lift does not validate an underestimated weight: the system may already be overloaded. Resolve weight before the trial.

Application: calculate a known component, then list the evidence needed for every addition and subtraction. If the object does not match the documented configuration, hold the lift rather than rounding the uncertainty away.


## A03 — Locate center of gravity in more than one direction

Center of gravity is the point through which the assembly's resultant weight acts in a uniform gravity field. For components with known weights and locations, choose a datum and calculate the weighted average: xCG equals the sum of each weight times its x coordinate divided by total weight. Repeat independently for y and z. State the coordinate system on the drawing. A component at a negative coordinate contributes a negative moment; changing the datum consistently changes coordinates, not the physical result.

Worked case: a 2,000-pound component acts at x=1 foot and a 1,000-pound component at x=7 feet. Total weight is 3,000 pounds. The moment sum is 9,000 pound-feet; xCG is 3 feet. Halfway between the outer ends would give the wrong answer. If those components have y coordinates 0 and 3 feet, yCG is 1 foot. The hook and connection geometry must account for the lateral offset as well as the longitudinal one.

For a freely suspended stable load, the combined center of gravity settles below its suspension. If the hook is horizontally displaced from the resultant center of gravity, the load may move or tilt as support is removed. Do not use people or a tag line to force an unstable load to appear level. A high center of gravity can create tipping hazards during transfer to skates or a landing cradle even if the suspended phase is stable.

Use documented component centers, not their outer edges, in the moment calculation. Account for movable contents and attachments. If liquid can shift, a single static center may not describe the full operation; a qualified plan must address that movement. A trial at minimum planned clearance can confirm expected balance under a verified plan, but unexpected tilt means lower safely and investigate. Never reach beneath the load to adjust a sling.

Application: draw the calculated center on two views of an assembly. Explain how removing a component changes both weight and center. Check the answer lies within the range of positive component coordinates; an impossible result often exposes a unit or sign error.


## A04 — Calculate support reactions and check the model

For a rigid body with two vertical supports, vertical forces must sum to the weight and moments must balance. If supports are at x=0 and x=L and weight W acts at x=a, the right reaction is W times a divided by L. The left reaction is W minus the right. These are vertical reactions, not automatically inclined-sling tensions. The model assumes the specified supports, a rigid body and no other applied forces or accelerations.

Worked case: a 12,000-pound load is supported at 0 and 8 feet, with its center at 2 feet. Right reaction is 12,000 times 2 divided by 8 =3,000 pounds; left reaction is 9,000 pounds. Selecting two 6,000-pound components because their sum is 12,000 would overload the left connection. Add the appropriate hardware and local geometry effects for each load path.

Verify the arithmetic two ways: reactions sum to 12,000, and right reaction times 8 feet equals weight times 2 feet. If the center lies outside the supports, one computed reaction becomes negative. A sling cannot push downward to supply a negative tensile reaction. That result identifies an invalid or unstable proposed support arrangement, not a permissible negative load.

Three or four legs do not necessarily share equally. Length differences, load stiffness, lifting-point elevations and elastic stretch affect sharing. Equilibrium alone may not determine every reaction. Use the applicable rated assembly data and a qualified load-sharing analysis rather than dividing by the number of legs. A slack fourth leg does not contribute simply because it is connected.

Application: draw separate free-body diagrams for the load, lifting beam and top connection. Label every force and verify that the assumed force directions match the actual geometry. Stop if the model omits a support, friction restraint or changing contact point that materially affects the answer.


## A05 — Resolve inclined sling forces

For a symmetric two-leg bridle supporting a centered load, each leg tension is W divided by twice the sine of the leg angle measured from horizontal. State the angle reference: a 30-degree angle from horizontal is not 30 degrees from vertical. As the legs become flatter, tension increases. The horizontal component also loads the object or lifting device and may require structural verification.

Worked case: W=4,000 pounds and each leg is 30 degrees from horizontal. Sine 30 degrees is 0.5, so each leg carries 4,000 pounds. The vertical component per leg is 2,000 pounds; its horizontal component is approximately 3,464 pounds. A 4,000-pound vertical sling rating alone does not prove the installed hitch, connector, bend and lifting point are acceptable.

For a coplanar two-leg bridle with unequal horizontal angles theta1 and theta2 and no other horizontal forces, equilibrium gives T1=W cos(theta2)/sin(theta1+theta2), and T2=W cos(theta1)/sin(theta1+theta2). These formulas apply only to the stated force model; the center of gravity and attachment positions must agree with it. Do not choose arbitrary angles independently of the actual load geometry.

Worked case: W=6,000 pounds, theta1=30 degrees, theta2=60 degrees. T1=3,000 pounds and T2 is approximately 5,196 pounds. Vertical components are 1,500 and 4,500 pounds. Horizontal components are equal and opposite, approximately 2,598 pounds. The steeper leg carries more in this particular unequal geometry because of the required equilibrium, illustrating why a memorized 'flatter always carries more' comparison cannot replace analysis of the whole system.

For wire-rope sling configurations, use the applicable manufacturer's rated data. OSHA guidance addresses using the next lower listed angle or qualified calculation for unlisted angles and special justification for very low horizontal angles. Calculation of tension does not create a rating for unidentified gear. Never use the design factor as extra usable capacity.

Application: calculate tensions, then independently resolve them into horizontal and vertical components. If they do not balance, check angle reference, calculator degree mode and geometry before selecting equipment.


## A06 — Select the whole rigging assembly

Selection is a comparison between calculated demand and the applicable working limit for every component in its actual configuration. Record sling material, length, hitch, angle, bend radius, edge contact, environmental exposure and compatible connectors. A sling tag may give different vertical, basket and choker ratings. A basket does not guarantee twice the vertical capacity under every angle or contact condition. A choker adds local bending and choke-angle effects; use its applicable data.

Alloy chain tolerates some conditions that damage synthetics, but damaged links, unsuitable heat exposure or incompatible components invalidate serviceability. Wire rope needs appropriate bending support and protection from kinks, crushing and sharp contact. Synthetic web and roundslings can be light and flexible but are vulnerable to cutting, heat and chemical attack; their protection must suit the edge, load movement and sling type. Metal mesh needs sound handles and flexible undamaged mesh. Natural or synthetic fiber rope requires identification, condition and application-specific ratings; an ordinary rope is not automatically a lifting sling.

Inspect identification and the full accessible sling before use, including terminations and contact areas. Isolate damaged or unidentified equipment and obtain the proper inspection decision; do not improvise a repair. Distinguish the material's removal criteria and scheduled inspection from a quick visual glance. Periodic inspection records do not replace the pre-use check. Running crane rope criteria are not interchangeable with wire-rope sling criteria.

Worked selection: a calculated leg demand is 4,500 pounds. A tag lists 5,000 vertical and 4,000 choker. The planned choker arrangement fails even though the vertical rating exceeds demand. Choosing a larger sling requires repeating fit, bend, connection and lifting-point checks, not merely changing the number on the form.

Application: prepare a component schedule with demand, actual configuration rating, evidence/manual and inspection identity. Mark any unknown value as a hold. Do not multiply nominal component ratings together; the load path is limited by its least adequate element and by structural/stability constraints.


## A07 — Connect hardware without creating hidden loads

A connection must fit, articulate and retain the load as intended. Inspect shackles, hooks, links, rings, swivels, chain components, turnbuckles and lifting points against their own identification and instructions. Verify pin/body compatibility, thread engagement, retainers, allowable loading direction and bearing contact. A physically fitting pin is not proof of an approved replacement. A hook's latch retains a connection; it is not a load-bearing support for tip loading.

Arrange a shackle so the applied forces act in its permitted direction and the sling eyes are not crowded or pinched. Multiple legs may require a suitable bow arrangement or other connector, but the manufacturer determines the limits and any side-loading reduction. Screw-pin rotation from a moving connection can loosen a pin; select the approved retained arrangement for the movement. Do not apply one manufacturer's angle table universally to every shackle.

Eyebolts and swivel hoist rings are different products. Verify thread size, engagement, seating surface, torque, permitted force direction and lifting-point structure. An eyebolt used at an angle may have a much lower limit or be unsuitable. A swivel ring must actually move freely into its intended loaded alignment and be installed to its instructions. Never substitute hand tightening where a torque requirement applies. A certification exercise's fixture instructions are not field installation instructions.

Turnbuckles and adjustable chain arrangements must have adequate engagement and approved locking/retention; the end fittings and side-load limits matter. Do not adjust a loaded assembly unless the actual device and approved procedure permit it. Compression terminations, wire-rope clips and wedge sockets require their specific rope, assembly, tail, orientation and inspection instructions. Do not invent clip spacing or torque. Construction wire-rope sling eyes must not be fabricated with clips or knots under the applicable sling rule; instructions for another rope termination do not waive that restriction.

Worked fault: a lifting ring is rated above the calculated force, but its shoulder cannot seat because a weld bead interferes. Stop. Torque alone cannot fix the missing support condition, and grinding the load or connector is not an authorized field solution. Obtain an approved lifting-point correction or a different approved connection.

Application: assemble a bench connection from its actual instructions and record torque/retention evidence. Have a second person identify the force direction and inspect the completed connection before using it in supervised lifting.


## A08 — Understand blocks, reeving and hoists

A pulley changes force direction and can provide mechanical advantage, but it also loads its support. For an ideal single fixed sheave with two parallel rope segments returning in the same direction, the support reaction is twice the rope tension. With 2,000 pounds in each segment, the ideal reaction is 4,000 pounds, not 2,000. For other angles, add force vectors. Include block weight and actual friction/dynamic effects as required by the equipment data and plan.

In an ideal multi-part system, the supporting rope parts share the load, but real reeving includes friction and unequal tensions. Use the manufacturer's permitted reeving, line pull, sheave and support data. Count only parts actually supporting the moving load. Trace the rope from its dead end through every sheave to the hauling end. Verify rope size, groove condition, fleet angle, guards and end termination. A theoretical mechanical advantage is not permission to exceed a winch or block rating.

Manual chain, lever, electric and air hoists have different controls and operating limitations. Verify the support, hooks, chain/rope condition, brake, controls, rated capacity and travel limits for the actual model. The hoist rating does not certify the beam or trolley. Keep the load aligned as permitted; side pulling, wrapping load chain around the object and extending a lever with a pipe are not acceptable shortcuts. A limit device is not a routine stopping target unless the manufacturer expressly designs it for that use.

For powered hoists, identify the disconnect or air isolation and what loss of power means for load holding and recovery. Never assume an emergency stop removes stored energy or lowers the load. Plan a safe suspended-load response without people entering beneath it. Test controls and brake response at the prescribed safe stage before the full movement; abnormal creep or noise requires stopping and isolation.

Worked case: a hand hoist is attached to a trolley whose beam support has no documented capacity. The load is below the hoist's rating. The operation is still on hold: the upstream support is unverified. No trial load is an acceptable way to discover the beam's capacity.

Application: trace the load path and identify the load on each block and support. Demonstrate the model's normal stop and emergency isolation on unloaded equipment before supervised operation.


## A09 — Choose below-hook devices for the actual load

Lifting beams and spreader arrangements affect forces differently. A lifting beam commonly resists bending; a spreader arrangement commonly carries compression with its top rigging. Neither may be substituted merely because it has the same marked capacity. Use the exact permitted span, attachment positions, top rigging and load distribution. Changing a pick point can change bending or compression demand. Account for device weight above and below each relevant connection.

Mechanical clamps, plate clamps, beam clamps and grapples depend on compatible material, thickness, orientation, surface condition and engagement. A clamp for one plate orientation may not permit turning or lifting stacked plates. Verify locking and the required minimum/maximum load conditions where specified. Do not assume teeth grip through paint, grease or a protective sheet. Keep hands clear of jaws and never suspend a person to test the grip.

Vacuum lifters require the specified surface, seal, load geometry and backup/monitoring provisions. Porous or dirty surfaces, curvature, leakage and power loss can alter holding ability. Magnetic lifters depend on material properties, thickness, contact, air gaps and the manufacturer's working conditions. A magnet can attract a piece without having sufficient rated holding capacity for it. Distinguish close-proximity and remote operation and maintain the planned exclusion area. Indicators are part of the system, not substitutes for suitability checks.

Worked decision: a vacuum device approved for smooth flat sheet is proposed for corrugated material of the same weight. Reject the substitution until the manufacturer/qualified planner approves the actual contact and holding conditions. Weight is only one selection variable. A second lifting device does not automatically cure the incompatibility because its load sharing and failure behavior also require planning.

Application: compare three device data sheets and select the one that expressly covers a supplied load. Identify what must be inspected, what loss-of-holding warning means and where people may stand. If no device fits, document the hold rather than selecting the closest-looking option.


## A10 — Plan and perform a controlled level lift

A level lift requires verified balance and connections that support the object without unacceptable deformation. Mark lifting points, center of gravity, sling lengths and angles on a drawing. Calculate individual demands and check all components. Provide edge protection that stays in place during take-up and movement. Define the load's intended orientation and the measurable acceptance tolerance in the approved plan; 'looks level' is not a repeatable criterion.

Brief the operator and signal person on take-up, trial, travel and landing stages. Establish an exclusion area and escape routes. Remove people from pinch points before tensioning. Take up slack slowly as directed. Check seating and alignment from a safe position; do not pull on a tensioned sling to reposition it. Lift only to the trial clearance specified in the plan, stop and verify balance, brake holding, load integrity and expected forces/clearances.

If the object tilts unexpectedly, lands on one edge, distorts or shifts in its sling, stop and lower to a stable support. Investigate the model and installation. Adjust only after the load is safely supported and the change is authorized. Do not use a worker's body weight to correct balance. Tag lines control appropriate movement from a safe location; they are not structural supports and must not entangle the handler.

Worked case: a calculated eccentric load should remain within the plan's level tolerance, but the first trial raises one end sharply. A sling may be the wrong length, the actual center may differ or an attachment may be binding. Lower and compare actual installation and load documentation; increasing speed to 'get it free' can introduce shock and uncontrolled release.

Application: in the approved low-energy laboratory, record pre-lift predictions, actual trial observations and the final decision. The evaluator must see independent recognition of a discrepancy, not just a successful movement under continuous prompting.


## A11 — Recognize why rotation changes the problem

Rotation changes the position of the center of gravity relative to supports and may change sling angles, contact points, clearances and force directions. A lug strong in one direction may be unsuitable in another. The object can pass through a position where a restraint becomes slack or the center moves beyond the supporting geometry. The rotation plan must address the full path, not only the initial and final positions.

Consider a deliberately simplified warning model. In body coordinates, support A is at (0,0), support B at (4,0), and the center at (2,1) feet. Weight is 1,000 pounds. Assume both support forces stay vertical while the rigid body rotates by phi. The horizontal coordinates become xB=4 cos(phi) and xCG=2 cos(phi)-sin(phi). Moment balance gives RB=1,000[0.5-0.25 tan(phi)] while the denominator is nonzero. RA=1,000-RB.

At zero degrees, RB=500 pounds. At 30 degrees it is about 356; at 45 degrees it is 250; at 60 degrees it is about 67. At approximately 63.435 degrees, RB reaches zero. Beyond that point the model demands a negative reaction at B, which a tensile support cannot provide. At 90 degrees the equation is singular. This model does NOT describe an approved method for turning the load upright. It demonstrates why a plausible initial arrangement cannot be extended through a rotation without analyzing its limits.

Real rigging may not maintain vertical forces or the assumed pivot, so actual calculations may differ substantially. A qualified plan must specify the actual connections, staged positions, allowable forces, controlled supports and load transfer. If an attachment or hoist must be changed, define a stable supported condition for that change. Never improvise while the load hangs.

Application: plot the model's reactions at the stated angles and mark the first invalid condition. Explain why increasing component capacity cannot solve a geometry that requires a sling to push. Then identify what additional engineering information a real rotation would need.


## A12 — Execute rotation and load transfer by hold points

Use a rotation fixture or real task only after its staged plan and equipment have been approved by the appropriately qualified planner. Identify the initial support, each lifting/controlling connection, the final cradle, the permitted direction and the means of preventing unplanned movement. Define measurable intermediate orientations and the hold decision at each. The plan must state where people stand and how the load is safely returned or secured if an abnormal condition develops.

Verify that every attachment is rated for the changing force direction. Establish who commands each hoist or crane and how commands are coordinated. With more than one lifting device, a movement by one can overload another even when total weight is unchanged. Do not assume equal load sharing from equal equipment ratings. Monitor the factors specified by the plan, such as orientation, displacement, clearances or properly selected load measurement; a sensor does not replace a sound force model.

At each stage, stop movement, verify the expected support/rigging condition from outside the danger area and obtain the planned release to continue. Unexpected slack, rapid force change, movement of a support or loss of communication is a stop condition. Use the predetermined safe recovery, not a rushed opposing movement. Keep hands and feet away from the closing space between the rotating object and its cradle.

Worked case: a controlling leg becomes slack earlier than the plan predicts. Do not pull faster on the other hoist. Stop and secure or return the load as the recovery plan permits, then investigate the actual center of gravity and geometry. Early slack can precede an uncontrolled roll or load transfer.

Application: complete an individual controlled rotation on a validated training fixture through the orientations expressly approved for it. Record the actual start, intermediate and final angles, equipment identities, commands and observations. A tabletop explanation is useful preparation but cannot be recorded as physical rotation proficiency.


## A13 — Jack, roll and transfer without losing stability

Jacking and rolling move the load through different support conditions. Verify floor capacity, route slope, openings, joints, obstructions, stopping arrangements and the object's center of gravity. A skate's rating does not prove the floor or load contact can support it. Dollies, rollers and skates must be compatible with the load and their instructions; steering and uneven surfaces can change sharing. Keep people out of the downhill path and pinch zones.

Use a jack with legible capacity, appropriate condition and positive overtravel protection. Provide a firm foundation and suitable cap contact to prevent slip. Raise in the planned controlled increments and promptly secure the raised load with the specified blocking or other support. Hydraulic pressure alone is not a substitute for securing it. Do not reach beneath the object to place blocking by hand where a loss of support would trap you; use the approved safe method.

An A-frame, trolley or beam clamp needs verified support, configuration, restraint and permitted direction of loading. Moving a trolley can introduce new forces and clearances. Do not pull an unverified frame sideways or use a beam flange clamp outside its specified orientation. Inspect the system as a connected load path, including anchorage and the structure beneath it.

Worked transfer: a tall cabinet is lifted from four floor contacts onto two rolling supports. The resulting support polygon is narrower, and a small lateral force can now tip it. Before transfer, verify the center projection remains safely within the planned support area throughout movement and turns, with appropriate engineered restraint. The cabinet's successful stationary lift does not establish rolling stability.

Application: carry out a low-height jack-and-secure exercise with a stable training load, then plan a separate route using skates. Identify each point at which support changes and demonstrate a stop before crossing an unverified floor joint.


## A14 — Communicate, land and respond to abnormal conditions

Use the agreed signal system and a designated signal person for the operation, with any required qualifications. Anybody can give an emergency stop; do not continue because that person is not the usual signaler. Test radios and agree on clear identification, function, direction, distance/speed and stop instructions as applicable. If the operator loses the needed communication or cannot interpret a command, stop safely until communication is restored.

Plan the landing before lifting. Verify support capacity, level, dunnage position, clearances and access for removing rigging. Lower in controlled stages while keeping people away from the underside and pinch points. Confirm stability and full intended support before releasing tension. If the load rocks or the sling is trapped, restore a safe condition and revise the removal method; do not yank the sling out with the crane.

Abnormal conditions include unexpected tilt, binding, support settlement, snagging, excessive wind, sudden force changes, device warnings and a person entering the exclusion area. Give stop, maintain communication and use the approved safe securing/recovery plan. Do not improvise a rescue beneath an unsecured load. Protect the area and summon appropriate emergency assistance where needed. Isolate suspect gear and preserve observations for investigation.

Worked event: the receiving area is obstructed after the lift begins. Do not land on an unplanned surface or hold indefinitely while workers move beneath the load. Use the preplanned safe holding/return option and re-establish the landing area. If no safe option exists, emergency direction must come from the responsible qualified team, not an improvised trainee decision.

Application: demonstrate a communication-loss stop and a controlled landing on the laboratory setup. After unloading, inspect gear, store it to prevent damage and document any defect, unexpected behavior and changed condition.


## A15 — Build a reviewable advanced rigging plan

A useful plan lets another qualified person reproduce the reasoning. Identify the actual object and revision, weight evidence, dimensions, center of gravity, lifting points and structural limitations. Show initial, intermediate and final configurations. Include free-body diagrams, component demands, applicable ratings, installation instructions, inspection identities and the load carried at each boundary. Record assumptions explicitly and resolve them before execution.

Include site/support checks, equipment configuration, weather limits, exclusion zones, path, landing and contingency arrangements. Define roles, communications and hold points. List changes that invalidate the plan: contents, attachment, lifting-point location, sling length, device type, orientation, support or environmental condition. A signature does not make an unsupported calculation correct; reviewers must examine the evidence and model.

Worked review: a plan calculates a sling demand of 5,196 pounds but lists a 5,000-pound applicable rating. The correct decision is no lift with that configuration. Do not round the demand down or use a breaking-strength value as a working limit. Revise the configuration with competent review, recalculate every affected force and update the installation drawing before briefing the crew.

The plan also defines the qualification boundary. Training on a small validated fixture does not establish competence for an industrial vessel, critical lift or multiple-crane operation. Record the actual demonstrated equipment and complexity. Where engineering design or a specialized operator is needed, state that requirement and obtain it rather than awarding authority by course title.

Application: submit a complete plan for the supplied training task. Exchange it for review using the same inputs. Resolve differing answers with diagrams, units and evidence, not majority vote. The approved plan is the basis for the practical, not paperwork completed afterward.


## A16 — Integrated worked review and independent performance

Start with the 3,000-pound assembly from A03: components of 2,000 pounds at x=1 foot and 1,000 pounds at x=7 feet. Supports at x=0 and x=8 feet produce a center at x=3 feet, a right vertical reaction of 1,125 pounds and a left reaction of 1,875 pounds. These vertical reactions can be used directly only for vertical support forces. If a common upper connection creates inclined legs, calculate their actual tensions and horizontal effects from compatible geometry.

Suppose the plan instead uses an approved beam providing vertical lower connections. Verify the beam's permitted support span and unequal loading, then include its own weight in the upper system. A beam advertised with sufficient total capacity may still be unsuitable for the eccentric distribution. Inspect the exact beam data and lifting points. Confirm that both lower connections remain vertical during the planned level lift, and account for any permitted movement.

The crew must now demonstrate the whole sequence: verify the object and plan; inspect and select components; install compatible hardware; establish exclusion and communications; take up slack; perform the low-clearance trial; confirm the expected condition; move through the approved route; land stably; release, inspect and record. An injected paperwork change—an additional component—must trigger a hold and recalculation before the next lift.

Rotation is a separate approved task, not an unannounced extension of this level lift. Use the validated rotation fixture and its own staged plan. Predict changes, execute hold points and stop on a simulated discrepancy before it becomes an actual hazardous condition. The evaluator injects discrepancies by scenario or safe instruction, never by secretly damaging equipment or destabilizing a load.

Independent performance means the learner makes the decisions and explains them without being coached through each step. An evaluator may stop danger at any time. A safe stop is evidence of judgment; continuing a known unsafe condition is a critical failure. Complete the written assessment separately and preserve the practical record. Only actual demonstrated scope may appear in the training achievement record, with employer authorization remaining separate.


# Part III — Signaling

## S01 — Know when signaling is needed and who may do it

The signal person's job is to communicate the intended safe movement while observing the load path and relevant hazards. It is not to operate the crane remotely by assumption, calculate unknown capacity on the fly or replace the lift director. Before a task, establish the operator, designated signaler, lift direction and receiving team's responsibilities. Identify how a worker can call a stop and how the team will resolve a changed condition.

In construction crane work, signaling is required when the operator cannot fully see the point of operation, when travel visibility is obstructed or when site-specific safety concerns make signaling necessary. A camera does not automatically remove the need; evaluate the actual visibility and reliability of the operation. Select a suitable method and ensure the signaler is qualified for it before work.

OSHA provides third-party qualified evaluation and employer-qualified evaluation paths. The employer-only assessment is not portable to another employer. The documentation must identify each signaling method and be available at the worksite. Qualification includes knowledge and practical ability, basic equipment dynamics and applicable rules. Unsafe later performance requires retraining and reassessment before resuming the role. A course attendance record alone does not establish that ability.

Worked case: an employee has only demonstrated hand signals but is assigned to direct a blind lift by radio. Do not treat the hand-signal record as radio qualification. Verify or complete the appropriate knowledge and practical evaluation and document that method before assigning the role. If the planned signaler is absent, the need for a signaler does not disappear.

Practice: examine three synthetic records—attendance only, employer hand-signal qualification and documented hand/radio qualification. Identify what each establishes, what it does not and what must be verified for the actual employer and task.


## S02 — Translate the task into crane functions

Hoisting changes the hook/load elevation through the load line. Booming changes boom angle. Swing rotates the upper works or boom about the crane's rotation axis. Telescoping changes the length of an extendable boom. Trolley travel moves the trolley along a tower-crane jib; crane travel moves the crane itself. These are different functions even when more than one can move the load toward a target. A command must name the intended function, not just point vaguely toward the destination.

Know which line is in use. Main and auxiliary hoists can have different capacities and geometry. The signal selecting a line is followed by the intended action, not interpreted as movement by itself. Verify the operator and signaler agree which line/load is being addressed. Do not direct simultaneous line use or a specialized attachment without the approved procedure and clear communication.

Directions use the operator's perspective. Standing opposite the operator makes your own left and right unreliable as commands. Before the exercise, place a model crane and identify the cab's reference direction, then practice translating targets from several signaler positions. Never change the meaning halfway through a movement because you walked around the load.

Worked case: the hook needs to rise while the boom should remain at its planned angle. A raise-boom signal could change radius and clearance rather than simply raise the hook. Use the hoist function and stop at the agreed point. If the operator responds with the wrong function, stop and resolve the misunderstanding rather than trying to compensate with another signal.

Practice: label the functions on a crane diagram and explain what each does to the hook path. Use a tabletop model to show the difference between trolley travel and whole-crane travel. Do not infer actual capacity from the model.


## S03 — Learn the standard hand-signal vocabulary

Use the official illustrated sheets in `reference/hand-signals.html` during instruction. They contain the complete OSHA Appendix A chart. The small memory cues below are not replacement illustrations. The instructor demonstrates each motion, the learner names it, and then the learner produces it from a task prompt. Practice from the operator's viewing position so a technically correct shape is also discernible.

| Function | Short recognition cue |
|---|---|
| Stop | One horizontal arm sweeps, palm down |
| Emergency stop | Both horizontal arms sweep, palms down |
| Hoist / lower | Upward / downward index circles |
| Raise / lower boom | Horizontal arm, thumb up / down |
| Swing | Horizontal pointing arm |
| Extend / retract telescope | Waist-level thumbs outward / inward |
| Raise boom, lower load | Thumb up; fingers open/close |
| Lower boom, raise load | Thumb down; fingers open/close |
| Dog everything | Hands together at waist |
| Move slowly | Other hand in front of action hand |
| Main / auxiliary selection | Tap head / tap elbow, then action |
| Travel / tower travel | Upright fingers, pushing motion |
| Crawler both tracks | Fists rotate away / toward body |
| Crawler one track | One fist indicates locked track; other rotates |
| Trolley travel | Palm up, thumb direction, horizontal motion |

Do not reduce stop to a stationary raised hand or use dog-everything as an ambiguous substitute for the required stop. Combined boom/load signals have a specific coordinated meaning; they are not permission to give unrelated conflicting commands. Move-slowly modifies the action signal. Watch the actual response and maintain the appropriate signal while movement is requested.

Worked drill: the evaluator shows a lowering hook on a model while boom angle stays fixed. The learner must produce lower, not lower boom. After a safe stop, the evaluator changes the task to reducing boom angle while maintaining the intended load motion. The learner identifies and produces the appropriate charted combined signal and explains the different meaning.

Practice every listed chart entry individually in both recognition and production. For crawler and tower functions, use a model with labeled tracks/trolley so the learner can explain the resulting movement. Correct confused signals before increasing speed or combining a route sequence.

Additional one-handed telescope vocabulary: the manufacturer reference identified in the source register describes extension with a fist in front of the chest and the thumb tapping the chest; retraction uses the outward-pointing thumb while the fist's heel taps the chest. These are not the two-handed inward/outward thumb cues. Demonstrate and assess both separately, and establish the appropriate signal agreement for the actual work. Do not mistake the one-hand form for a special command to extend only one boom section. The manufacturer reference is instructional corroboration, not a claim that this course has audited every current ASME provision.


## S04 — Make hand signals visible and control the handoff

A clear signal at the wrong location is ineffective. Select a position with a view of the necessary load path and a reliable view to the operator, outside swing, crush, fall and travel hazards. Consider sun behind the signaler, dark backgrounds, rain, gloves, reflective clothing and obstructions. A visible hand above a wall may not communicate a full arm motion. Test visibility from the actual operator location before movement.

One designated person directs a crane at a time except that anyone may give stop/emergency stop. Plan handoff at a safe stopped point: identify the incoming signaler, obtain the operator's acknowledgment and make clear when responsibility transfers. Do not let two people compete with slightly different directions. If an obstruction requires another position, stop before moving out of effective communication unless the approved method maintains it.

The standard chart is the baseline. Nonstandard hand signals require the appropriate pre-operation agreement where standard signals are infeasible or do not cover the operation. Do not invent a signal during a lift because the operator seems to understand. A new communication technology needs demonstrated effectiveness or an applicable equally effective consensus-standard basis, not merely a convenient screen.

Worked case: the signaler steps behind a column but keeps signaling. The operator cannot see the full action. Stop operations requiring signals, re-establish a suitable position/method, and resume only after the proper signal is understood. Continuing from the last visible command risks moving beyond the intended limit.

Practice: use safe floor markers and an empty model hook to choose three signaling positions, then identify a visibility failure. Demonstrate the stop, relocation and deliberate handoff. No learner stands in a real load's fall zone to test visibility.


## S05 — Build concise voice commands in the correct order

Before work, the operator, signaler and lift director where present agree on voice signals and communicate effectively in the language used. Repeat the agreement when a participant changes, confusion occurs or the command system changes. Accent, noise or unfamiliar terminology must be resolved by a workable communication method, not by pretending to understand.

The required command sequence is function and direction; distance and/or speed; function and stop. For a training example: 'Hoist up; two feet, slowly; hoist stop.' The stop is given at the planned stopping point as the movement is monitored, not spoken prematurely as one rushed sentence that the operator cannot apply. If more than one crane is addressed, identify the intended crane before function/direction or use an equally effective identification method.

Avoid conversational shorthand such as 'bring it this way' or 'a little more.' Those phrases hide function, direction and amount. Specify the intended function and maintain timely updates consistent with the agreed system. Do not keep issuing movement instructions while distracted from the load. An unclear transmission requires a safe stop and clarification, not guessing the missing word.

Worked case: two cranes share a coordination channel and a voice says 'swing left.' Neither operator should have to infer which crane is meant. The team must establish identification and use it consistently. A signaler facing the opposite direction must still use the operator's perspective. Practice with 'Crane Blue: swing right; slowly, toward the marked limit; swing stop' only after that limit and terminology are agreed and visible to the directing team.

Practice: write commands for hoist, lower, swing, telescope and trolley movements with a final function-stop command. Speak each into a recorder, then have a partner identify the crane, function, direction and amount without seeing the prompt. Correct missing information before the practical test.


## S06 — Set up radio and electronic signaling

Test the actual transmitting and receiving equipment on site before operations. Check battery condition, spare power, channel, microphone, headset and reception at intended positions. Verify that wind, machinery noise, structures and other users do not obscure commands. The operator's reception must be hands-free. A personal phone held to the ear is not the required reception arrangement simply because its audio is clear.

Use a dedicated signal channel under the applicable rule, with its defined coordination exceptions. A shared general conversation channel invites missed or mistaken commands. For multiple-crane coordination, identify recipients reliably and prevent conflicting directions. Define the loss-of-communication response and test it safely before a load moves. Do not switch channels mid-operation without stopping and re-establishing the system.

Video may assist visibility but introduces field-of-view, latency, blind-zone and failure concerns. A picture of the load is not necessarily a picture of the whole route or receiving area. Verify the complete signaling arrangement, not only that a camera displays an image. If required transmission is interrupted, safely stop until the channel and a proper understood signal are restored.

Worked case: a radio passes a test next to the cab but fails behind the structure where the signaler will stand. The operation is not ready. Change equipment, positioning or the approved communication plan and repeat the on-site test at the necessary locations. Do not plan to shout from behind the obstruction as an untested backup.

Practice: set up the training radios, conduct a location-by-location test, demonstrate hands-free reception and deliberately simulate a dead transmission with the equipment unloaded. Record the observed stop and restored communication before resumption.


## S07 — Understand load motion, deflection and stopping

A suspended load behaves like a pendulum. Starting, stopping or changing swing can create motion that continues after the crane function stops. Sudden corrective commands can amplify the swing. Anticipate the route and request controlled movements early enough to avoid chasing the load back and forth. The operator manages the equipment; the signaler communicates the safe intended movement and warns of hazards.

Boom deflection under load can change hook position and radius. A hook positioned over an unloaded object may move as load comes onto the boom. Observe the actual line and load rather than assuming the initial position remains unchanged. Keep the hoist line appropriately vertical; do not direct side dragging or use the crane to pull a snagged load free. A tilted line can impose side loads and produce sudden movement when the obstruction releases.

Load shape matters as well as weight. A large flat panel can catch wind and rotate, while a compact object of the same weight may behave differently. Center of gravity, rigging arrangement and clearances affect stability. A signaler does not approve unknown weight or capacity by intuition. Obtain the established lift information and stop if actual behavior differs from the plan.

Worked case: during take-up, the load moves sideways before leaving its support. Stop and have the responsible team investigate alignment, snagging or changed geometry. Do not order more hoist to force it clear. A safe trial can reveal a discrepancy but cannot substitute for verified capacity and rigging.

Practice: use a small unloaded teaching pendulum or model to compare gradual and abrupt movement. Explain the lag and identify when to request a stop. Do not deliberately generate excessive swing with a real crane as a classroom demonstration.


## S08 — Recognize equipment limits and avoid two-blocking

Two-blocking occurs when the moving hook block or similar lower assembly contacts the upper block/boom-tip assembly. Hoisting upward is one possible cause, but geometry changes such as telescoping can also consume clearance. Watch the relevant clearance and understand the actual configuration. Anti-two-block equipment is a safeguard; it is not a target to routinely reach or a reason to ignore a developing hazard.

Know the operating envelope established by the qualified team and operator: intended functions, radius, boom configuration, load and site limitations. The signaler does not override a load chart, warning or operator safety decision. If a command is unsafe for the equipment, stop and resolve the issue. Confusing main and auxiliary line selection can move the wrong hook toward an obstruction; identify the line before the action.

When crane travel is involved, look beyond the load. Ground conditions, slope, clearance, tail swing, adjacent equipment and the operator's travel view matter. Use the charted travel signal for the actual equipment and operator's perspective. Crawler one-track commands differ from both-track travel and must be demonstrated correctly on a model before any real movement. Tower trolley motion is not a command for the crane to travel along its base.

Worked case: while the boom extends, hook-to-tip clearance shrinks. The signaler must recognize the developing condition and stop the relevant operation, allowing the operator and plan to resolve it. Waiting until a device trips or attempting a guessed simultaneous correction can create an avoidable event.

Practice: identify two-blocking, side loading, wrong-line selection and travel-obstruction hazards from original diagrams or a safe model. State the stop and the information needed before resuming. No live equipment interlock is defeated for assessment.


## S09 — Read the site, weather and electrical hazards

Walk the planned route with the responsible team before signaling. Identify overhead services, structures, swing radius, pedestrians, traffic, receiving edges, floor openings, poor lighting and escape space. Choose positions that let you see necessary clearances without entering the load path. Establish barriers and coordinate with other activities so a delivery or pedestrian cannot unexpectedly enter the operating area.

Power-line safety requires the employer's applicable work-zone assessment and controls. Do not estimate voltage from appearance or assume covered lines are safe. For equipment operations within the scope of 1926.1408, the rule's 20-foot assessment trigger and clearance/control options are not a universal permission to work at any guessed distance. Verify the chosen applicable method, boundaries and responsible personnel before movement. Higher voltages and closer operations have additional requirements.

A dedicated power-line spotter may need specific positioning, visual aids and continuous operator communication. Do not casually combine that dedicated function with competing load-direction tasks. The plan must establish compatible roles and reliable timely information. If the boundary cannot be maintained or the electrical status is uncertain, stop and obtain responsible resolution.

Weather can change visibility, communication and load control. Apply equipment/plan limits and observed conditions; do not invent one universal wind speed for every load. Lightning, gusts, rain and ice may require suspension and safe securing. A sail-like load can become difficult before another nearby lift does.

Worked case: an approaching truck blocks the signaler's safe escape route while a panel is being moved. Stop safely, restore the controlled area and revise traffic coordination. Do not step beneath the panel to regain visibility. Practice identifying two alternative safe positions and the condition that would make each unusable.


## S10 — Blind lifts, multiple cranes and personnel lifting boundaries

For a blind lift, plan how the signaler observes the required route and landing while maintaining communication. A relay arrangement needs deliberate responsibilities and transfer, not a chain of people independently changing commands. The operator must know who is directing at each stage. Stop before a handoff or loss of view makes the next movement uncertain.

Multiple-crane work needs its own qualified plan and direction. Crane identification is essential, but correct wording alone does not validate load sharing or coordination. The signaler must understand the approved sequence and stop criteria without independently redesigning it. A movement by one machine can alter demand on another. If responses diverge, stop under the coordinated plan and resolve the condition.

Personnel lifting is a specialized operation with additional prerequisites, equipment, platform, inspection/test and communication requirements. Ordinary cargo-lift training does not authorize it. The signaler verifies that the applicable personnel-lift plan and roles are established and does not improvise a person-carrying arrangement. This course's practicals use no suspended people.

Worked case: a worker proposes riding a material load to guide its placement because radio reception is poor. Reject that improvisation. Restore an effective signaling method and use the legally appropriate access/lifting arrangements under responsible planning. A communication problem cannot be solved by creating an unauthorized personnel lift.

Practice: review three scenario cards—blind landing, two cranes and proposed personnel platform. List the information you need, identify your signaling responsibility and identify the approval/design decisions outside this course's authority. Stop is a valid and necessary outcome when prerequisites are missing.


## S11 — Stop, clarify, recover and land

Stop early enough to prevent a hazard, using the agreed standard command. If the operator does not respond as intended, do not pile on more movement signals. Stop and establish why: visibility, wrong function, misunderstood direction, equipment response or another person signaling. Resume only when the problem is resolved and a proper command is understood.

At landing, observe the receiving area and clearances. Direct controlled movement while keeping people out of pinch/fall hazards. The receiving team establishes stable support; do not signal release of rigging based only on the load touching something. If the object rocks, dunnage shifts or rigging is trapped, stop and let the responsible team establish a safe correction. The signaler must not enter beneath an unsecured load to inspect it.

During suspected electrical contact or other emergency, stop/warn and follow the established emergency response while keeping people away from the potentially energized equipment/load and surrounding hazard. Call appropriate emergency/utility assistance. Do not touch the equipment or attempt an improvised rescue that exposes additional people. Detailed equipment escape/rescue actions require the site emergency plan and trained personnel.

Worked case: an unidentified worker gives emergency stop while the designated signaler is directing a swing. The operator must stop safely. The signaler resolves the reported hazard rather than dismissing the person as unauthorized. The one-directing-person rule does not suppress emergency warnings.

Practice: demonstrate ordinary stop, emergency stop, communication-loss stop and a restart after a deliberately resolved scenario. Explain why restart is a new deliberate decision rather than automatic continuation of the last command.


## S12 — Put the entire signaling task together

Begin with the task brief and the actual qualification scope. Identify the crane/operator, method, load line, route, landing, hazards, limits and stop/handoff arrangements. Inspect/test the communication equipment, select a safe viewing position and confirm operator perspective. Use the correct command for each planned movement and observe its effect continuously.

In the training route, direct a model or approved low-risk crane exercise through take-up, small hoist movement, controlled swing or trolley movement as applicable, a planned stop and landing. The evaluator introduces only safe verbal/simulated changes: obstruction, radio interruption, wrong response or a new signaler. Recognize the change, stop and resolve it without giving ambiguous competing instructions.

Complete both hand and voice/radio tasks if both methods are to appear on the achievement record. Demonstrate every charted signal separately, including main/auxiliary selection, combined boom/load signals, telescope, crawler and tower functions. Model-based function demonstrations must be identified as such; actual worksite qualification must be determined by the appropriately qualified evaluator and employer for the methods used.

Worked closeout: a learner accurately names every signal but cannot produce a distinguishable stop or reliably identify the addressed crane by radio. That learner has not passed the practical. Provide targeted instruction, change the scenario and reassess independently. Memorizing a chart is only one part of competent application.

Record the exact method, assessment basis, observations and evaluator. Do not state 'all crane work qualified' or imply that an employer-only assessment transfers to another employer. A defensible record describes what the individual actually demonstrated and what remains outside the assessed scope.


# Part IV — Crane-system knowledge

## C01 — Identify the machine, task and limits of your authority

A mobile crane moves a suspended load through a changing working radius. Its advertised tonnage describes a favorable configuration, not every lift. Before accepting work, identify the machine by model and serial number, the installed boom and attachments, the actual configuration, the governing work rules and the employer's authorization. Construction crane requirements under OSHA Subpart CC and general-industry requirements under 1910.180 are not interchangeable simply because the same machine performs both jobs. Ask the employer to establish the applicable work scope before operation.

Lattice-boom cranes use a truss structure with configured sections and often rope-operated boom hoists. They may be crawler-mounted or truck-mounted. Telescopic cranes use nested boom sections; their capacity depends on section extension and the applicable configuration chart. A swing-cab station rotates with the upper works; a fixed station does not. That changes the operator's sight lines and relationship to the controls as the boom rotates. A remote station adds location, communication and emergency-stop issues; it does not eliminate operator responsibility.

Three separate questions govern construction operation: have you received the necessary training, do you hold the required certification or license for the type, and has your employer evaluated you for the equipment and assigned work? Answering only one is insufficient. An employer evaluation must address actual size, configuration, controls, safety systems and tasks. A course certificate cannot establish performance that was never observed. Record restrictions explicitly: for example, telescopic swing-cab practice on one model is not proof of lattice-friction-crane ability.

Operators in training require the supervision specified in 1926.1427(b). Do not accept personnel lifting, multiple-crane lifts, restricted power-line work or other prohibited trainee assignments because a trainer is nearby. This course's practice excludes those tasks entirely. The operator's trainer must be qualified for that equipment, remain onsite and provide the required monitoring without competing duties. Stop if supervision or communication is lost.

Worked decision: a worker holds a current external crane certificate but has never operated the assigned crane's luffing-jib configuration. The correct action is employer-directed familiarization, training and evaluation before that assignment. The certificate is relevant evidence; it is not a substitute for the missing equipment/task assessment. Common error: treating a card's expiration date as the only condition of permission.


## C02 — Plan the lift and establish the operating envelope

Start with the load, its pick point, its destination and every part of the route between them. Obtain a reliable weight, dimensions, center of gravity, lifting-point information and rigging plan. Identify the greatest loaded radius along the entire path, the needed hook height, overhead clearance, rotation sector and boom clearance. Include the block, slings and attached lifting devices in the load accounting. A lift that is possible at pickup may be impossible at placement.

Walk the site with the lift supervisor and signal person. Locate pedestrians, occupied structures, traffic, pits, excavations, utilities, pipe racks and adjacent equipment. Establish a swing/crush exclusion zone around rotating upper works and counterweights, plus load-path controls. Make barriers understandable to everyone who could enter. Coordinate adjacent cranes whose work areas overlap; neither operator should assume the other will move first. Arrange a landing area that supports the load and permits safe rigging removal without trapped hands or uncontrolled rolling.

Define one signal source for normal movement, how control transfers and what happens if visibility or radio contact is lost. Anyone may give a stop or emergency stop. Brief the operator, riggers and signal personnel on the actual sequence and stop conditions. A plan needs a practical way to stop, lower or secure the load if conditions change; 'we will work it out in the air' is not a contingency.

Height calculations support planning but do not replace the range diagram. A simplified right triangle with a 50-foot boom at 60 degrees has about 43.3 feet of vertical projection and 25 feet of horizontal projection from its pivot. The real crane has pivot height and offset, loaded deflection, block dimensions and clearance needs. Therefore these numbers do not independently establish either load radius or hook clearance. Use the manufacturer's diagram and dimensions.

Application: the pick radius is 25 feet, but the far landing corner requires 40 feet. Plan and chart the 40-foot condition, not the easier pick. If a barricade makes the planned route unavailable, stop and replan rather than swinging through a different, unchecked sector. Common error: calling an ordinary lift 'routine' to avoid checking its changed conditions.


## C03 — Ground support, leveling and setup

Crane support must resist the largest reactions produced by the actual operation. Total crane weight divided evenly among four outriggers is not a valid prediction of maximum pad load. Boom direction, counterweight, lifted load and configuration redistribute forces. Obtain the manufacturer's reaction information and a competent site assessment; use engineering where required. Hidden voids, buried tanks, trenches, unsupported slabs, recently placed fill and water-softened soil can fail beneath apparently hard surfaces.

The controlling entity has ground-preparation and known underground-hazard responsibilities under 1926.1402. The operator still must recognize unsuitable conditions and report them. Ask for relevant site information before setup. A missing soil assessment is not repaired by adding random timber. Mats require adequate area, strength, support and placement; bridging a void demands a designed solution. Keep supports away from excavation influence zones as determined for that site, not by a universal guess based on trench depth.

Deploy outriggers, stabilizers or crawlers according to the actual manual. Match permitted outrigger extension positions to the correct chart and control-system settings. Check both beam extension and jack support; a sensor display cannot prove the pad sits on adequate ground. Partial outriggers are acceptable only when the manufacturer permits that configuration and its chart applies. Follow the specified tire condition and crane leveling method. Recheck level and settlement as loads, weather or support conditions change.

Worked example, for reasoning only: an established maximum support reaction is 48,000 pounds and an engineer-specified allowable bearing pressure is 2,000 pounds per square foot. The simple minimum area is 24 square feet. A 4-by-4-foot mat offers 16 square feet, producing 3,000 pounds per square foot if uniformly loaded; it fails that comparison. A 5-by-5-foot area produces 1,920 pounds per square foot, but that calculation alone does not prove mat strength, uniform load spread or ground capacity. Obtain an approved support arrangement.

Stop for pad sinking, mat rotation, unexpected level change or cracking ground. Safely secure the load using the established procedure, clear personnel and have the support condition reassessed. Do not jack or shim a loaded crane as an improvised correction. Common error: accepting yesterday's setup after overnight flooding without reassessment.


## C05 — Assembly, disassembly and safe access

Assembly and disassembly are planned operations under an A/D director who meets the applicable competent/qualified-person requirements. Use manufacturer procedures or properly developed employer procedures where permitted. The operator follows that plan; possessing an operator certificate does not automatically qualify someone to direct assembly. Discuss crew duties, boom support, assist-crane loads, center of gravity, pin-removal order, stability and exclusion zones before work starts.

Lattice sections can collapse when the wrong pins are removed or when support is placed incorrectly. Never stand under a boom while removing pins unless the specific regulatory exception and protective measures apply; this course's practice does not use that exception. Use supports at approved locations and verify the section is unloaded before releasing its connection. Keep hands out of pin and joint pinch points. Never substitute mismatched pins or sections because their dimensions appear similar.

Telescopic attachments and swing-away jibs have their own deployment sequences. Confirm permitted boom angle, support, attachment retention, pin positions and stowage configuration before moving them. An assist crane must remain within its capacity through the changing geometry. Counterweight installation and removal can change backward stability; neither maximum counterweight nor minimum load is automatically the safest condition. Follow the specified erection and dismantling limits.

Use designated steps, handholds and walkways. Keep access clean, carry tools so hands remain available and use approved fall-protection arrangements for the task. The applicable crane fall-protection provisions distinguish work and equipment circumstances; do not assume ordinary platform guardrails protect someone walking a boom. The instructor's equipment supplement identifies actual access routes, anchorages and rescue arrangements before any elevated exercise. No learner climbs a boom during this program's introductory practice.

After assembly, obtain the required qualified-person inspection before use. Scenario: a boom section is pinned in place but the retaining devices are missing. Do not 'make one quick lift' while replacements are found. The assembly is incomplete. Common error: interpreting a successful no-load motion as a substitute for the post-assembly inspection.


## C07 — Rope, reeving, blocks and line pull

A wire rope is an engineered assembly of wires, strands and core. Diameter, construction, grade, lay, rotation characteristics and termination must match the crane manufacturer's requirements. A rope with greater published breaking strength is not automatically an acceptable replacement: bending behavior, drum/sheave compatibility, rotation and design factor matter. Installation, end connections, lubrication and replacement are performed under the required qualified procedures, not improvised by an operator.

Inspect accessible running and standing ropes, especially crossover zones, repetitive pickup points, reverse bends and terminations. Look for broken wires, diameter loss, kinks, crushing, corrosion, birdcaging, heat damage, displaced core and improper fittings. A rope lay is the distance in which one strand wraps once around the rope. Under 1926.1413, running-rope category-II criteria include six random broken wires in a lay or three in one strand in a lay; rotation-resistant rope has different shorter-length counts. Standing-rope criteria differ again. Use the correct rule and any stricter manufacturer requirement; never transfer a running-rope threshold to every rope.

Prior power-line contact, a broken strand or rotation-resistant-rope core failure is not a 'watch it next week' defect. Stop affected operation and follow the required removal/replacement disposition. Do not handle a moving rope or measure it while exposed to a pinch point. Isolate the equipment and use the authorized inspection method for close examination. Wedge sockets, clips and other terminations need the right size, orientation, installation and inspection; an apparently tight improvised connection is not acceptable.

Reeving uses several supporting parts of line to distribute the suspended load. The allowable load depends on the crane's reeving table, permissible line pull, rope, block and system efficiency. Simple multiplication is only a preliminary screen. Example: if an instructional reeving table expressly permits 24,000 pounds on four parts, a 25,000-pound total suspended load fails even if the structural chart permits 30,000. Adding parts must be an approved configuration change, with appropriate rope length, block and reeving—not an improvised fix.

Know the difference between an overhaul ball and a multiple-sheave block. Include their required weights in chart deductions, including an unused suspended device when the manufacturer's notes require it. Maintain required drum wraps; construction operations must not lower below two full wraps, and a manufacturer may require more. Common error: using maximum winch pull as the allowable lifting capacity of the whole crane.


## C08 — Safety devices, operating aids and power systems

Safety devices and operating aids have different failure rules. Required safety devices under 1926.1415 include applicable level indication, boom/jib stops, brake locks, outrigger/stabilizer holding devices and horn. Operations must not begin with a required safety device inoperative; if one fails, stop safely and take the equipment out of service until corrected. Alternative measures are not a general option for these devices.

Operating aids include specified boom/jib limiting devices, anti-two-block protection and indicators for boom angle, length, radius, load or rated capacity as applicable. Under 1926.1416, particular temporary alternatives and repair deadlines may apply to an aid during repair. The operator does not invent an alternative. Stop safely, identify the exact device and rule, notify the employer and follow only a documented compliant arrangement, including more protective manufacturer instructions. The course's learner practice uses functioning aids throughout.

Two-blocking occurs when the hook block or ball contacts the boom-tip or another upper sheave assembly, potentially damaging the rope or dropping the load. Hoisting, telescoping and booming can change the separation. Monitor the geometry continuously; a functioning alarm is not permission to hoist until it activates. If clearance is disappearing, stop the motions creating the hazard and use the model's safe recovery sequence. Do not assume lowering the boom always increases separation.

A load-moment or capacity system depends on correct inputs. Match actual counterweight, boom, jib, outrigger position, reeving and work sector. A false setup entry can produce a reassuring but wrong display. Verify against physical configuration and the manual chart. Never bypass a limiter to finish a lift.

Power may pass through mechanical clutches/brakes, hydraulic pumps/valves/cylinders, electric drives or combined systems. Neutral controls do not mean stored energy is gone. Hydraulic thermal expansion or contraction can affect cylinder position and pressure; load drift demands safe response. Lattice friction systems require practiced coordinated brake/clutch control; hydraulic-crane familiarity is not enough. Application: the display says full outriggers but one beam is at partial extension. Stop and resolve the mismatch before lifting; the electronic number is not the setup.


## C09 — Load charts: select, read and calculate

Read the chart's identity and notes before its numbers. Match model, boom and jib, counterweight, outrigger/crawler condition, tires where applicable, work sector and operating mode. A chart for another configuration is not made valid by reducing its number informally. Know whether a listed capacity includes or excludes the hook block and other devices, and identify every required deduction. The chart's notes are operating restrictions, not optional explanatory text.

Radius is the horizontal distance from the crane's center of rotation to the vertical load line under the relevant loaded condition, not simply boom length or distance from an outrigger. Loaded boom deflection can increase radius. Determine the most restrictive point along the path. When a value lies between chart entries, apply the manufacturer's stated selection rule. Do not interpolate or average capacities unless explicitly allowed. A blank chart cell means no capacity supplied for that condition, not a value to estimate.

Gross chart capacity minus required deductions yields the payload allowance under that chart's convention. Compare the complete suspended load to every applicable limit: chart, reeving/line pull, hook/block, rigging, attachment, support and planned operating conditions. The lowest governing limit controls. A structural-limit region cannot be exceeded merely because tipping is unlikely, and a stability limit is not a promise that the crane will safely lift until it tips.

Worked example: a valid chart supplies 18,000 pounds at the governing radius. Required deductions are a 900-pound block, 300 pounds of slings/shackles and a 600-pound lifting beam. Net payload allowance is 16,200 pounds. A verified 16,500-pound payload exceeds it by 300 pounds. Do not round away the excess. Reconfigure or relocate through an approved new plan and recalculate.

Use the fictional B05 chart for instruction, then examine the applicable actual manufacturer chart under qualified supervision; this combined course does not award operator authority. Finding a printed number is only the first step. Explain why that table and column apply, which notes constrain the lift and what change would invalidate the calculation. Common error: subtracting rigging once in a net calculation and then comparing that net number with a total that subtracts it again.


## C13 — Lattice-boom applications

Identify the installed lattice sections, their approved order, boom length, pendant arrangement, jib and counterweight. Inspect section members and connections for damage, missing pins or inappropriate repair. A bent lattice member is not merely cosmetic. Verify crawler extension or truck-outrigger configuration and the corresponding chart. Erection capacity and boom-raising limits may control even when no payload is attached.

Rope-operated boom hoists, pendants and stops have limits distinct from hydraulic telescopic booms. Monitor boom angle, loaded radius and permitted backward-stability conditions. A near-vertical boom with excessive counterweight or an improperly released load can create backward instability. Multi-drum machines require correct identification of each line, brake and clutch. An inactive drum or hanging block can still create an interference or deduction issue.

On a friction machine, control of hoisting and lowering relies on coordinated clutch and brake operation. Do not transfer familiar hydraulic lever habits to it. Practice begins with the trainer demonstrating the actual power flow, brake holding method and emergency securing sequence without a suspended practice load. Learners operate only after they can identify the controls reliably. The program does not permit free-fall training or personnel hoisting.

For crawler travel, establish the permitted boom direction, load, route and support; crawler movement can alter level and load distribution. A chart valid over one end or side does not automatically apply throughout swing. For lattice truck cranes, check the applicable sector, outriggers and counterweight. The umbrella 'lattice boom' describes related equipment, not identical handling.

Application: a learner finds a chart for a 100-foot boom but the assembled boom includes an extra section. Stop and identify the actual configuration, then obtain its chart and erection/operating limits. Do not use the shorter-boom capacity. Common error: treating a lattice-boom crane as a telescopic machine whose boom can simply be shortened during operation.


## C14 — Telescopic swing-cab and fixed-cab applications

Telescopic extension changes boom length, section overlap and often capacity. Some machines require specific extension sequences or permitted section combinations. Read the actual chart and system configuration, not only total length. Verify whether telescoping under load is permitted, and under which limits. Extending a boom can increase radius and reduce hook-to-tip clearance; simultaneously check capacity and two-block risk.

For swing-cab cranes, the operator rotates with the upper works. Preserve awareness of the counterweight, carrier, outriggers and objects behind the cab. For fixed-cab or fixed-station machines, the boom rotates relative to the operator. Controls and line of sight require deliberate familiarization; do not rely on the visual orientation learned on a swing-cab machine. At remote stations, choose a clear location outside crush/fall zones, protect communication and know the stop response to a lost link.

Match full, intermediate or asymmetric outrigger settings only to configurations expressly supported by the manufacturer. 'One short leg' is not a standard derating. Sensors need correct physical inputs and setup. Tire-supported lifting and pick-and-carry use separate conditions. A roadable boom truck can require a lift configuration very different from its travel setup.

Jib stowage or installation may require deductions even when lifting from the main boom. Apply extension offset, boom angle and other notes precisely. Do not use main-boom capacity for a jib-tip lift. A service-truck crane is not automatically covered by a large mobile-crane practical demonstration; its configuration, controls and rating require their own assessment. This package teaches the distinction and requires explicit scope on records rather than implying every mobile crane subtype was evaluated.

Worked decision: the chart offers a permitted intermediate-extension setup, but the actual left and right beams differ from that diagram. Do not average the extensions. Restore an approved setup or obtain the exact permitted configuration and repeat planning. Common error: entering a favorable setup into the rated-capacity system to silence a warning.



# Part V — Integration

# VNDRLY/U Integrated Rigging and Signaling — integration instruction

This course joins routine rigging, advanced rigging, crane-system knowledge and signaling into one assessed work sequence. The complete coursebook physically includes the relevant VNDRLY instructional chapters, rather than asking the learner to obtain earlier programs. These integration chapters address decisions at the boundaries between specialties. This is an original VNDRLY program, not NCCER certification or a substitute for employer task-specific qualification, licensed engineering or crane operator authority.

## B01 — Coordinate roles without losing independent safety checks

The rigger checks the load, rigging and connections; the operator controls the crane within its configuration and limits; the signaler communicates movement while observing the required route; the lift director coordinates the operation. One person may have more than one demonstrated skill, but that does not mean they can perform competing duties at the same instant. A rigger handling a connection may not be positioned to observe the whole path and give reliable signals. Plan the transition of duties before tensioning.

Start the brief with the actual object and intended sequence. Each role repeats the information it needs: weight and center, rigging configuration, crane setup and governing radius, path, landing, exclusion, communication and hold points. The operator's chart check and the rigger's component check should agree on the supported weights. If one includes a lifting beam and the other omits it, resolve the discrepancy rather than averaging the answers.

Use a deliberate ready-to-lift checkpoint. The rigger confirms the installed configuration and clears the pinch area. The signaler verifies the operator can receive the intended method and that the path is controlled. The operator confirms readiness within the plan. This is not a scripted permission to ignore a new hazard: anybody can call stop. A short checklist works only if its observations are actually made.

Worked case: the same worker finishes placing a sling and immediately signals hoist while still holding the sling at a pinch point. The team stops, establishes safe position and rechecks readiness. Combining titles did not remove the need to clear the connection before take-up. A better sequence assigns a visible transition and lets the operator see that the directing role is established.

Application: draw a responsibility timeline for a lift from initial inspection to rigging removal. Mark periods when a person cannot safely perform two roles. Assign coverage and handoff points rather than adding a generic 'all crew responsible' note that leaves nobody clearly directing.

## B02 — Handle pipe, rebar and bundles as loads with internal movement

A bundle is not necessarily one rigid object. Individual lengths can slide, roll, telescope or escape the rigging. Shipping bands may organize material without being rated lifting attachments. Verify bundle construction, individual lengths, end alignment, weight and the approved lifting method. Do not hook under a band simply because a forklift previously carried the package that way.

For pipe, consider rolling, surface condition, coating damage, sharp ends and whether contents or attachments change weight. Use the approved number and locations of supports and a retention arrangement that controls the actual material. A hitch that squeezes the outer pieces may not restrain shorter internal pieces. Keep people out of the possible end-ejection and rolling paths. Prepare chocks or a suitable rack at landing before moving the bundle.

Rebar combines flexible lengths, protruding ends and potential slippage. Verify the lifting arrangement supports the intended bundle without unacceptable bending or losing individual bars. Protect slings from sharp contact and keep hands away from closing gaps as the bundle settles. Do not straighten, pull or rearrange a suspended bundle by hand. Land and secure before correcting the arrangement.

Worked case: a bundle includes several short tubes hidden among longer ones. A two-point outer arrangement appears balanced, but the shorter pieces are not actually captured. Hold the lift and obtain an approved containment/rigging method for all pieces. Adding a tag line to one long tube does not restrain the short contents.

Application: on an unloaded tabletop bundle, identify every potential moving piece and the direction it could escape. Develop the landing plan and explain which packaging is organizational versus rated structural support. Actual lifting practice uses a verified safe training bundle with a qualified approved arrangement; the evaluator never secretly adds a loose piece to a live load.

## B03 — Use gantries, equalizers and cantilever devices within their configurations

A gantry system transfers load through beams, legs, jacks or wheels into the supporting surface. Its apparent symmetry does not prove equal reactions. Verify permitted span, height, bracing, support, travel conditions, lifting-point positions and manufacturer configuration. A device rated for stationary use may not permit rolling under load. Floor capacity and local wheel/jack reactions need separate verification.

An equalizer device can help distribute forces only within its permitted geometry and travel. If a pivot binds or reaches its limit, the expected equalization may disappear. Do not credit equal sharing without considering the complete load path and approved device data. A spreader typically controls compression forces and sling separation; a lifting beam typically carries bending. Their total marked ratings are not interchangeable configurations.

A cantilever lifting bar has a load offset from the support and may rely on a counterweight or specifically designed geometry. Its stability and member forces depend on distances and connections, not only total weight. A home-made counterweight addition is a design change. Verify actual device ratings, attachment points and the approved load location before use.

Worked teaching model: a 2,000-pound force acts 3 feet from a pivot and a balancing force acts 6 feet on the other side. Ideal moment balance requires 1,000 pounds, before device self-weight or any other forces. That arithmetic does not authorize building a cantilever device: structural strength, stability, retention and manufacturer/design limits remain unverified. The exercise teaches why distance matters, not a recipe for a fabricated lifting appliance.

Application: review a supplied gantry/device manual and label its permitted configuration. Introduce a changed span or offset on paper and identify the specific approval/calculation affected. If the actual manual does not cover the proposed arrangement, the correct answer is hold, not interpolation.

## B04 — Handle and reeve wire rope without damaging or misapplying it

Identify rope diameter, construction, core, lay and intended application from traceable documentation. A rope that fits a groove is not necessarily the correct replacement. Strength, flexibility, rotation behavior and termination compatibility affect suitability. Distinguish a wire-rope sling from a crane running rope; their inspection criteria and assembly requirements differ.

Unreeling and installation must follow the actual rope/equipment instructions. Support the reel properly and control its motion. Avoid dragging rope over sharp edges, introducing reverse bends or pulling a loop tight into a kink. Keep hands away from rotating reels, drums, sheaves and tensioned rope. Use an approved method to control rope ends and stored energy; do not cut a tensioned rope or improvise splicing/termination work beyond competence.

Trace the specified reeving path before installation. Confirm the dead end, sheave sequence, supporting parts, drum direction, required rope length and permitted termination. Check groove condition and guards. Incorrect reeving can create interference, uneven winding or a different load path than the chart assumes. Initial spooling/tension and run-in follow manufacturer procedures; the course does not supply universal values.

Worked case: a proposed replacement has the same diameter as the original but its construction and rotation characteristics are unknown. Do not install it. Obtain the specified rope and verify documentation. If the existing rope has a kink or crushed area, straightening it until it looks round does not restore it to service.

Application: use a bench model with slack non-load-bearing line to trace the approved path, identify each support and spot a deliberately wrong route. Inspect isolated wire-rope specimens for damage and state the applicable rope/slings distinction. Actual crane rope installation requires the equipment-specific qualified procedure and is not awarded by a bench exercise alone.

## B05 — Read the crane envelope as a rigging team member

The crane's lifting capacity depends on configuration and the full route. Identify boom type and length, base/support arrangement, counterweight, attachments, operating sector and actual load radius. A telescopic truck, rough-terrain carrier, all-terrain carrier or lattice crawler has configuration-specific data; a familiar appearance does not select the correct chart. A stationary supported chart is not a pick-and-carry chart.

Ask which radius governs, including landing and possible deflection. A range diagram helps establish reach/height but is not a capacity chart. Gross capacity, required deductions and complete suspended weight must be compared consistently. The rigging team supplies the weights of beams, slings and attachments; the operator/qualified plan applies the actual crane's chart conventions. A blank or out-of-range chart condition is not a number to guess.

Original practice data, not a real crane chart: a fictional stationary configuration has gross capacities of 12,000 pounds at 20 feet, 9,000 at 30 feet and 6,000 at 40 feet. For this exercise only, intermediate radii use the next larger listed radius; no interpolation. Required deductions are 700-pound block, 200-pound slings/connectors and 300-pound beam. Reeving limit is 10,000 pounds total suspended. A 5,000-pound payload at a governing 34-foot radius needs 6,200 pounds total; the applicable chart gives 6,000, so the lift fails by 200 pounds even though reeving alone permits it.

Jib or extension erection/stowage changes setup hazards and may affect chart selection/deductions even when the attachment is unused. Follow the actual assembly/disassembly plan, support/pin sequence and responsible direction. Never remove a pin because a diagram seems familiar or stand under an unsupported component to align it. The rigger's contribution is competent handling within the approved sequence, not independent crane configuration approval.

Application: use the fictional data to document governing radius, total suspended weight and the limiting comparison, then explain which actual manufacturer documents are needed for a real task. Inspect a supplied assembly drawing and identify support-before-release hold points without operating a crane or removing real pins.

## B06 — Signal overhead cranes and derricks without transplanting the wrong function

An overhead bridge crane moves along a runway, while its trolley traverses the bridge and its hoist raises/lowers the load. These axes differ from a mobile crane's swing and boom functions. A gantry may carry a similar bridge on legs. Establish the operator's orientation and the specific equipment's agreed standard signals before directing movement. Do not use a swing command to describe bridge travel merely because the load goes sideways.

A derrick uses a mast or equivalent supported structure with a hoisting system, sometimes with a boom. Its guying/bracing, anchorage and permitted motions differ from a mobile crane carrier. Know which motions actually exist and which line/function is addressed. The plan and equipment instructions govern; this program does not authorize changing guy tension or anchorage to solve a motion problem.

For overhead signaling, practice distinct hoist, lower, bridge travel, trolley travel, stop, emergency stop and slow movement using the applicable standard/manufacturer chart. Teach the resulting movement on a labeled model, then demonstrate from the operator's view. If a function-specific signal differs from the OSHA mobile/tower reference, obtain and use the actual applicable chart and agreement; do not invent a gesture. This course's supplemental reference supplies an original described practice sequence and cites the primary chart used at delivery.

Worked case: a worker says 'left' while facing a pendant operator from the opposite side of the bay. The intended axis and reference are ambiguous. Stop and identify bridge versus trolley, direction and agreed amount/speed. A clear function command is safer than guessing which sideways movement the worker meant.

Application: move model tokens along perpendicular bridge/trolley axes and produce separate intended commands. For a derrick model, identify hoist and boom movement without treating a fixed mast as a traveling crane. Actual scope on the record identifies the equipment/function charts assessed rather than claiming all crane families from mobile-only practice.

Chart-comparison exercise: Michigan's official overhead/gantry Table1 illustrates bridge travel with a forward open-hand pushing motion and trolley travel with a palm-up directional thumb and horizontal hand motion. It identifies a trolley block by one or two raised fingers before the action. Its stop is a rigid horizontal palm-down arm; emergency stop moves that hand rapidly side to side. Its operator indication for a disconnected magnet spreads the hands, palms up. These are **context-specific overhead examples**, not replacements for the OSHA mobile/tower stop gestures taught in Part III. Compare the actual applicable chart before work. A magnet-disconnected indication is not an electrical isolation procedure or permission to enter beneath an unsecured load. Practice all nine entries in the supplied overhead reference and explicitly name which chart context is in use.

## B07 — Manage an integrated change and incident response

Changes cross specialties. A new sling length changes angle and hook height; a changed crane position changes radius and signal visibility; a new landing rack changes stability and removal access. Stop at the change and identify every affected assumption. The operator, rigger and signaler each recheck their part before the lift director releases the revised operation.

When a hazard appears during movement, issue the stop, maintain communication and use the approved safe securing/return option. Do not create a second emergency by entering beneath a load, touching possibly energized equipment or adjusting a connection under tension. Protect the area and summon the appropriate emergency/qualified assistance. Preserve equipment identities and observations for investigation; no field repair is made simply to avoid delay.

Worked case: a radio interruption occurs while a bundle is approaching a landing rack, and the rack has shifted. Restoring the radio alone is insufficient. The team must also restore or revise the landing support and verify the bundle/control arrangement before restarting. Clearing one symptom does not clear the entire changed condition.

Application: complete a change sheet with original condition, observed change, affected weight/geometry/capacity/communication/support assumptions, required reviewer and restart evidence. Practice the stop verbally on a safe model; never create a real support failure for an exercise.

## B08 — Demonstrate the combined work, not three disconnected quizzes

The combined capstone begins with an original work order for a verified training load and route. The learner checks load/center, chooses and inspects rigging, explains the crane-chart boundary, assembles connections, briefs signals and demonstrates safe take-up, trial, movement and landing. The learner changes roles only at planned safe handoffs. A qualified operator remains responsible for actual crane controls.

The advanced portion uses the separately validated rotation/transfer fixture and its approved stages. The learner identifies changing forces and stops on a simulated discrepancy. The signaling portion requires both recognition and production, hand and voice/radio tasks, and the equipment-specific functions claimed. Bench demonstrations and live tasks are recorded distinctly. No amount of correct arithmetic substitutes for observed safe connection or signaling performance.

Worked grading decision: a learner rigs correctly but gives an ambiguous radio command to the wrong named crane. The combined task fails its safety gate even if the load was never moved because a simulator was used. Correct the communication gap and reassess a changed integrated sequence. Conversely, a learner who deliberately holds on missing capacity data is making the correct safety decision, not failing to 'finish the lift.'

Application: write the final work record with the actual equipment, method, calculated demands, observed stops, evaluator and limits. External NCCER certification is not awarded by this package. The VNDRLY result states the instruction and demonstrated scope without claiming external endorsement, engineering authority or universal job qualification.

## RI-A — Job briefing and load verification

## SG-P03 — Visibility, position and handoff

Task: choose a safe position on the marked model route, direct a short sequence, then respond to a simulated obstructed view and hand off to another designated signaler while stopped.

Criteria: 1 sees necessary route and is discernible to operator; 2 remains outside marked danger areas; 3 uses operator perspective; 4*stops before required communication is lost and prevents conflicting signalers; 5 deliberately transfers responsibility and resumes only after acknowledgment. Expected: no walking under the represented load or continuing from memory behind an obstacle.

## SG-P04 — Ordered voice commands

Task: give independent hoist, swing, telescope and trolley commands for supplied model movements. Each has function/direction, distance or speed and function-stop. Repeat with a second named crane and changed signaler position.

Criteria: 1 correct crane/line identification; 2 correct function and operator-perspective direction; 3 intelligible distance/speed; 4*clear timely function-stop and no ambiguous recipient; 5 observes response and stops/clarifies a deliberately wrong simulated response. Expected: commands are delivered as the movement proceeds, not a rushed recital. Record actual spoken sequence.

## SG-P05 — Radio setup and interruption

Task: inspect/setup radios, demonstrate a dedicated signaling arrangement and hands-free reception, test at required positions, then direct a model task with a simulated transmission failure.

Criteria: 1 battery/spare/channel/device check; 2 on-site/location clarity test; 3 suitable hands-free receiving arrangement; 4*safe stop during interruption with no guessed continuation; 5 re-establishes communication and proper understood command before restart. Expected: a near-cab test alone does not cover a known dead zone. Record each location and method assessed.

## SG-P06 — Dynamics and equipment hazards

Task: use models/diagrams to identify swing lag, boom deflection, side pull, wrong line, two-blocking risk and crane versus trolley/track travel. Choose the safe signaling response to each.

Criteria: 1 explains momentum and controlled stopping; 2 recognizes deflection/alignment effects; 3 distinguishes equipment functions/line selection; 4*stops unsafe side pull/two-blocking rather than driving to an interlock; 5 obtains responsible resolution before restart. Each named hazard requires a documented response, not one generic 'stop' for the whole station.

## SG-P07 — Site and specialized-operation decisions

Task: inspect a synthetic site drawing with electrical boundary, pedestrian path, blind landing and a weather change. Then evaluate two-crane and proposed person-riding-load scenarios.

Criteria: 1 safe position/exclusion/escape selection; 2 identifies applicable electrical plan/roles rather than guessing voltage; 3 accounts for visibility/wind and actual limits; 4*rejects unauthorized personnel lifting or unplanned coordination; 5 identifies qualified planning and communication prerequisites. Expected: do not confuse a signalperson certificate with approval of every lift. All hazards are simulated, not introduced into an active operation.

## SG-P08 — Integrated route, stop, landing and record

Task: brief and direct a complete approved model or actual low-risk route using hand signals, then voice/radio. Include a controlled stop, handoff or method change while stopped, simulated unexpected response and stable landing verification.

Criteria: 1 complete prebrief/method verification; 2 accurate sustained commands with observation; 3*honors emergency stop from anyone and resolves discrepancy; 4 controlled landing without premature release or danger-area entry; 5 records assessed methods and actual setup honestly. Expected: independent application, not evaluator-led movements. A record must identify simulation versus actual equipment and does not claim crane operator competence.
