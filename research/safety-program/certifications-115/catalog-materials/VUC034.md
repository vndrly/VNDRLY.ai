# VNDRLY/U Offshore Aviation — Helicopter Escape

Original VNDRLY instruction, version 1.0, 17 November 2026. A standalone passenger emergency and helicopter-escape course, not a pilot qualification or a replacement for the whole offshore initial-safety program. It does not issue OPITO HUET approval. EBS and CA-EBS variants are recorded separately. The included survival reference provides the complete relevant prior teaching; these ten chapters develop the aviation-specific decisions and exercises.

## H01 — Readiness for an aviation emergency course

The course combines passenger knowledge with controlled physical performance. A learner must be able to understand the briefing, communicate a stop and carry out the required actions safely. Agree language and accessibility support before the exercise. Translating theory does not solve a communication problem during immersion unless the safety signals and response are also understood.

Use the required medical screening route for the location and activity. Report new illness, breathing or ear concerns, medication effects and conditions affecting participation through a confidential channel. Qualified medical personnel resolve uncertainty. A swimming background or previous successful flight does not establish fitness for underwater compressed-air training. An instructor must not diagnose a condition away to keep a course on schedule.

Understand the exercise progression and the right to stop. The facility demonstrates the stop/distress signal and its recovery response on land. A learner who cannot reliably communicate it is not ready for immersion. Stopping safely is appropriate; it leaves an incomplete requirement to resolve later rather than a full pass. Do not conceal a problem because other learners seem confident.

Worked case: a learner can read the translated coursebook but does not understand the controller's spoken instruction. Establish effective briefed communication and demonstrate comprehension before the practical. If that cannot be achieved safely, hold the exercise and arrange suitable support. The correct outcome is not to depend on copying the person in the next seat.

## H02 — From shorebase to the assigned seat

Check destination, identity, manifest and baggage through the carrier's process. Resolve mismatches before boarding. Follow prohibited-item and loading instructions; do not bring an undeclared article aboard because it fits in a pocket. Secure loose objects and keep assigned routes clear. On a helideck, obey ground-crew direction and remain away from rotor hazards. A dropped article is reported, not chased.

The assigned seat matters because it determines restraint, reach and escape options. Do not casually change seats after briefing. Identify the nearest usable exit and a realistic alternative, then locate the actual release controls. Aircraft may float, settle or capsize after ditching; do not assume the cabin will remain upright or dry long enough to improvise. Follow the specific briefing rather than treat these possibilities as a forecast of every event.

Listen for both warned and unanticipated emergency instructions. Know the means of communicating a problem to the crew without interfering with flight duties. If the restraint cannot be secured or a control cannot be reached, tell the crew before departure. Travel acceptance can also depend on region/operator requirements such as verified body measurements; those are not waived by this course.

Worked case: an employee is offered a different seat and says the nearest window looks identical. They verify the new seat's actual exit operation and restraint before departure. Practice identifying primary and alternate routes on two different diagrams, marking unknown features for clarification rather than inventing an answer.

## H03 — Build a usable survival ensemble

Inspect and don the specific issued suit, lifejacket and breathing system with its instructions. Identify each item's function: thermal protection, buoyancy, breathing or signaling. Do not assume a buoyant garment also provides the required thermal or respiratory protection. Check condition, service status, fasteners and compatibility; the entire ensemble must fit the wearer and allow essential movement while seated.

Route straps correctly and secure excess without obstructing controls. Locate inflation and breathing controls by touch after donning, with the restraint in place. A helpful buddy check supplements the wearer's understanding. If a closure is reopened or a strap adjusted, repeat the relevant check. Reject damaged or uncertain equipment through the proper process; temporary improvised repairs are not acceptable for life-support use.

Inflation timing is critical. A lifejacket inflated inside a submerged cabin can impede escape and force the wearer against the structure. Follow the equipment/aircraft sequence, clearing the cabin before the appropriate inflation action. After escape, establish effective flotation and use the taught backup method if needed. Do not confuse the instruction not to inflate inside with an instruction to abandon flotation outside.

Worked case: a correctly sized suit has a pocket accessory that snags the buckle during dry rehearsal. Remove or resolve the incompatible configuration and repeat the seated reach test. The learner demonstrates inspection, donning, buddy check and control reach before any water assessment.

## H04 — Breathing systems are not interchangeable

Identify the actual emergency breathing system before learning its operation. Rebreather equipment and compressed-air equipment have different principles and deployment methods. CA-EBS contains a finite air supply delivered through the unit's breathing arrangement; it is not a diving system, a fire respirator or permission to search underwater. The supply duration is not a universal number and varies with equipment and use.

Use the complete model-specific readiness procedure. A gauge alone does not prove the breathing path is configured correctly. Inspect the fitted components, their service condition and the approved supply/functional indications. Have the instructor demonstrate deployment, mouthpiece seal, any nose clip, clearing and displaced-mouthpiece recovery for the actual unit. Learn and practise dry before supervised water use. Do not operate an unknown control because another device used a similar shape.

Compressed-air breathing introduces pressure-related hazards even in shallow water. Follow the controlled breathing and movement method; do not hold breath during ascent after breathing compressed air. Report pain, coughing that prevents safe participation, breathing difficulty or distress immediately. Do not deliberately purge away air or repeatedly experiment with a poorly understood valve. The instructor controls any approved clearing exercise and rescue response.

Worked case: the unit indicates pressure but its supply configuration is unclear. The learner holds use and requests a competent check rather than relying on color. On the dry trainer, they explain the air path and perform the actual readiness sequence, demonstrating understanding rather than reciting a brand name.

## H05 — Prepare for a warned emergency landing

A warning creates an opportunity to prepare, not permission to release restraints early. Follow crew directions for posture, equipment and the nominated exit. The correct brace position depends on seat and restraint. Secure loose articles and identify the stable reference that will support orientation. Locate the release and exit controls before visibility or position changes.

Keep the restraint secured through the required phase. Releasing while the aircraft is still moving violently can cause injury and remove the person's known position. The actual aircraft/trainer procedure establishes the sequence for opening the exit and releasing the restraint. Maintain a dependable reference when changing hand position; a loose bag or another panicking passenger is not a stable anchor.

Emergency landing on land and ditching into water are different conditions. On land, use the crew-directed safe exit and move to the designated safe area away from aircraft hazards. In water, follow the taught escape and flotation sequence. If immediate independent action is necessary because no usable crew direction is available, apply the aircraft-specific emergency briefing and current hazards; this does not mean invent a new procedure.

Worked case: on a dry trainer cue, a learner reaches for the buckle before adopting the instructed brace. Stop and correct the sequence on land. Practise again with a changed exit instruction so the learner demonstrates both preparation and attention rather than a memorized motion.

## H06 — Maintain orientation through escape

Water and inversion can make a familiar cabin seem unfamiliar. The learner therefore establishes a known reference and rehearses the actual route before exposure. During the approved escape sequence, maintain orientation, operate the actual exit and release the restraint at the taught stage. Avoid rushing into an unknown opening or following a light without knowing where it leads. Keep the path clear for other occupants once outside.

Training is progressive: explanation, demonstration, dry coached practice, independent dry performance and then the validated water conditions. The controller specifies seat, exit, visibility, rotation and equipment for each condition. Do not combine breathing-system exercises and immersion conditions casually; the approved plan determines where equipment is worn or excluded. Rescue personnel remain ready throughout.

If the learner cannot complete the sequence, the briefed stop/distress signal initiates recovery. The instructor reviews the actual difficulty, checks wellbeing and selects an appropriate safe retraining step. Repeated exposure without resolving the cause is not instruction. An assessor must observe critical actions; surfacing alone does not prove correct restraint timing or exit operation.

Worked case: a learner surfaces safely but the observer saw the restraint released before the required reference was established. The attempt is not an independent pass. Record the sequence error, retrain and assess a fresh approved condition. Practice explaining why a successful outcome can still conceal an unsafe method.

## H07 — Deal with a blocked exit or disrupted sequence

The primary route may not be usable. Preparation includes knowing an actual alternative, not merely promising to find one in the emergency. In dry instruction, identify the alternative opening, the route to it and the controls involved. A window that looks convenient may not be an exit. The crew briefing and trainer procedure establish what can be operated and how.

A blocked route does not justify inflation inside the cabin, pushing another occupant into danger or abandoning all orientation. Maintain the taught reference and use the approved alternative sequence. During training, obstructions and changed exits are only those in the validated, briefed scenario. Staff must retain immediate recovery access; they never physically trap a learner to create realism.

Separate a learner problem from an equipment fault. If a control does not behave as expected, the exercise team stops and checks the trainer. Do not tell the learner to use more force against an unverified fault. Defective equipment is isolated and corrected by competent staff before reuse. A rescheduled attempt is preferable to normalizing a jammed release.

Worked case: the primary dry-trainer exit is marked unavailable and the learner cannot identify an alternative. Return to route instruction. Do not progress to submersion to encourage improvisation. In a later approved assessment, the learner demonstrates the learned alternative with the correct controls and a clear recovery plan.

## H08 — From cabin escape to raft survival

Once clear, establish effective flotation, protect the airway and regain control of breathing. Cold water can cause immediate breathing disruption and rapid loss of useful movement. Avoid an exhausting swim toward a distant object when a nearby raft or rescue plan offers a better refuge. Keep signaling aids accessible and use them as taught; do not discard protective equipment merely to move faster.

Approach and board a raft using its designated aid and coordinated assistance. Avoid lines and entanglement points. Move to an assigned balanced position so others can board. The designated person manages the raft's mooring, sea anchor, canopy and supplies through the actual instructions. Do not cut or release a line merely because it is in the way. Protect survivors from wind, water and further heat loss.

Keep watch and communicate relevant observations. A deteriorating companion needs prompt rescue/medical attention. Follow the rescue crew's instructions for a strop, basket or other device; never improvise a connection to clothing. Ground demonstrations do not qualify a learner to run a winch. If someone is recovered unresponsive, capable rescuers and trained first aiders apply the appropriate emergency response; this course does not confer an unrelated advanced medical or rescue-team qualification.

Worked case: survivors are crowded at one side of the raft and a tired person is still in the water. They coordinate a taught boarding assist while redistributing safely, keeping the person's airway clear. The assessor records each individual's actions rather than awarding a group pass to everyone present.

## H09 — Recover from a stopped exercise and learn accurately

A stopped exercise provides information. First recover and check the learner, then distinguish medical symptoms, equipment malfunction, misunderstood instruction and a skill error. Each requires a different correction. Medical uncertainty goes to qualified review; a faulty trainer goes to competent maintenance; an unclear instruction is retaught; a skill is practised at the appropriate earlier stage.

Debrief with observable facts. Instead of saying the learner panicked, describe that they lost the reference and released early. Ask what they perceived without pressuring them to agree with an assumption. Confirm the intended sequence and demonstrate it again safely. Offer adequate rest and a new readiness check before reassessment. Do not punish a learner for using the stop signal or promise a pass merely for trying again.

The assessor records prompts, assistance and rescue intervention. A later independent success is a new result, not permission to delete the earlier one. Different conditions may require separate observations. A medical deferral or unobserved critical action remains outstanding until valid evidence resolves it.

Worked case: a learner asks to stop after coughing during breathing practice. The team recovers safely and follows the medical/readiness process rather than immediately repeating the same exposure. The record names the incomplete condition and the next safe action without unnecessary clinical disclosure.

## H10 — Keep training, travel and credentials separate

The final decision must state exactly what was taught and demonstrated: equipment variant/model, trainer conditions, date, evaluator and limitations. A generic underwater-escape label can imply more than the evidence supports. A learner who completed dry work only receives a dry/partial record, not a full water-performance credential. A written pass cannot erase a critical practical failure.

VNDRLY owns this course; it does not issue another organization's approval. A carrier or employer separately verifies the training it accepts, medical requirements, destination induction and other travel conditions. Helicopter-escape training alone is not the complete offshore safety induction, a firefighter qualification or authorization to enter dangerous water for rescue.

Update readiness when circumstances change. A new aircraft, different EBS, altered fit, new health condition or applicable refresher rule may require additional action before travel. Preserve evidence and make unresolved restrictions visible to the responsible coordinator. Never assume a date printed on a card guarantees suitability for every future flight.

Worked case: a dispatcher asks whether a learner can travel on a different breathing-system variant after passing this course. The response states the actual assessed variant and refers the conversion/acceptance decision through the proper route. Practice writing a record that is useful for dispatch without overstating competence.

# VUC034 delivery and individual evaluation

## Delivery design

Audience: offshore helicopter passengers needing initial or renewed escape instruction. Record the selected EBS/CA-EBS variant and actual equipment; do not present a variant-free credential. This is a standalone original VNDRLY program. It is not OPITO-approved HUET, a full BOSIET program or a pilot/rescue qualification.

Plan **16 instructional hours**, excluding breaks: readiness/passenger preparation H01–H02,2 hours; ensemble/breathing H03–H04,4 hours; emergency preparation/escape/alternative route H05–H07,6 hours; survival/recovery H08–H09,3 hours; final and scoped record H10,1 hour. Practical observation is embedded; add remediation rather than compress safety controls. The schedule is VNDRLY's planning choice, not an OPITO hour claim.

Joining instructions describe physical demands, immersion/inversion, breathing-system exposure, fitness disclosure and stop rights. Verify identity, language comprehension, confidential medical clearance and jurisdiction-specific participation requirements before practicals. Repeat day-of health review. A course record is not medical clearance. Defer affected activities when uncertainty cannot be resolved safely.

Use a purpose-designed HUET facility, competent controller/operators, designated rescue personnel, appropriate dive supervision, breathing-equipment competence and medical response arrangements. Record staffing, positions and capacity from the validated current facility plan. Do not invent or reduce ratios. Verify trainer stops/recovery, rescue access, communications, water/environment conditions, equipment service and hygiene before each block. Dry prerequisites must pass before immersion. No private pool/vehicle improvisation, surprise inversion, forced participation, air deprivation or breath-hold competition.

Required resources: current model-specific instructions, suits/lifejackets and EBS trainers, static seat/exit trainer, validated immersion trainer, rescue equipment, approved pool/raft, inert rescue-device demonstration and assessment forms. Demonstrate controls and stop response. The facility attaches its approved condition matrix listing seat, exit, orientation, equipment worn/excluded and recovery arrangements for each required exercise. CA-EBS is not automatically worn in every immersion condition; additional shallow-water breathing practice is a separately controlled activity. Current clinical/medical and manufacturer instructions govern, not a guessed setting in a generic course.

Stop for distress, pain, breathing difficulty, equipment malfunction, misunderstood instruction or loss of rescue control. Recover, review and document before any restart. Assess actual individual actions; the instructor must be able to see critical performance or use another validated observation method. No credit from simply reaching the surface.

## Assessment rules

Ten stations, each five criteria scored0 absent/unsafe,1 safe but prompted/incomplete,2 independently correct. Pass 8/10 and all starred criteria2. Multiple conditions within a station need separate records. Stop unsafe attempts; remediate the identified gap and use a changed approved reassessment. Preserve original attempts and actual assistance. Medical deferrals remain outstanding.

Written final:30 short-answer items,60 minutes,60points; pass 48/60 and at least 4/6 per H-domain, with every dangerous misconception resolved. Protected key and changed cases remain instructor-only. Full VNDRLY scoped completion requires written and all applicable practical conditions; otherwise issue partial/not-yet-competent status. No external credential, travel acceptance or actual learner completion is generated by these materials.

Setup: joining instructions, fictional fitness/language cases, stop-signal demonstration. Learner explains demands, discloses a fictional change through the correct channel and demonstrates understanding of safety communications.

Criteria: identifies relevant readiness change; *holds unresolved fitness issue; uses confidential review route; *demonstrates understood stop signal; distinguishes attendance from completion. Changed case: translated theory but misunderstood controller instruction. Resolve communication before water.

Criteria: correct manifest/destination resolution; respects crew/rotor boundaries; *locates release; *identifies actual primary/alternate exits; secures loose articles. Changed case: aircraft/seat change after briefing. No live rotor exposure.

Criteria: rejects defect; *correct closures/strap fit; *essential controls accessible; verifies compatibility after adjustment; explains correct inflation timing. Changed case: accessory snags the buckle. Resolve before progression.

## P04 — Breathing-system operation

Setup: identified EBS/CA-EBS training model and current instructions. Demonstrate inspection, deployment, seal and prescribed clearing/recovery dry; learner performs independently in each required hand/condition. Conduct applicable shallow-water CA-EBS conditions only with separate clearance and facility controls.

Criteria: identifies variant/limits; *complete readiness configuration; correct deployment/seal; *approved clearing/recovery and breathing method; responds correctly to stop/distress. Changed case: dry pressure-indicated/supply-uncertain card. Never secretly cause actual breathing failure. Record each water condition independently; dry pass cannot cover it.

## P05 — Warned emergency preparation

Setup: static trainer, actual restraint/exit procedure and crew-cue cards for land landing versus ditching. Learner identifies reference, braces and follows the proper preparation/release sequence.

Criteria: *correct brace/restraint timing; maintains stable reference; locates nominated exit; follows current crew cue; distinguishes land/water actions. Changed case: different nominated exit in dry conditions. Correct mistakes before immersion.

## P06 — Controlled underwater escape

Setup: validated HUET facility with controller/rescue team and passed dry prerequisites. Run each required seat/exit/partial-submersion/inversion condition in the approved matrix, recording equipment worn/excluded and observed sequence.

Criteria: *orientation/reference maintained; *restraint/exit sequence correct; *condition-specific breathing-equipment rules observed; clears without premature flotation; reaches safe recovery point. Changed reassessment: another approved condition after review. No surprise or unapproved obstruction. Unseen critical action, intervention or unsafe sequence prevents independent credit.

## P07 — Alternative route and fault decision

Setup: dry trainer with a marked unavailable primary route and an approved alternative; a separate fictional control-fault card. Learner demonstrates the alternate and identifies when equipment must be held. Water alternatives only if specifically authorized in the validated progression.

Criteria: identifies real alternative; *does not invent an exit; maintains reference; *stops on unresolved control fault; reports for competent correction. Changed case: alternate cannot be identified. Correct result is further instruction/hold, not forced immersion.

## P08 — Flotation and raft recovery

Setup: controlled pool/rescue cover, survival ensemble, raft and ground-level inert rescue device. Observe airway/flotation, feasible refuge decision, safe boarding and recovery-interface response. No uncontrolled sea entry or live winch operation.

Criteria: *effective flotation/airway; controlled breathing/energy decision; avoids line entanglement; *boards/assists without displacing protection; follows rescue-device instructions. Changed case: tired survivor and crowded boarding point. Record individual contributions and assistance.

## P09 — Stop, diagnose and reassess

Setup: fictional event log showing either misunderstood instruction, learner skill error, new symptoms or trainer malfunction. Learner/instructor role-play recovery, factual debrief and correct next step; no hazard is deliberately created.

Criteria: *prioritizes safe recovery; distinguishes cause using facts; *uses qualified medical/maintenance route when indicated; selects suitable retraining stage; preserves first result and changed reassessment. Changed case: cough/distress during breathing practice rather than a dry skill error.

## P10 — Completion and dispatch boundary

Setup: mixed fictional exam/station records and a requested equipment conversion. Learner prepares the completion decision and dispatch handover.

Criteria: reconciles every required condition; *does not erase critical failure; states actual variant/model; *does not claim external approval/travel clearance; identifies outstanding work and follow-up. Changed case: high written score with unobserved inverted condition. Partial result remains necessary.

# Included survival teaching

Nine complete reviewed VNDRLY chapters support H01–H10. Their original D-identifiers are retained for mapping. These reference chapters do not confer a separate initial-survival qualification.

## D01 — Readiness and the authority to stop

Offshore emergencies combine isolation, restricted exits and limited time. Training therefore needs both correct decisions and demonstrated actions. A person who can describe a harness but cannot release the actual buckle has an unresolved skill gap. A person who can operate equipment but conceals a health condition also creates a preventable risk. The instructor's job is to make both limitations visible before exposure, not to force everyone through the same exercise regardless of condition.

Read joining instructions, disclose relevant conditions confidentially and confirm the medical pathway for the actual training jurisdiction and activity. Fitness for ordinary offshore employment and fitness for compressed-air shallow-water training are not interchangeable assumptions. Respiratory or ear/nose/throat concerns, a new illness, medication effects or anxiety affecting participation require review through the designated medical route. Instructors do not diagnose or override restrictions. A declaration made last month does not describe a new chest infection today. Stop and report a change before entering the water.

Learn the stop signal on land. Any learner can stop an exercise without being penalized for protecting safety. The team must acknowledge the signal, stabilize the learner, recover safely and review before restarting. An incomplete activity remains incomplete; it is not silently converted to a pass. Privacy matters: the class needs to know an exercise is postponed, not another person's diagnosis.

Worked case: Lena holds an offshore medical but reports painful ears on arrival. A colleague says the pool is shallow so it cannot matter. Lena reports the change; the responsible medical and training personnel decide fitness before pressure exposure. Completing the classroom portion does not authorize an underwater attempt. Practice stating the stop signal, identifying the medical contact and separating attendance from demonstrated competence.


## D03 — Passenger preparation and the helicopter briefing

Before boarding, confirm identity, destination, manifest, baggage and prohibited-item controls. Tell dispatch about a mismatch rather than boarding on someone else's place. Equipment must fit the individual and the aircraft seating arrangement. Where a shoulder-width measurement or other operator requirement applies, verify it through the required process; do not estimate from clothing size. Travel clearance, medical clearance and survival training are separate checks.

Listen to the briefing even if the aircraft looks familiar. Identify the actual seat restraint, nearest and alternative exits, how an exit opens, brace position, EBS controls and lifejacket inflation method. Practise only as instructed. A familiar-looking buckle may operate differently. Ask before departure if you cannot reach or understand a control. Loose straps, baggage or clothing must not obstruct another passenger's escape path.

Approach or leave only when directed by the ground crew and within the approved route. Keep clear of the tail rotor and other restricted areas. Secure loose articles and carry items as instructed. Never chase a dropped object near a helicopter. If a hat blows away, stop and tell the crew; the object is replaceable, a passenger entering a rotor hazard is not. During flight follow crew instructions and remain restrained until the appropriate release instruction or emergency sequence applies.

Worked case: Ravi changes seats after briefing and assumes the same exit remains his primary route. He must understand the new seat's restraint, reference points and exits before departure. The lesson is not to memorize one direction, but to build an aircraft-specific escape plan. In the dry trainer, identify these controls by touch as well as sight.


## D04 — Survival clothing and lifejacket readiness

An immersion suit reduces exposure; a lifejacket provides flotation. A combined approved system can perform several functions, but no item should be credited with an unverified capability. Check the issued system against its instructions: identity and service status, visible damage, closures, correct size, fitted straps, accessible inflation controls and survival aids. Obtain a replacement for a defect rather than taping it over. A badly fitted lifejacket may ride up; a suit closure left open can admit water and defeat protection.

Don in the demonstrated sequence and complete the matching buddy check. The wearer must still understand the equipment after a helper checks it. Confirm reach to restraint and breathing system with the complete ensemble. Do not add unfamiliar accessories that can snag or obstruct controls. Adjustments made after inspection require another relevant check. Training equipment must be distinguished from flight-service equipment so a depleted training cylinder or inert component is never issued for travel.

Follow the helicopter procedure for flotation. Premature inflation inside a cabin can obstruct movement and trap a person against the interior. Inflate only at the appropriate point after clearing the aircraft, as taught for that system and emergency. Outside the aircraft, confirm effective flotation and use the taught backup inflation method if necessary. Do not remove flotation merely because swimming feels easier without it.

Worked case: a passenger's suit fits while standing but prevents reaching the buckle when seated. The correct response is to resolve the fit before boarding, not to hope the emergency will allow more room. Practice the seated reach check, identifying a deliberately tagged defective training item and explaining why inflation timing matters.


## D05 — EBS and CA-EBS: function, limits and checks

A rebreather EBS and a compressed-air EBS cannot be treated as interchangeable. A rebreather depends on its specific breathing-bag and deployment arrangement; a CA-EBS supplies a finite volume of compressed breathing air through its designed regulator. Neither is a general diving system, smoke-escape respirator or authorization to remain underwater. The purpose is limited emergency escape. Learn the exact unit's pre-use inspection, deployment, breathing and recovery instructions with a qualified instructor.

A pressure indication alone does not prove air is available at the mouthpiece. NOPSEMA's 2023 alert described a flight on which multiple cylinders were not correctly configured despite checks. The lesson is to check the complete approved configuration and function through the operator/manufacturer procedure rather than rely on a reassuring gauge color. Do not invent a universal valve direction, pressure threshold or duration: those depend on the unit. Report damaged hoses, contaminated mouthpieces, inaccessible controls, uncertain valve state or an unsuccessful check.

For compressed-air training, pressure changes create hazards distinct from ordinary swimming. Do not hold your breath while ascending after breathing compressed air. Do not descend, ascend or try underwater deployment beyond the instructed controlled exercise. Shallow water does not remove the need for fitness screening, supervision and rescue arrangements. Ear pain, breathing difficulty, chest discomfort or distress means stop and follow the rescue/medical response. Never force pressure equalization or keep attempting because another learner found it easy.

Worked case: a learner practiced with a rebreather last year but receives CA-EBS today. They need the relevant equipment instruction and assessment, not a handwritten conversion on the certificate. During dry practice identify each control and explain the finite-air and pressure-exposure limitations. Actual underwater competence is assessed separately.


## D06 — Dry escape rehearsal and brace preparation

Dry rehearsal reduces the number of unfamiliar actions during a stressful exercise. Sit in the assigned trainer seat with the approved ensemble. Identify the restraint release, stable hand references, primary exit and alternative. Have the instructor demonstrate how the actual exit opens. Practise maintaining orientation while reaching the necessary controls, without pulling another passenger or using a loose object as a handhold. Secure loose straps that could snag.

On a warned emergency, follow crew instructions for brace and preparation. The exact brace posture depends on seating and restraint; use the aircraft briefing rather than a generic pose from a different aircraft. Prepare the breathing system at its prescribed stage. Keep the restraint secured through the phase when it prevents impact injury and loss of orientation. An urge to release immediately is understandable but can leave a person displaced from their known exit.

The training progression is briefing, demonstration, coached dry practice, independent dry demonstration and only then the approved water progression. Before each stage, the instructor checks readiness and confirms stop signals. A learner who cannot reliably identify the release in dry conditions needs more practice before immersion. Speed is not the first success measure; correct control, orientation and response are.

Worked case: an instructor asks a learner to identify an alternative exit and the learner reaches for a window that is not an emergency exit. Correct the understanding in the dry trainer. Do not use submersion to reveal a basic knowledge gap. Practice a second seat position so the learner demonstrates understanding rather than a memorized reach.


## D07 — Controlled helicopter underwater escape

Only a purpose-designed facility with competent staff, a validated exercise plan and immediate rescue capability conducts underwater escape. This written lesson is preparation, not permission to practise in a private pool or submerged vehicle. The instructor controls immersion, rotation, emergency stop and recovery. Learners are briefed on the exact progression and can stop at any stage. No surprise inversion, competitive breath-holding or forced participation is acceptable.

During the approved escape sequence, maintain a known reference and follow the taught timing for restraint and exit operation. Water, reduced visibility and inversion can make normal visual assumptions unreliable. Follow the actual exit path and equipment instructions rather than swim toward a guessed light source. Avoid premature release that loses orientation or premature inflation inside the cabin. Clear the opening without obstructing other occupants and follow the trained route to the surface and survival area.

If disoriented or unable to complete the sequence, use the stop/distress signal taught by the center; trained rescuers intervene. A rescue intervention is valuable evidence of a skill gap, not a shameful event to hide. Record what happened, correct the cause and use a fresh appropriate reassessment only after readiness review. An observer cannot mark an unseen buckle release as correct simply because the learner eventually surfaced.

Worked case: a learner performs an upright escape but freezes during an inverted exercise. Recover safely, check wellbeing, review the precise difficulty and determine the suitable retraining progression. Do not call the inverted exercise passed on the strength of the upright attempt. Each required condition has its own observation record.


## D08 — Cold-water response and conserving capability

Sudden cold-water immersion can disrupt breathing and movement before prolonged cooling becomes the main problem. Use flotation to support the airway, concentrate on regaining controlled breathing and avoid immediately exhausting yourself in a long swim. Keep the mouth and nose clear of spray as the equipment allows. An immersion suit and lifejacket improve protection but do not eliminate cold stress or guarantee a rescue time.

Once stable, assess the realistic safe refuge: a nearby raft under instruction is different from a distant platform against current. Stay with effective flotation and the group where safe. Reduce unnecessary movement and heat loss using the taught supported posture or group arrangement; never adopt a posture that submerges the airway. A person without effective flotation may need movement to remain afloat. Do not impose one posture regardless of the person's condition or equipment.

Use the whistle, light or other approved signaling aids correctly. Conserve energy, keep awareness of aircraft/raft hazards and report a deteriorating companion. Do not tether people together with an improvised arrangement that can entangle or pull them under. In a raft, reduce wind and water exposure, protect casualties and follow the person coordinating survival. Do not drink seawater. Inventory supplies and use them under the survival plan.

Worked case: two survivors want to swim toward a structure that appears close but is moving away in current. Their raft is nearby and the rescue team has their location. Stabilizing flotation, boarding the raft when directed and signaling is safer than an unsupported endurance swim. Practice explaining the decision from current conditions, not an assumed swimming ability.


## D10 — Liferafts, water entry and rescue interfaces

Controlled evacuation into survival craft is preferable to unnecessary exposure in the sea. Water entry is a last-resort action governed by the emergency and equipment instructions. Check the landing area and hazards, prepare the ensemble correctly and use the trained entry method and permitted height. Never jump into unknown water or perform a high entry because a photograph shows another facility doing so. A practice entry needs its own engineered environment, rescue cover and authorization.

At a liferaft, follow the instructed approach and boarding method. Avoid lines, canopy components and other entanglement points. Use the designed boarding aid and coordinated assistance. Once inside, move to balance the load and make space for others. Do not stand up unnecessarily or use a sharp object carelessly near an inflatable chamber. The assigned person manages lines, sea anchor and equipment in accordance with the raft instructions; do not cut a line merely because it is inconvenient.

For rescue, follow the vessel or helicopter crew's signals. A winch strop or rescue device must be applied as instructed; never improvise a connection to clothing or grab a dangling aircraft component. Control loose articles and await the crew's approach instructions. In training, use a non-lifting ground demonstration unless a qualified facility separately authorizes a rated lifting exercise with its own safeguards.

Worked case: a tired survivor cannot climb the raft ladder alone. Other occupants use the demonstrated coordinated assistance while keeping the raft stable and the person's airway clear. Pulling only on a loose lifejacket can displace it. Practise on the designated trainer and record the actual assistance used.


## D14 — Integrated survival and keeping the qualification honest

Emergency performance is a sequence of decisions, not a set of disconnected tricks. Travel preparation makes equipment usable; briefing establishes orientation; controlled escape gets a person clear; flotation protects the airway; muster and signaling connect the person with rescue; first aid supports survival until definitive care. A failure early in this chain may not be repaired by enthusiasm later.

Use a structured final scenario. Receive a fictional destination and equipment record, identify a mismatch, correct it through the responsible person, respond to a warned emergency, demonstrate the approved trainer sequence, join the survival group and complete a casualty handover. The assessor records each learner independently. Watching a successful team does not establish the performance of a person who never operated the equipment.

After an incident or drill, account for people, isolate defective equipment and document observations without blame or invented certainty. Preserve the first attempt when reassessment is required. Identify the exact missing skill, provide instruction, then observe a changed exercise under safe conditions. A written pass cannot cancel an unresolved critical practical failure. Changes in aircraft, breathing system, health, employer requirements or elapsed time can require additional training before travel.

Worked case: an employee passes all written questions but has not completed the underwater portion because of a medical restriction. Their record states classroom completion and practical outstanding. It must not say full initial-survival qualification. The materials package can be complete while a particular learner's qualification remains incomplete; these are different facts.

The VNDRLY record names the assessed equipment variant, conditions, dates, evaluators and limitations. It never issues OPITO approval or guarantees acceptance by a carrier. An employer checks actual destination and operator requirements before dispatch. Review lessons periodically, rehearse through authorized drills and report a readiness gap before it becomes an emergency.
