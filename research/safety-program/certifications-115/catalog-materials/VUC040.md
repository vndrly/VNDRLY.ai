# VNDRLY/U Asset Integrity — Risk-Based Inspection

VUC040 · Original available-source learning materials ·2026-11-18

## Purpose and boundary

Risk-based inspection(RBI) connects credible deterioration, failure likelihood, consequences and inspection/mitigation decisions. It is not a calculator for automatically extending intervals or making an unfavorable condition acceptable. This course prepares inspectors, maintenance/integrity personnel and engineers to contribute to a defensible RBI program, challenge weak evidence and implement its approved actions. Prerequisites are basic equipment/damage-mechanism literacy, arithmetic and ability to read an inspection record. It does not confer API580 certification or authority to approve an operating asset.

API's public program identifies RP580 fourth edition2023 as its examination basis.[S01] This is an original available-source VNDRLY learning program, not verified clause-by-clause equivalence. Supplied matrices, scores, probabilities, costs and limits are synthetic training inputs. They are not API581 calculations, regulatory limits or a site's tolerable-risk criteria. Live decisions require the actual controlled method, applicable requirements and authorized competent team.

## Q01 — Risk is a decision model, not a color

Risk combines the likelihood of a defined event with its consequences. Define the event before assigning a score: a small leak, a large rupture and failure of a protective device can have different likelihoods and outcomes. A single asset color may summarize several scenarios, but it must not hide how that summary was produced.

Likelihood is not severity, and uncertainty is not the same as low likelihood. A rare catastrophic event can remain important even when its expected financial loss is modest. Preserve safety, environmental and business dimensions rather than turning every consequence into dollars by default. An ordinal matrix score is a ranking convention; multiplying two category numbers does not create a measured probability.

**Worked example.** An exercise assigns likelihood category2 and consequence5, giving a ranking score10 under its supplied rule. That score is neither10% failure probability nor$10 loss. **Changed case:** another scenario scores5×2=10. Equal products do not establish equivalent hazard, uncertainty or required controls.

## Q02 — Define scope and exclusions

State the equipment population, boundaries, failure modes, operating period and decisions the study will support. Include connected systems and common causes where they affect the event. Exclusions need reasons and another responsible program where appropriate. A scope boundary should not accidentally drop a small-bore connection or a temporary bypass that can release the same hazardous inventory.

Distinguish pressure-boundary integrity from functional reliability of trips, alarms or relief systems. They may interact but need suitable methods. RBI does not replace process hazard analysis, mechanical integrity, fitness-for-service assessment or emergency planning. It supplies part of the evidence and priorities within that system.

**Worked example.** A vessel study excludes its connected drain because the register lists only large nozzles. The drain is still a pressure-boundary release path and needs explicit review. **Changed case:** electrical control failure is excluded from the pressure-boundary model. Assign it to the appropriate functional-safety/reliability process rather than implying it is harmless.

## Q03 — Assemble a competent team

Process, operations, inspection, materials/corrosion, maintenance and consequence/risk expertise contribute different evidence. Complexity and hazard determine needed competence. One person may hold several roles if genuinely competent, but a spreadsheet operator cannot substitute for missing technical knowledge. HSE's RBI guidance emphasizes team competence and management of the process.[S02]

Document roles, assumptions, disagreements, reviewers and decision authority. Operations should explain actual startups, upsets and temporary practices. Inspection explains what methods and coverage established. Materials expertise connects exposure to credible damage. Management owns resources and authorized risk decisions. Preserve dissent that could change a result; do not vote away an unresolved technical concern merely to finish a meeting.

**Worked example.** The team has design temperatures but no operator who can explain frequent cold washes. Obtain actual operating history before excluding wet/shutdown mechanisms. **Changed case:** a contractor provides a complete model but cannot explain its inputs. Independent competent review is still required.

## Q04 — Data quality and traceability

Required data can include identity, geometry, materials, design basis, service history, inspections, repairs, process conditions, inventories and safeguards. Record origin, date, units, confidence and applicability. A default may be useful for a documented conservative screen, but must not masquerade as a verified fact. Missing data can increase uncertainty and demand investigation.

Check links between tags, drawings, measurements and model records. A precise value attached to the wrong component is worse than an honestly unknown value. Distinguish a measured minimum from a nominal specification and an inspected area from an assumed representative area. Preserve revisions and transformations so a reviewer can reproduce the result.

**Worked example.** A model imports8 mm nominal wall where the latest mapped minimum is5 mm. Correct the input and reassess; the resulting lower risk was a data error, not an improvement. **Changed case:** two reports disagree. Reconcile methods, locations and dates instead of selecting the larger value.

## Q05 — Damage mechanisms and credible locations

Identify deterioration from material, environment, stress and time across normal operation, startup, shutdown, upset and idle periods. A mechanism list without locations or conditions cannot direct inspection. General loss, local pitting, cracking and property degradation need different models and detection strategies. A thickness trend alone cannot represent every mechanism.

Review industry incidents and similar-service experience as well as the site's history. A plant with no recorded failures may still have unrecognized vulnerabilities. CSB recommendations illustrate the importance of mechanism-specific inspection coverage and prevention.[S05] Do not exclude a mechanism because the existing method was incapable of detecting it.

**Worked example.** A dry-gas circuit periodically condenses liquid in a low point. That region needs wet-service review even if the inlet is dry. **Changed case:** the low point is drained now. Prevention of future retention does not establish the condition left by previous exposure.

## Q06 — Grouping and corrosion circuits

Grouping can make a study manageable when components share relevant material, service and damage behavior. The grouping basis must be explicit and revisited when components differ. Different alloys, temperatures, phases, injection points, deadlegs and replacement histories can require separate treatment. A common line number does not guarantee uniform deterioration.

Representative inspection requires a reason why sampled locations describe the group. Favorable accessible readings may systematically miss the susceptible parts. Define component and spatial coverage separately. If new evidence contradicts a grouping assumption, split or revise the group and assess similar components; do not force the outlier into an average.

**Worked example.** Four fittings have low loss but one intervening pipe has much higher loss and unknown composition. The fitting sample is not a sound basis for every pipe segment. **Changed case:** all components have one reading. That is component contact, not necessarily adequate coverage of localized damage.

## Q07 — Probability, frequency and time horizon

A probability is dimensionless and tied to an event and time period. A frequency has units such as events/year. They are related only through a defined model. For a constant-rate Poisson illustration, probability of at least one event over timeT is1−exp(−λT). This teaching model is not an RBI equipment-failure model and does not capture accelerating damage by itself.

Specify whether the analysis estimates leak frequency, rupture probability, conditional failure after an initiating event or another quantity. Do not multiply quantities with incompatible time bases. Historical counts need exposure denominators and consideration of reporting, population and service changes. Zero observed events do not prove zero probability.

**Worked example.** At a synthetic constant rate0.02/year, a one-year probability is about1.98%, not exactly2% under this model. Over five years it is about9.52%, not a universal prediction of asset failure. **Changed case:** damage accelerates after a feed change. The constant-rate assumption must be reconsidered.

## Q08 — Evaluate likelihood with mechanism-specific evidence

Likelihood estimation may use qualitative judgment, semiquantitative factors or a quantitative model. The method should suit the decision, data and uncertainty. Complexity does not guarantee accuracy. Understand what the model assumes about damage rate, resistance, failure mode, inspection and uncertainty before using its output.

Separate actual condition from a category label. An unresolved crack is not made acceptable by assigning a low frequency. Known deficiencies require the applicable integrity response. Use appropriate engineering assessment for condition acceptance and RBI for the decisions it legitimately supports. Do not adjust inputs simply to move a point below a target line.

**Worked example.** A model gives low likelihood because an inspection flag is marked“effective,” but the inspection was thickness-only for a cracking concern. Correct the effectiveness basis and reassess. **Changed case:** the score rises. That is improved recognition of existing uncertainty, not necessarily newly created physical damage.

## Q09 — Consequence scenarios and release assumptions

Consequences depend on what is released, how much, how fast, its state and hazards, isolation, detection, occupancy and surroundings. Small and large release scenarios can behave differently. The physical model needs valid inventory boundaries and release assumptions; a vessel's own inventory may understate connected feed.

Consider toxic exposure, fire, explosion, environmental impact and business interruption as appropriate. Consequence analysis can require specialized modeling. A learner should identify missing inputs and challenge applicability rather than invent dispersion distances or fatality probabilities. Document weather, occupancy and other assumptions if the selected method uses them.

**Worked example.** A model assumes immediate isolation but the valve is manual and access may be unsafe after release. The credited isolation time is unsupported. **Changed case:** a remote valve is installed. Its function, reliability, detection and response basis still need verification before credit.

## Q10 — Safeguard credit and dependencies

Only credit safeguards according to the method's requirements and actual evidence. Installation is not proof of availability, testing or effectiveness. A detector, alarm and operator response can be linked parts of one chain, not three independent protections. Common power, maintenance, environment or human dependencies can defeat several functions together.

Distinguish reducing likelihood of loss of containment from reducing consequences after release. Inspection may improve knowledge and lead to action; isolation may limit released inventory; neither should receive the other's credit. Avoid counting a safeguard in both likelihood and consequence without a justified method.

**Worked example.** Detection and automatic isolation share the same failed power supply. Treating them as independent overstates protection. **Changed case:** redundant supply is added but no functional test exists. A design improvement is not yet demonstrated performance credit.

## Q11 — Risk matrices and ranking discipline

Define categories, boundaries, aggregation rules and decision actions before ranking. A matrix is a communication and prioritization tool, not a proof of acceptable condition. Ordinal categories do not have equal numerical spacing unless the method establishes it. A one-category change can reflect uncertainty or a boundary effect rather than a large physical change.

Keep scenarios visible beneath the asset summary. A high-consequence scenario should not disappear when averaged with many low-consequence scenarios. Compare like measures and preserve separate safety/environment/business results. Risk-acceptance authority and criteria must be explicit; the analyst does not invent them after seeing the results.

**Worked example.** Under a synthetic product-ranking rule,3×4=12 exceeds2×5=10. That does not prove the first scenario has higher measured expected loss or that the second is acceptable. **Changed case:** management changes the acceptance threshold to fit the schedule. Treat that as a separate governed decision, not an analytical correction.

## Q12 — Inspection effectiveness is conditional

An inspection's value depends on the damage mechanism, morphology, location, method, coverage, sensitivity, timing and response to findings. A report with“no relevant indications” does not eliminate all deterioration. HSE guidance emphasizes method-specific capabilities and limitations.[S03] The evidence should support the particular update made to likelihood or uncertainty.

Detection alone does not repair damage. Risk reduction may arise when findings trigger repair, replacement, operating changes or justified confidence updates. A favorable result from an unsuitable method should not earn credit. Record inaccessible areas and assumptions about representativeness rather than treating them as inspected.

**Worked example.** A few thickness points are credited as a high-effectiveness crack inspection. Withdraw that credit and select a suitable qualified plan. **Changed case:** a suitable crack method covers only half the susceptible region. Capability is improved, but coverage limits remain.

## Q13 — Updating beliefs without overstating certainty

New evidence should change the assessment only to the extent supported by its reliability and relevance. A Bayesian calculation can illustrate this discipline, but real model updating requires a validated method. State prior event definition, detection performance and false-positive/negative assumptions. Never turn a classroom probability into a field acceptance limit.

**Original illustration.** Prior probability of a specified detectable flaw is10%; test detection probability is80%; specificity90%. Probability of a negative is0.10×0.20+0.90×0.90=0.83. Probability of that flaw given a negative is0.02/0.83≈2.41%. It is not zero. The result depends on these synthetic assumptions and covers only that defined flaw/test population.

**Changed case.** If coverage is incomplete, the stated80% detection for a fully examined region cannot automatically be applied to the whole asset. Model the coverage limitation or retain uncertainty. A posterior probability is not permission to ignore a known indication.

## Q14 — Select inspection tasks and intervals

An RBI output should specify equipment/location, mechanism, method, coverage, timing, prerequisites, acceptance/disposition route and responsible owner. “Inspect next outage” is incomplete when the outage date is unknown. The plan must respect applicable requirements, engineering limits and evidence quality; RBI is not an automatic waiver of existing obligations.

Choose timing based on the actual method and decision basis. Inspection performed too late may not allow safe response. Inspection performed with inadequate access may not achieve the credited effectiveness. For covered U.S. PSM processes, OSHA requires documented inspections/tests and an appropriate recognized engineering basis; applicability must be established rather than assumed.[S04]

**Worked example.** The risk model proposes36 months, but a supplied binding project requirement limits the task to24 months. Do not schedule36 without an authorized applicable basis for changing that requirement. **Changed case:** shutdown is delayed beyond24. A new date alone does not resolve the overdue obligation.

## Q15 — Mitigation beyond inspection

When inspection cannot adequately address a risk, consider reducing the damage driver, changing materials, repairing/replacing equipment, changing operation or reducing consequences through appropriate safeguards. The selected action must have a credible link to the scenario and verification of effectiveness. More frequent inspection is not always the best or sufficient response.

Compare options without treating cost as the only criterion. Some obligations and unacceptable conditions are not traded away by a favorable cost-benefit ratio. Identify implementation hazards and management-of-change needs. A replacement alloy may introduce another mechanism; a lower operating temperature may affect process performance or condensation.

**Worked example.** Rapid unpredictable cracking is proposed to be managed by occasional spot thickness checks. That task cannot provide the claimed control. Reconsider the mechanism, suitable condition assessment and stronger mitigation. **Changed case:** equipment is replaced but the damaging process condition remains. Evaluate the new equipment's susceptibility and controls.

## Q16 — Optimization and resource allocation

Optimization chooses among feasible actions subject to safety, legal, technical and resource constraints. It does not mean maximizing interval or minimizing spending. A ranking can help allocate inspection effort, but data quality and consequence severity must remain visible. Inspecting many easy low-risk items is not equivalent to addressing a few critical unresolved assets.

An expected financial-loss illustration multiplies a specified scenario frequency by its consequence cost, with compatible units. It is not a universal monetary value for safety. If scenarios overlap, adding their values can double count. If costs exclude outage duration or environmental response, the comparison is incomplete.

**Worked example.** Synthetic frequency0.01/year and consequence$2,000,000 gives$20,000/year expected loss under that simplified model. A$5,000 inspection does not automatically save$20,000; its effect and follow-up actions must be established. **Changed case:** two scenario costs represent the same incident pathway. Avoid adding them as independent events.

## Q17 — Change, excursions and reassessment triggers

Reassess when inputs, mechanisms, condition or safeguards change. Triggers can include new inspection findings, feed changes, operating-window excursions, repairs, material substitutions, inventory/occupancy changes, new failure experience or model corrections. Periodic review also remains necessary under the applicable program. A calendar review date does not justify waiting after a significant new finding.

Link RBI to management of change and actual operating data. Preserve event timing, uncertainty and affected scope. A normal reading after an excursion does not establish that prior exposure caused no damage. The reassessment must produce an action or documented unchanged conclusion with reasons, not just a refreshed date.

**Worked example.** A unit changes to a more corrosive feed six months after RBI approval. Reopen affected mechanisms and tasks now rather than waiting for annual review. **Changed case:** the feed returns to its old composition. Existing damage and the excursion record still require assessment.

## Q18 — Implementation, findings and overdue work

An approved plan must become executable work with owners, resources, access and dates. Track whether the specified method and coverage were actually achieved. If inspection finds unexpected damage, route it through the appropriate integrity response and update the study. A closed work order is not proof that its intended examination occurred.

Deferral needs a governed technical/risk decision under applicable requirements, with clear duration and interim controls. It is not a clerical extension. An overdue task with no assessment remains overdue. Record unsuccessful access, incomplete coverage and failed safeguards; those are inputs to decisions, not reasons to mark the task complete.

**Worked example.** A work order closes after crews reach the asset but insulation cannot be removed. The credited examination is incomplete. Correct the status and obtain the required follow-up/disposition. **Changed case:** a remote screening method is substituted; verify whether it satisfies the original decision need before claiming equivalent completion.

## Q19 — Audit, validation and performance measures

Audit both the technical model and the management process. Check input traceability, mechanism completeness, method applicability, consequence assumptions, safeguard credit, action implementation and change triggers. Reproducing a spreadsheet number verifies arithmetic, not the model's validity. Benchmarking against outcomes and independent competent review can reveal systematic errors.

Measure meaningful performance: unresolved high-priority actions, overdue required tasks, coverage achieved, data-quality improvements and response to unexpected damage. A count of inspections alone can reward easy work while significant risk remains. Avoid changing score definitions without preserving comparability.

**Worked example.** A program reports100% completion because every work order was closed, but three critical regions were inaccessible. Audit the actual examination evidence and correct the metric. **Changed case:** the model is updated and scores fall without physical changes; determine whether improved evidence justifies the update or assumptions were weakened.

## Q20 — Communicate a defensible decision

The final record should state scope, team, method/version, data sources, assumptions, uncertainties, scenarios, results, criteria, actions, authority and reassessment triggers. Give management an understandable summary without hiding technical limitations. Link every important conclusion to evidence and preserve versions so later reviewers can reconstruct the decision.

Separate proposed, approved, scheduled, executed and verified actions. A mitigation ordered but not installed is not an implemented safeguard. An examination performed but not interpreted is not a resolved condition. The responsible authority needs to know what remains uncertain and what decision depends on it.

**Worked example.** A proposed isolation upgrade lowers modeled consequence only after its performance is assumed. Show both current and proposed states; do not report the proposed risk as today's risk. **Changed case:** installation finishes but testing is pending. Retain the distinction until the required functional evidence exists.

# VUC040 applied decision workbook

All organizations, assets, quantities, risk criteria, intervals and costs below are synthetic. They are supplied to make exercises reproducible, not asserted API limits or an approved operating method. Attempt each task before its worked response. Retain calculations, assumptions and revisions. No live equipment operation is involved.

## W01 — Establish the study boundary

**Record.** System S40 contains vessel V1, its inlet line L1, outlet L2 and drain D1. Drawing revision 3 shows D1 blanked. Operator log on 12 November records D1 reopened to a recovery header. The equipment list omits D1. A manager proposes excluding the recovery header because another department owns it. The study covers loss of containment over the next 12 months; startup and shutdown occur twice annually.

**Task.** Write a study boundary, identify three evidence gaps and assign review responsibilities. Distinguish ownership from physical influence.

**Worked response.** Include V1/L1/L2/D1 and evaluate the connected recovery header's effect on inventory, backflow and isolation; the exact physical boundary requires verified current configuration. Review startup/shutdown scenarios within the stated period. Gaps: drawing versus log configuration, missing drain identification/condition, and header pressure/isolation/service information. Operations verifies configuration and modes, inspection/materials personnel establish condition and credible deterioration, and process engineering establishes connectivity/consequences. The authorized study owner approves scope and unresolved interfaces. Department ownership alone cannot establish a safe exclusion.

**Changed case.** A current drawing confirms D1 blanked, but no field verification exists. This improves documentary consistency, not proof of installed isolation. Record confidence and obtain appropriate verification without directing an unsafe live-line inspection.

**Rubric, 10 points.** Scope/modes 2; physical interfaces 2; three gaps 3; competent owners 2; honest limitation 1. Unsupported exclusion of connected inventory is a critical error.

## W02 — Data quality and population selection

**Record.** Four carbon-steel circuits share a nominal service label. A: dry continuous service, 100 measured points, minimum 7.1 mm. B: intermittent wet service, 15 points, minimum 5.8 mm. C: wet dead leg, no measurements; nominal drawing 8 mm. D: recently replaced dry line, 60 points, minimum 8.0 mm. All figures are records, not acceptance limits. The draft uses the average of A/B/D minima for C and says its inspection coverage is 100% because every accessible location was measured.

**Task.** Calculate that proposed average, explain why it does not establish C's condition, and write a better data-quality statement.

**Worked response.** (7.1+5.8+8.0)/3 = 6.967 mm. This average of different populations' minima cannot establish an unmeasured wet dead leg. A/D dry service and D's age differ; C's stagnation and wetting matter. Nominal 8 mm is not current thickness. State C unmeasured, identify access and mechanism uncertainty, and develop a technically justified examination/condition response. The denominator must be the intended susceptible region, not only accessible locations. Actual failure margin cannot be calculated without a valid required thickness and appropriate damage evaluation.

**Changed case.** C receives one reading of 7.0 mm at the root, but its stagnant end is inaccessible. Do not call the whole circuit inspected or use one favorable point to dismiss localized damage.

**Rubric.** Arithmetic 2; population reasoning 3; nominal/current distinction 2; coverage statement 2; limit of conclusion 1. Inventing C's condition is critical.

## W03 — Time basis, likelihood and ranking

**Record.** A purely illustrative constant-rate event model has rate 0.04 events/year and probability of at least one event over T years of 1−exp(−0.04T). A separate ordinal matrix assigns likelihood categories 1–5 and severity categories 1–5; its ranking is their product. Matrix case A is 2×5; B is 4×3. Neither category is a frequency. The training action rule is: product at least 12 goes to priority review; severity 5 also requires explicit major-consequence review regardless of product. These are classroom rules only.

**Task.** Compute one- and three-year event probabilities, rank A/B and state which reviews apply.

**Worked response.** One-year probability is 0.03921, or 3.921%; three-year is 0.11308, or 11.308%. The one-year frequency and probability are not identical, and linear multiplication of probability by three is not the given model. A scores 10 and needs major-consequence review. B scores 12 and needs priority review. B's product does not prove its physical risk is 20% greater than A. A lower product cannot bypass the severity rule.

**Changed case.** Rate rises to 0.08 after a process change: one year 7.688%, three years 21.337%. Old results no longer represent the supplied rate; actual rate change requires evidence, not an arbitrary multiplier.

**Rubric.** Four original numerical results 4; review rules 2; units/time 2; ordinal/model limits 2. Treating score as measured probability is a critical interpretation error.

## W04 — Release inventory and safeguard evidence

**Record.** V1 contains 2,000 kg of hazardous fluid. Connected feed can continue at 40 kg/min. The study assumes an isolation response after 5 minutes, producing an inventory estimate of initial inventory plus feed until isolation. No credit for evaporation, drainage or other losses is included. Current evidence shows only a manual valve, with access potentially obstructed during the event. A proposed remote valve is not installed. An alternative sensitivity uses 20 minutes before isolation.

**Task.** Compute both release-inventory inputs, identify what neither calculation predicts and correct the current/proposed comparison.

**Worked response.** Five-minute assumption: 2,200 kg; twenty-minute assumption: 2,800 kg. Difference 600 kg, or 27.27% of the smaller estimate. These are scenario inventory inputs, not dispersion, ignition, fatality or environmental consequence calculations. The manual five-minute response lacks demonstrated suitability/reliability under event conditions; retain uncertainty and assess credible alternatives with competent process expertise. A proposed remote valve belongs in a proposed future scenario, not current risk credit. Installation alone will not establish performance without required testing and management controls.

**Changed case.** Both automatic trip and alarm/operator response depend on the same power supply. Do not multiply independent-failure probabilities without addressing that shared failure and dependencies.

**Rubric.** Inventory 3; percentage 1; limitation 2; current/proposed 2; dependency reasoning 2. Giving credit to uninstalled protection is critical.

## W05 — A negative examination is evidence, not immunity

**Record.** For a specified flaw population only, assumed prior flaw prevalence is 0.10, detection probability is 0.80, and specificity is 0.90. Assume a representative examined population and the stated performance applies. Bayes calculation for a negative result: numerator p×(1−POD); denominator that numerator plus (1−p)×specificity. A real job examined only 60 of 100 required weld locations, selected for access rather than randomly.

**Task.** Calculate the illustrative posterior flaw probability after a negative result, coverage percentage, and explain why the calculated posterior cannot simply be assigned to the whole job.

**Worked response.** Numerator 0.02, denominator 0.83; posterior 0.024096 = 2.410%. Examined-location coverage is 60%. Forty locations remain unexamined and access selection may correlate with different damage exposure. The demonstration assumes applicable performance for a defined flaw and population; it is not an empirical job-specific risk estimate, nor does it account for subsequent damage growth. Inspection can improve knowledge without removing the defect or mechanism. Preserve uncovered regions and method limitations in the plan.

**Changed case.** With POD 0.50 and the same prior/specificity, posterior is 0.05/0.86 = 5.814%. This is a sensitivity illustration, not permission to manufacture a favorable performance number.

**Rubric.** Bayes workings 3; coverage 1; sampling/method limitations 3; no physical repair claim 2; changed result 1. Assigning examined-population certainty to uncovered regions is critical.

## W06 — Turn a study into executable tasks

**Record.** Study recommends examination in 30 months. A supplied binding site requirement limits this task to 24 months from the last valid examination. Last valid examination: 1 January 2026. A planned outage is 1 July 2028. The job plan requires examination of 20 identified susceptible zones by a method competent for the expected localized damage; eight zones need insulation removal. Access resources are currently funded for only five of those eight. A generic work order says “inspect vessel.”

**Task.** Establish the latest date under the supplied rule, the scheduling conflict, and a usable task specification.

**Worked response.** Latest date is 1 January 2028; the July outage is six months later. RBI does not itself waive the binding rule. Arrange compliant examination/authorized resolution under applicable requirements; do not silently move the deadline. Specify asset, zones, mechanism and examination objective, qualified method/procedure/personnel, preparation/access, acceptance/disposition basis, records, due date, owner, and how incomplete coverage or unexpected indications are escalated. Three required insulation-access zones lack resources; record that open issue instead of planning 100% completion.

**Changed case.** All insulation resources are approved, but the selected method cannot detect the relevant crack orientation. Resources resolve access, not method suitability. Obtain qualified technical method review before crediting effectiveness.

**Rubric.** Date/conflict 2; task specificity 4; access gap 2; method suitability 2. A clerical deadline extension or knowingly unsuitable method is critical.

## W07 — Compare actions without buying a lower score

**Record.** Mutually exclusive hypothetical annual events A and B have frequencies 0.01/year and 0.02/year and financial losses $2 million and $100,000 per event. Use frequency×loss only for this annual expected-loss comparison. The training budget is $12,000. Action X costs $10,000 and, if installed and verified, is assumed to reduce A's frequency to 0.004/year. Action Y costs $6,000 and only collects better information; no physical frequency reduction has been demonstrated. Action Z costs $5,000 and, if verified, reduces B loss to $40,000/event. A separate major-harm review remains mandatory for A; the financial comparison cannot waive it.

**Task.** Calculate baseline, X-only and Z-only expected financial loss. Identify feasible action combinations and distinguish modeled benefit from approved selection.

**Worked response.** Baseline A $20,000/year plus B $2,000/year = $22,000/year. X-only totals $8,000+$2,000=$10,000/year, an assumed $12,000/year reduction. Z-only totals $20,000+$800=$20,800/year, an assumed $1,200/year reduction. X+Z costs $15,000, over budget. Y+Z costs $11,000 and is feasible by budget, but Y cannot be assigned an unsupported physical reduction. X alone is within budget; selecting it requires validated assumptions, implementation and safety/environmental/mandatory reviews, not just payback. Mutually exclusive scenario treatment prevents inappropriate double counting in this supplied example; real event structures require explicit analysis.

**Changed case.** X's quoted cost excludes $4,000 installation. Its $14,000 actual total exceeds the budget. Update the decision record; do not reduce the declared scope of X to make it appear affordable.

**Rubric.** Arithmetic 4; feasibility 2; evidence/credit 2; nonfinancial criteria and decision authority 2. Using dollars to waive major-harm requirements is critical.

## W08 — Reassessment, performance and honest closure

**Record.** Ten scheduled actions are labeled closed. Seven have examination reports demonstrating the planned method/coverage; two were access visits without examination; one has data awaiting interpretation. A process excursion exceeded the study's temperature envelope. An equipment list was updated but the damage review was not. An executive slide says “100% risk actions complete; no reassessment needed.”

**Task.** Produce a corrected completion metric and a three-step response with owners.

**Worked response.** Seven of ten actions have the specified execution evidence: 70%, subject to validating their interpretation/disposition. Two access-only jobs and one uninterpreted result must not be treated as verified technical closure. Operations documents excursion conditions/duration and immediate operating response; materials/inspection/process specialists assess mechanism and consequence implications; the authorized study owner governs reassessment, interim controls, actions and deadlines. Updating an inventory field does not update the technical basis. Avoid asserting that the excursion necessarily caused damage; investigate its significance.

**Changed case.** The pending result is interpreted and accepted with the required authority; eight of ten now meet closure evidence, or 80%. The excursion reassessment and remaining access tasks are still separate open items.

**Rubric.** Metrics 2; status distinctions 2; technical reassessment 3; owners/actions 2; no unsupported conclusion 1. Concealing overdue/unexamined work is critical.

## I40 — Integrated RBI review board exercise

**Dossier.** Vessel A has wet intermittent service and connected feed. Its selected accessible thickness readings are stable, but its low-point regions are unexamined. Its manual isolation time is unverified. Line B has dry steady service, a traceable examination of all study-designated regions, and no recorded change since the study. Its required next task is due in two months, but scheduling shows four months. Vessel C changed feed composition last week; its old study excluded environmental cracking and has not been reviewed. A proposed replacement alloy has been ordered, not installed. The supplied administrative matrix gives A score 10/severity 5, B score 6/severity 3, C score 8/severity 4. Rule: severity 5 requires major-consequence review; these scores are illustrative only.

**Assignment.** Produce a one-page management decision plus evidence register and action log. For each asset identify reliable facts, uncertainty, affected scenario, needed competent review, owner, due constraint, and current versus proposed controls. Do not calculate actual safe life or authorize operation from this dossier.

**Instructor worked disposition.** A needs explicit major-consequence review under the supplied rule, susceptible-region evidence and defensible isolation/consequence assumptions. Stable accessible readings do not clear its low points. B's technical evidence is better, but its task schedule conflicts with its due date by two months; correct scheduling or use an actually authorized compliant route, not a score-based waiver. C needs change-triggered mechanism review; retain current material and service in its current-state assessment. The ordered replacement is not a current safeguard. Priority cannot be justified solely by sorting the three products; mandatory due dates, major harm, changed conditions and uncertainty all matter. Record no unsupported final operating approval.

**Changed dossier.** A's low points are examined with a suitable method and no relevant indications; its isolation uncertainty remains. B is rescheduled before its due date, but execution has not occurred. C's new material is installed, but material verification and startup review remain open. Update only the facts that changed; distinguish scheduled, installed, examined, interpreted and verified states.

**Scoring, 30 points.** Asset-specific facts/uncertainties 6; scenario/mechanism reasoning 6; feasible actions/owners/deadlines 6; current/proposed/status accuracy 6; defensible management summary/source traceability 6. Pass 24/30, at least 3/6 each dimension, and no unresolved critical error. Critical errors include inventing safe operation, assigning credit to unverified future controls, hiding due-date conflicts, or declaring negative limited coverage proof of an undamaged asset. # VUC040 delivery, practice and credential guide

## Outcomes, audience and preparation

On completion, a learner can define a study and its interfaces; distinguish risk ranking, frequency and probability; challenge input quality and mechanism omissions; explain consequence/safeguard assumptions; design a traceable inspection action; recognize binding constraints and reassessment triggers; and defend an evidence-based management decision. This is original VNDRLY learning assessment, not API certification, a validated API 581 model, fitness-for-service authority or permission to extend live inspection intervals.

Learners need basic process-equipment and damage-mechanism knowledge and arithmetic. Before instruction ask them to distinguish nominal from measured thickness, identify a wet dead leg, calculate a percentage and name the owner of an operating decision. Diagnose gaps and supply prerequisite teaching; do not treat failure as misconduct or silently lower the final standard. Instructors need relevant integrity/RBI experience, ability to explain uncertainty and quantitative assumptions, and familiarity with the actual jurisdiction when discussing field examples. A qualified specialist must handle technical issues beyond an instructor's competence.

Supply coursebook, workbook, calculator with exp function, blank records, projection or accessible equivalent and the private assessment pack to authorized assessors only. Offline printed materials can deliver the entire declared course; paid-standard access and live software are not required. Do not ask learners to inspect operating equipment, enter confined spaces, remove insulation or manipulate isolation devices for this course.

## Facilitation and scoring

First ask “what do you actually know?” before accepting a recommendation. Separate fact, assumption, inference and authorized decision on the board. Have learners explicitly label units and time basis. Compare safe alternative answers rather than require the instructor's wording. A learner may correctly refuse a numerical answer when the necessary model or evidence is absent; reward the supported limitation and next action.

Every station uses the identified supplied chapter/workbook record and produces a signed individual response. Score five dimensions 0–2 each: fact extraction, reasoning/calculation, uncertainty/limits, appropriate action/authority, and auditable communication. Two means correct and justified, one means safe but incomplete, zero means absent/incorrect/unsafe. Pass each at 8/10 with no zero dimension and no unresolved critical error. “Critical” includes invented evidence, unsupported live operating authorization, concealing overdue work or treating future safeguards as current. Record the specific error and remediation; the label is not a punishment substitute for instruction.
