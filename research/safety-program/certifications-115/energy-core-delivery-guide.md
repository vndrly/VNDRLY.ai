# Energy Safety Core — learner and delivery guide

Revision: 2026-11-17. Program: G99. Scope: onshore energy-industry safety orientation.

## Who this program is for

This program prepares new and returning field workers to recognize common wellsite hazards, communicate a concern, check the boundaries of their assignment, and choose a safe response when conditions change. Office personnel who arrange field work can use the same instruction to understand why permits, site coordination, competent people, and emergency arrangements matter.

There is no prior safety-course prerequisite. Learners must be able to understand the instruction and questions in the delivery language. A supervisor must arrange understandable instruction when language, literacy, hearing, vision, or another access need prevents comprehension. Time spent clicking through a language the learner does not understand is not training completion.

The program teaches general decisions before exposure. It does not train someone to enter a hazardous space, conduct a rescue, operate lifting equipment, select respiratory protection, or perform electrical work merely because those hazards appear in the lessons. Those duties need the task-specific training and authorization described in the relevant lesson. The credential awarded is VNDRLY Energy Safety Core; the application must not label it a Veriforce, PEC, SafeLandUSA, or SafeGulf card.

## Learning sequence

Work through these ten chapters in order. For each chapter, read the explanation, work the scenarios before reading their solutions, then answer its learning checks. Use the explanation after an incorrect answer to identify the decision that went wrong. Preserve the original response and the acknowledged correction as separate records.

1. Worker responsibilities, speaking up, and reliable communication: turn an uncertain handoff into a specific question, use an actionable stop-work message, and distinguish observation from accusation.
2. Hazard recognition, job analysis, and critical control verification: describe who can encounter a hazard, prefer stronger controls, and reopen the job analysis after a change.
3. Permits, simultaneous operations, and hazardous energy boundaries: recognize permit limits, interacting work, stored energy, and the difference between affected and authorized roles.
4. Fatal-risk recognition: identify entry, ignition, fall, suspended-load, and excavation exposures; stop short of specialist work and improvised rescue.
5. Chemical information, H2S, atmosphere alarms, and respiratory boundaries: find usable chemical information, respond to alarms without relying on odor, and identify when respiratory or gas-testing expertise is required.
6. Occupational health, exposure prevention, and worker hygiene: recognize acute and delayed harm, control exposure at its source, and report symptoms or suspected exposure promptly.
7. Journey management, driving, and mobile-equipment interfaces: plan for fatigue and route changes, separate pedestrians from vehicles, and stop movement when communication fails.
8. Tools, guarding, PPE, and walking-working surfaces: identify defects, prevent contact with moving parts, select suitable protection through the work plan, and keep access routes usable.
9. Emergency action, first aid, fire, and spill boundaries: report an emergency, withdraw using the site plan, account for people, and leave specialist response to trained responders.
10. Organizational safety, personal readiness, and integrated jobsite decisions: combine the earlier decisions in a changing job, communicate limits, and verify correction before an authorized restart.

The ten course chapters contain the full instruction, worked cases, and explained learning checks. This guide supplements those lessons; it is not a replacement for them.

## How to study a field decision

For each situation, answer four questions before choosing an action: What do I actually know? Who could be exposed? What control or authorization is missing? What can I do from a safe position? This separates recognition from intervention. Seeing a coworker collapse beside a tank establishes an emergency, not permission to enter. Your useful actions can be raising the alarm, keeping others out, giving responders the location, and reporting the last known position without becoming another casualty.

Do not convert missing information into an assumed safe condition. An unreadable label does not mean a container is harmless. An expired permit does not become current because the crew started yesterday. A supervisor's absence does not transfer that person's authority to whoever is nearest. State the missing fact and identify who can resolve it.

## Integrated learner exercise: a changed delivery

The following is a fictional tabletop exercise. No live plant, vehicle movement, gas release, energized equipment, or emergency alarm is used.

You arrive at a land-based production site to deliver a replacement pump. Your induction identifies the north pedestrian entrance and Muster A outside that entrance. The shift coordinator controls deliveries. The driver is authorized to drive the truck, but you are not an equipment operator or a gas tester. The unloading plan calls for a designated operator, a separated landing area, and a pre-lift briefing. You notice a hose crossing the marked pedestrian route. A mechanic says that the isolation is ready, but the equipment tag in the work pack does not match the tag on the skid.

**First decision.** Write a radio message identifying the location, observation, people exposed, and immediate safe action. Then identify two separate authorizations that a delivery confirmation does not provide.

**Worked answer.** A useful message is: “Coordinator, this is the pump delivery at the north skid. A hose blocks the pedestrian route and the work-pack equipment tag differs from the skid tag. We are holding outside the unloading area. Please confirm a safe route and have the authorized person resolve the equipment identity before unloading or maintenance starts.” A delivery confirmation does not authorize the lift and does not establish isolation. The driver should remain in the safe waiting arrangement identified by the site, not drive around the barrier to make room.

**Why other responses fail.** Moving the hose without knowing its contents or operating condition could create another hazard. Accepting the mechanic's reassurance without resolving the tag mismatch can direct work to the wrong equipment. Checking isolation personally would exceed the learner's assigned role. Taking a photograph is useful only if allowed and possible from the safe position; documenting the condition does not control it.

**Change one.** Another contractor requests hot work beside the proposed landing area. A foreman points to yesterday's permit and says the job will take only two minutes. Decide what must happen before work proceeds.

**Worked answer.** Keep the conflicting activities paused and contact the controlling coordinator and permit issuer. They need to reassess the changed location, ignition sources, materials, vehicle movement, and overlapping work. A prior permit and a short task duration do not establish today's controls. Your role is to identify the conflict and remain outside the affected area, not approve hot work or prescribe the gas-test result.

**Change two.** A site alarm sounds. A worker suggests finishing the unload because the detector sometimes alarms. Describe your immediate sequence and the information you give at accountability.

**Worked answer.** Follow the site's alarm and evacuation instructions, alert others without moving into danger, and use the designated safe route and assembly arrangement. Do not stop to diagnose the detector or retrieve belongings. At accountability identify yourself, the people last seen with you, their last known location, and any route obstruction. Report facts; do not mark an unseen person safe. Do not reenter until the authorized site response permits it. If the planned route is unsafe, follow the site's alternative escape instructions and communicate your location from safety.

**Change three.** The coordinator later announces that the alarm is resolved. A coworker assumes that all work can resume. Identify what still needs to be checked.

**Worked answer.** Emergency reentry and permission to restart this particular task are separate decisions. The equipment identity, pedestrian route, lifting arrangements, simultaneous operations, and relevant permits still need resolution by their responsible people. Join the revised briefing, confirm your role and communication method, and repeat back the critical restrictions. Record the change and hand it over to the next shift if unfinished.

## Exercises and practical boundaries

The companion application packet contains observable tabletop tasks and a record form. These activities let an instructor assess application of orientation knowledge without exposing learners to live hazards. They do not impose a universal live-equipment test on orientation learners.

For employees assigned an actual regulated task, the employer must supply the relevant equipment-specific instruction, qualified evaluator, and applicable records. For example, a person assigned to use covered PPE must demonstrate understanding and ability to use it under the applicable PPE rule. This program does not turn every attendee into a forklift operator or respirator user. Do not mark a task authorization complete from an orientation quiz.

## Examination and completion

The existing examination policy is a separate 40-question assessment sampled across ten domains, with an 80 percent passing threshold. Learning remediation is not examination credit. Answer every question before submitting. Saved answers allow resuming an unfinished attempt; starting another attempt must not erase an active attempt or previous failures. Correct answers and explanations remain unavailable until the attempt is submitted.

If unsuccessful, review the missed topics, work through the relevant lesson explanations, and take a fresh attempt under the configured retake policy. The current software contract permits up to 100 attempts; it is an implementation limit, not evidence that unlimited immediate retaking produces competence. Any change to this policy must be versioned and must preserve historical results. Exhaustion of the limit requires an explicit administrative resolution rather than deleting attempts.

The credential record must identify the learner and the exact completed program version. A completion record is not automatically an employer's permission to perform a task or a partner's agreement to accept this credential. Historical approved results must survive additive curriculum revisions. A learner must not have to surrender a legitimate earlier result merely because content is improved.

There is no invented universal renewal date. Revisit relevant instruction when duties, equipment, site conditions, procedures, or applicable requirements change, or when a demonstrated knowledge gap needs correction. Follow any actual employer, site, or role-specific refresher requirement separately.

## Instructor preparation and debrief

Read the lessons and worked answers before facilitating. Use the learner's actual site induction only after the employer confirms it is current. Mark every fictional exercise as fictional. Do not run a surprise evacuation, trip a plant alarm, handle hazardous chemicals, enter a space, or operate equipment to make the exercise realistic.

Ask learners to explain their reasoning rather than repeat the worked answer. If a learner chooses unsafe entry or unauthorized intervention, stop the scenario, identify the exposure path, and reteach the boundary before retrying with different conditions. Record what the learner did, the specific feedback, and the subsequent response. An instructor's signature alone is not an observation.
